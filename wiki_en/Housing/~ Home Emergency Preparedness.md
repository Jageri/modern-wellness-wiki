# Home Emergency Preparedness

**Mortality Impact**: Unknown; no evidence translates a generic household emergency kit into an all-cause mortality or longevity effect
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐ (Official checklists provide an action framework; evidence that household-preparedness interventions change preparedness behavior is low to very low certainty, and evidence for actual casualty outcomes is insufficient)
**Actionability**: High, but preparation must be adapted to local hazards, warnings, and household needs

## Conclusion
Household emergency preparedness is not simply buying a large or expensive kit. A more defensible approach has layers: receive local warnings and know when to evacuate; keep basic supplies accessible; install and maintain appropriate warning devices for specific hazards such as fire and carbon monoxide; and make individualized plans for prescriptions, medical equipment, children, older adults, disabled people, and pets. China's 2020 national basic checklist is a useful starting point, but it is not a clinical guideline and does not prove that one generic checklist reduces all-cause mortality [1-3].

## Evidence
- A 2024 systematic review included 17 studies of community household-preparedness interventions. Preparedness behavior improved slightly (SMD 0.53, 95% CI 0.16–0.91), but certainty was very low; effects on preparedness supplies (OR 6.12, 95% CI 0.13–284.37) and knowledge (SMD 0.96, 95% CI -0.15–2.08) were unclear. The studies did not establish a reduction in post-disaster casualties or all-cause mortality [2]
- In a retrospective cross-sectional survey of 1,114 households after Superstorm Sandy, having a kit, medication supply, or plan did not reduce adverse outcomes. Some “prepared” households reported more damage or health-service use, probably reflecting greater baseline risk, recall bias, and residual confounding; this does not show that preparation is harmful [3]
- A review of 83 independent samples on evacuation found that risk perception, previous evacuation, and already having an evacuation plan were among the more consistent predictors of evacuation. This supports planning ahead, but it does not prove that possession of a plan itself reduces mortality [4]
- Hazard-specific protection should not be collapsed into a generic kit. A London cluster-randomized community trial distributed 20,050 free smoke alarms but did not reduce fire injuries, hospital admissions, or deaths; installation and working-alarm rates were low in the follow-up sample, showing that distribution is not the same as protection [5]
- Among 408 residential carbon-monoxide exposures in Wisconsin with detector information, the odds of poisoning were 3.2 times higher (95% CI 1.5–6.9) when no detector was present than when a detector had alarmed, and outcomes were more severe. This was an observational poison-center record study, not a randomized trial, and it cannot be converted into a mortality effect for generic emergency kits [6]
- Disasters can interrupt chronic-disease care. A review supports planning for medicines, health information, and continuity of care, while emphasizing that many recommendations come from lessons after disasters and must be evidence-informed, consistent, and feasible [7]

### Scope of the Chinese national basic checklist
The 11 items in the Ministry of Emergency Management's 2020 source are: drinking water; ready-to-eat food; fire extinguisher and fire blanket; escape respirator; flashlight; multi-purpose knife; radio; emergency whistle; topical medicines; disinfecting wet wipes; and medical surgical masks. For water, the source says to cover three days of basic drinking-water needs, “at least 3 L per person.” This is the checklist's minimum stock wording, not a complete medical estimate of daily fluid requirements [1].

The national checklist does not include prescription medicines, smoke alarms, or carbon-monoxide alarms. Nor does it replace local warnings, sheltering instructions, or evacuation orders for earthquakes, floods, typhoons, wildfires, temperature extremes, chemical incidents, or other hazards. Alarm location, type, testing, and replacement should follow product labeling, local law, and fire or fuel-safety authorities; households using fuel-burning equipment or backup generators need particular carbon-monoxide precautions [8-10].

### ⚠️ Limitations
- Owning supplies, a preparedness questionnaire score, performance in a simulation, and fewer real casualties are different outcomes and cannot substitute for one another
- Hazards, housing, infrastructure, warning coverage, and evacuation capacity vary. The Chinese national checklist and U.S. fire, poison, and medication advice each have geographic and regulatory limits
- Older adults, disabled people, people with chronic illness, pregnant or postpartum people, infants, people living alone, lower-income households, renters, and people with language, hearing, or visual barriers may face cost, storage, transport, information, and evacuation barriers; individual preparation cannot replace community and public response
- Early prescription refills, refrigerated medicines, controlled substances, and insurance or reimbursement rules differ by jurisdiction; one reserve duration is not appropriate for everyone
- Household-preparedness studies are heterogeneous and often depend on self-report, cross-sectional designs, or post-disaster recall. Few measure injury, hospitalization, or death, and publication bias cannot be excluded

## Do
1. Identify local hazards, authoritative warning channels, evacuation routes, a meeting point, and an out-of-area contact; follow official evacuation instructions when issued
2. Use the 11-item national basic list as a first check, then adapt it to local risks and household members rather than treating it as a closed shopping list [1]
3. Store supplies where they do not block an exit and everyone can reach them quickly; use labels to check water, food, medicines, batteries, extinguishers, respirators, and alarms
4. Treat smoke alarms, carbon-monoxide alarms, and evacuation drills as separate safety measures. Buying or receiving a device is not enough: it must be correctly installed, kept working, and audible or accessible to household members [5,6,9]
5. Make a prescription and medical-device continuity plan with a clinician or pharmacist. Keep medicine names, doses, allergies, prescription/health information, refrigeration needs, and backup-power requirements; do not stop, substitute, or stockpile medicines on your own [7,8]
6. Arrange specific assistance, transport, communication, assistive devices, and supplies for mobility, hearing, vision, cognitive, or communication needs, as well as for infants and pets; rehearse a short scenario relevant to local hazards

## Don't
1. Don't claim that buying a kit, completing a survey, or performing a simulation has been proven to reduce mortality
2. Don't ignore a warning or remain in an evacuation zone to retrieve supplies
3. Don't run a generator, charcoal burner, fuel-burning tool, or vehicle engine inside a home, garage, basement, or other enclosed or partly enclosed space; a carbon-monoxide alarm does not make this safe [10]
4. Don't rely on expired, damaged, untested, or unfamiliar extinguishers, respirators, or alarms, and don't wait for an emergency to read their instructions
5. Don't impose one budget, medication duration, or supply quantity on every household. When resources are limited, prioritize warnings, evacuation, drinking water, essential medicines, and functioning safety devices, and seek local community or public assistance

## Seek Immediate Help
- If authorities order evacuation, or a smoke or carbon-monoxide alarm sounds, leave as directed and call the appropriate emergency service; do not delay to retrieve the kit
- If carbon-monoxide exposure is possible—especially when several people develop headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness, or altered consciousness together, or improve after leaving—move to fresh air and contact emergency services or the local poison center; do not re-enter to find the source
- Severe breathing difficulty, altered consciousness, uncontrolled bleeding, major burns, or crush injury requires emergency help
