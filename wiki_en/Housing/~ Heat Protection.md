# Heat Protection

**Mortality Impact**: No single all-cause mortality reduction is generalizable to individuals; air conditioning and heat-prevention plans are associated with lower heat-related mortality, but estimates depend on population, place, and the intervention bundle
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (Observational longitudinal and case-crossover studies plus authoritative guidance; causal certainty and generalizability are limited)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion
Heat is associated with a substantial mortality burden, but “heat protection” is not one intervention and cannot be summarized as `-14~20%`. A global model for 2000-2019 estimated that heat-related deaths accounted for 0.91% of all deaths (95% empirical CI 0.56%-1.36%); this is an exposure-attributable estimate, not the effect of protection [1]. Cooling, air conditioning (AC), warnings, outreach, and medical response have different evidence bases, and mortality studies are predominantly observational.

## Evidence
- A longitudinal study of 311 cities found that the heat-attributable excess-death fraction declined over time in Canada, Japan, Spain, and the US. The model estimated that rising AC prevalence explained 14.3%-20.0% of that decline [2]. This is the share of an observed temporal attenuation explained by AC prevalence, not a 14%-20% reduction in an individual's all-cause mortality.
- A case-crossover study of 73,578 deaths in 615 Ontario nursing homes during 2010-2023 found an all-cause mortality odds ratio on extreme-heat days of 1.11 (95% CI 1.06-1.16) in facilities without AC and 1.03 (0.98-1.07) in facilities with AC; the relative odds ratio was 1.08 (1.01-1.15) [3]. This facility-level association is not an 8% absolute risk reduction applicable to everyone.
- A longitudinal model across 102 cities in 14 European countries associated heat-prevention plans with a 25.2% reduction (95% CI 19.8%-31.9%) in heat-attributable excess deaths, an absolute difference of 1.8 deaths per 100,000 people per year (1.3-2.4) [4]. These plans bundle warnings and multiple public-health actions, so the estimate cannot be assigned to one alert, AC, or any single component.
- Safe fan thresholds remain disputed. A 2024 critical review concluded that above 35°C, the extra evaporation from fans is insufficient to meaningfully lower core temperature; older adults and people with impaired sweating should especially avoid relying on a fan as their only cooling measure [5]. WHO advises following local alerts, spending 2-3 hours a day in a cool place when the home cannot be kept cool, and cautions that fans do not prevent heat illness in very hot conditions [6].

### Limitations and risks
- The key mortality studies were not randomized. Income, building age, crowding, access to care, behavior change, and concurrent policy may still confound estimates.
- The global burden analysis modelled and extrapolated data from 750 locations in 43 countries; it is not a registry count of individually certified “heat deaths.” Risks differ by climate and population.
- Nursing-home participants averaged about 87 years of age and about two-thirds were women. AC status also differed with facility ownership, building standard, and crowding, limiting extrapolation to younger community populations.
- AC can increase energy costs, outage vulnerability, and greenhouse-gas emissions. Affordable cooling, passive shade and insulation, and reliable power require joint planning.
- The key studies reported public or nonprofit research support: Sera listed UK MRC grants, the European heat-plan study declared no competing interests, and the nursing-home study reported none. No AC-industry funding was identified, but observational bias remains possible.

## Do
1. Follow local heat alerts. Prioritize check-ins for older adults, infants, pregnant people, people living alone, and those with heart, lung, or kidney disease.
2. Block daytime sun and reduce indoor heat sources; ventilate at night when outdoor air is cooler and air quality permits.
3. Use AC or go to a reliably cooled public place when the home is overheating. Mortality studies do not establish a universal thermostat setting; adapt local health guidance to health status and appliance instructions.
4. Drink fluids and reduce outdoor activity and exertion during the hottest period. If a clinician has prescribed fluid restriction, or medicines that affect temperature, sweating, or fluid balance, seek individualized advice and do not stop medicines on your own.
5. Maintain AC and filters according to the appliance instructions, and make a power-outage plan with a cool destination, transport, and contacts.

## Don't
1. Do not rely on a fan as the only protection in extreme heat, especially for older adults or people with impaired sweating; use local temperature-humidity advice and combine it with environmental cooling or relocation [5][6].
2. Do not describe `14.3%-20.0%` as an individual's all-cause mortality reduction or assign the effect of a bundled heat plan to AC or a warning message alone.
3. Never leave a child or animal in a parked vehicle for any length of time [6].
4. Do not give fluids to an unconscious person or use antipyretics for suspected heatstroke [6].

## Emergency signs
Confusion, fainting, seizures, marked loss of coordination, or very hot skin can indicate heatstroke. Call local emergency services immediately, move the person to shade or AC, remove excess clothing, and keep actively cooling with cold water, wet towels, ice packs, and fans. Place an unconscious person on their side and do not give fluids [6].
