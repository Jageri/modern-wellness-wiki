# Strength Training

**Mortality impact**: Compared with no resistance training, any resistance training is associated with about 15% lower relative risk of all-cause mortality (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.77–0.93) [1]. This is a pooled observational association, not a randomized-trial estimate of life extension and not a 15% absolute risk reduction for an individual.
**Evidence level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Multiple prospective cohorts and meta-analyses are broadly consistent; causality is not established)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion

Strength training is associated with lower all-cause mortality, and the association remains in statistical models that account for aerobic activity. The hard-outcome evidence is mainly from observational cohorts using self-reported exercise; no long-term randomized trial has shown that strength training itself reduces all-cause mortality by 15%.

## Hard-outcome evidence

- A 2022 systematic review included 10 studies, with 6 contributing to the all-cause mortality meta-analysis. Any resistance training versus none had an RR of 0.85 (95% CI 0.77–0.93). The CVD mortality RR was 0.81 (0.66–1.00), whose upper bound was exactly 1.00; cancer mortality RR was 0.86 (0.78–0.95) [1].
- Another review of 16 prospective cohorts reported that muscle-strengthening activity was associated with 10%–17% lower risk across all-cause mortality and several chronic-disease outcomes. Roughly 30–60 min/week was near the low point of fitted curves, but results at higher volumes were uncertain; this does not establish 60 minutes as a causal “optimal dose” [2].
- In a US cohort of 416,420 adults, with aerobic activity entered in the same model, muscle-strengthening exercise once per week had an HR of 0.89 (0.81–0.97); at seven times per week the HR was 0.99 (0.94–1.04) [3]. This means the association was statistically significant at once weekly, not that a causal minimum dose or a harmful/ineffective high-frequency threshold was proved.
- In a 2024 pooled analysis of 17 annual samples totaling 482,756 US adults, meeting the guideline of muscle strengthening at least twice weekly was independently associated with lower 5-year mortality (HR 0.86, 0.81–0.90); findings were similar for 10-year mortality [4].
- In a Korean adult cohort, the estimate for muscle strengthening at least 5 days/week pointed in the same direction but included the null (HR 0.83, 0.68–1.02) [5]. Effect sizes therefore are not fully stable across settings and exposure definitions.

## How to interpret combined aerobic exercise

Both resistance and aerobic exercise are worth doing, but the difference between a combined-exercise group and a completely inactive group must not be described as resistance training providing an “additional 40%–50%” reduction on top of aerobic exercise. In a 2019 meta-analysis, resistance training alone versus no exercise had HR 0.79 (0.69–0.91), while combined resistance and aerobic exercise versus no exercise had HR 0.60 (0.49–0.72) [6]. Both estimates use the same inactive reference; 40% is not the incremental effect of adding resistance training.

## Hard outcomes versus surrogate outcomes

- All-cause death is a hard outcome. Strength, muscle mass, glucose, bone density, grip strength, and gait speed are functional or physiological measures and cannot substitute for mortality.
- A meta-analysis of randomized trials in adults aged 75 years and older found improved muscle strength with resistance training (difference in effect size 0.97, 95% CI 0.50–1.44), with improvement also in the 80+ subgroup. Grip-strength change was not significant, and the trials did not test long-term mortality [7].
- Low grip strength can reflect muscle function, but also existing illness, nutrition, and overall frailty. Raising grip strength does not automatically produce an equal reduction in mortality risk.

## Limitations and applicability

- Most studies measured leisure-time exercise once by self-report, with coarse information on exercise type, load, duration, and long-term adherence.
- People who strength train are often healthier, better educated, and wealthier, and may smoke less and eat differently. Statistical adjustment cannot eliminate healthy-user bias, residual confounding, or reverse causation.
- Some analyses excluding early deaths, smokers, or people with baseline chronic disease produced similar patterns, but illness or frailty causing lower activity still cannot be ruled out [3].
- Reviews included relatively few studies, mostly from the US. Publication bias could not be tested reliably and pooled effects may be overestimated [2]. Extrapolation to children, adolescents, institutionalized or severely ill people, and culturally different populations is limited.
- These studies generally do not provide a universally applicable absolute risk reduction or NNT. Absolute benefit depends on age, baseline mortality risk, follow-up duration, and other activity.
- The major reviews did not report funding by commercial equipment or supplement companies; Momma 2022, Coleman 2022, and Martinez-Gomez 2024 declared no competing interests [2–4]. This does not prove every underlying cohort was free of bias.

## What to do

1. Following WHO guidance, work toward muscle-strengthening activity of moderate or greater intensity involving all major muscle groups on at least 2 days/week, while retaining aerobic activity. The “2 days” guideline is not a precise optimum established by mortality studies [8].
2. If new to training, sedentary, older, or frail, start with controllable movements and lighter resistance, then gradually increase resistance, repetitions, or sets. People at high fall risk should prioritize protected or supervised training.
3. No specific barbell lifts are mandatory. Machines, resistance bands, body-weight exercise, and sustainable daily resistance activities can be selected to match ability.
4. People with known cardiovascular disease, uncontrolled symptoms or blood pressure, recent surgery or acute injury, marked osteoporosis, or high fall risk need an individualized plan rather than a copied load. Stop and seek prompt assessment for chest pain, fainting, or unusual breathlessness during training.
5. Protein intake should be individualized to total energy intake, age, diet, and health conditions such as kidney disease. Mortality evidence does not establish that everyone needs 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day.
