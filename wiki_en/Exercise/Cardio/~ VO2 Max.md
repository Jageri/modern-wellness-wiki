# VO₂ Max and Cardiorespiratory Fitness

**Mortality Impact**: Higher cardiorespiratory fitness is consistently associated with lower all-cause mortality, but mortality evidence is mainly observational and does not show that raising VO₂max necessarily extends life
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (strong prognostic association; training can improve VO₂max, but mortality causality is unproven)
**Actionability**: Moderate to high (aerobic training can improve VO₂max, with changes depending on baseline fitness, prescription, adherence, and measurement method)

## Conclusion

VO₂max is the maximal oxygen uptake measured during incremental exercise. Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET), which directly measures respiratory gases, is the standard direct assessment of cardiorespiratory fitness. Treadmill performance converted to METs, submaximal tests, and wearable values are estimates and should not be treated as identical to directly measured VO₂max.

Low cardiorespiratory fitness is an important prognostic marker, but it also reflects existing cardiac or pulmonary disease, recent illness, body composition, medication use, habitual activity, and reverse causation. Training trials show that VO₂max is modifiable; they do not establish that reaching a numerical threshold reduces mortality according to observational risk gradients.

## Evidence

- Mandsager 2018 included 122,007 adults referred for symptom-limited treadmill testing. The exposure was **peak estimated METs**, not VO₂max measured by gas analysis. Using age- and sex-matched fitness percentiles, the low group (<25th percentile) had an all-cause mortality HR of 5.04 (95% CI 4.10–6.20) versus the elite group (≥97.7th percentile). This comparison in a clinically referred population is not a personal training effect or a universal absolute risk. [PMID: 30646252](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30646252/)
- Kodama 2009 pooled 33 observational cohorts of healthy participants, expressing maximal aerobic capacity in METs. Each 1-MET higher fitness level was associated with an all-cause mortality RR of 0.87 (95% CI 0.84–0.90); low versus high fitness had an RR of 1.70 (95% CI 1.51–1.92). This supports a dose-related prognostic association, not an equivalent causal effect from a training-induced 1-MET gain. [PMID: 19454641](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19454641/)
- An American Heart Association scientific statement concludes that cardiorespiratory fitness adds risk-classification value beyond traditional risk factors. Its focus is prognosis and clinical management, not a treatment threshold that guarantees longer life.[PMID: 27881567](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27881567/)
- A 2025 individual-participant-data meta-analysis (42 studies; 1,544 participants) showed that training can improve VO₂max, with results depending on prescription method. In controlled studies, mean changes were 4.1 versus 1.8 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹ for physiological-threshold versus traditional-anchor prescriptions, with heterogeneous individual responses. The outcome was fitness change, not mortality or lifespan.[PMID: 39538060](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39538060/)
- A 2022 meta-analysis comparing sprint interval training with moderate-intensity continuous training found no difference in VO₂max improvement (Hedges' g = -0.004, 95% CI -0.08 to 0.07), while all included studies had unclear risk of bias and poor reporting quality. HIIT therefore cannot be claimed to be universally superior.[PMID: 33722760](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33722760/)

## How to Use This Measure

1. **Identify the method**: For precise measurement or clinical interpretation, use standardized CPET. Wearables, treadmill METs, and submaximal estimates may track trends, but should not be equated with laboratory VO₂max.
2. **Compare like with like**: Repeat testing with the same equipment, protocol, and health status when possible. Interpret results against reference values specific to age, sex, and protocol rather than universal targets such as “>40 for men” or “>35 for women.”
3. **Train regularly and progress gradually**: Continuous aerobic and interval training can both improve VO₂max. Choose a sustainable approach. High intensity is not required for everyone, and no fixed Zone 2 or HIIT template has been proven to extend life.
4. **Put safety before a maximal number**: People with unexplained chest pain, syncope, marked palpitations or abnormal breathlessness, or known heart or lung disease should not self-administer maximal testing or abruptly begin all-out intervals. CPET requires contraindication screening and appropriate monitoring.[PMID: 34782330](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34782330/)
5. **Do not convert the result into years of life**: A higher measured value indicates better performance on that assessment; it does not prove that an individual's mortality risk fell in proportion to an observational HR or RR.

## Limitations

- Mortality studies are mainly observational and remain vulnerable to referral and selection bias, reverse causation, healthy-user effects, and residual confounding.
- Direct VO₂max, measured VO₂peak, estimated METs, and wearable estimates have different errors and meanings.
- mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹ is relative to body mass; weight change can alter it without a proportional change in absolute oxygen uptake.
- Age, sex, test modality, disease status, and region alter reference distributions, so a single threshold is not universal.
- Training studies usually assess short-term fitness surrogates. Long-term randomized mortality trials isolating the effect of raising VO₂max are lacking.
- Bias and reporting limitations in comparisons of HIIT with continuous training weaken claims that one method is best; publication bias may also inflate training effects.
