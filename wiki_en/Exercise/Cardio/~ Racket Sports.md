# Racket Sports

**Mortality Impact**: No single generalizable effect estimate
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (Several observational cohorts point in a similar direction, but effect sizes vary widely and causality is unproven)
**Actionability**: Medium (requires a venue and equipment; some activities require a partner)

## Conclusion

Participation in tennis, badminton, squash, or related racket sports is associated with lower all-cause mortality. Current evidence does not show that racket sports themselves cause the reduction or that they are generally superior to running, swimming, or cycling. The striking “47% lower” figure is a single subgroup estimate from one British cohort; later cohorts found substantially smaller associations, so it should not be presented as an expected individual benefit or prescription target.[1–4]

## Evidence

- The British cohort included 80,306 adults aged 30–98 years. During a mean 9.2-year follow-up, 8,790 deaths occurred. Among 2,917 people reporting badminton, tennis, or squash participation in the previous 4 weeks, 54 died; among 77,391 nonparticipants, 8,736 died. The fully adjusted HR was 0.53 (95% CI 0.40–0.69). The model adjusted for age, sex, smoking, education, BMI, long-standing illness, baseline cardiovascular disease or cancer, and physical activity other than the sport of interest.[1]
- `HR 0.53` compares participants with nonparticipants in an observational analysis; it is not a randomized or direct head-to-head comparison with other sports. The study examined 6 sport groups, 2 mortality outcomes, and several dose measures, without reporting multiplicity correction for the primary analyses. There were only 54 all-cause and 9 cardiovascular deaths among racket-sport participants. Exposure was self-reported once for the previous 4 weeks and may not represent long-term participation.[1]
- The US NIH–AARP cohort included 272,550 older adults (mean age 70.5 years), with 118,153 deaths over a mean 12.4 years. For 7.5 to less than 15 MET-hours per week, tennis, squash, or racquetball participation was associated with all-cause mortality HR 0.84 (95% CI 0.75–0.93), a much smaller relative difference than 47%. The model adjusted for other leisure activity, and the study estimated a false-discovery rate for multiple testing, but the exposure was still self-reported once in an observational cohort.[2]
- A 2024 systematic review included 76 studies and about 2.6 million participants. Evidence was insufficient to meta-analyze mortality for racket sports; mortality meta-analyses were possible only for cycling, running, and swimming. This directly limits replication of a “best sport” claim or a fixed racket-sport percentage.[3]
- A 2025 cohort of US male health professionals used repeated exposure measures and a 2-year lag. Medium/high-intensity, medium/high-volume tennis was associated with HR 0.83 (95% CI 0.65–1.05) versus no tennis, with the CI including 1; with a 4-year lag, the HR was 0.85 (95% CI 0.61–1.19). Participants were healthier, predominantly White men aged 65 years or older, and tennis strata had few events, limiting generalizability.[4]

## Interpretation

- Several cohorts are compatible with lower mortality among racket-sport participants, but estimates range from roughly 16% to 47% and do not establish a stable, reproducible sport-specific effect.[1,2,4]
- Selection bias, healthy-user bias, residual confounding, and reverse causation remain plausible. People able to keep playing may differ in physical function, socioeconomic circumstances, diet, and other activity. Lag analyses and adjustment for total activity reduce only part of this problem.[1,2,4]
- Hand-eye coordination, intermittent exertion, and social interaction are plausible hypotheses, but mortality studies have not randomized these components. They do not establish that doubles is better than singles or that one racket sport has an extra longevity effect.
- Cohorts grouped different activities: the British study combined badminton, tennis, and squash, whereas NIH–AARP combined tennis, squash, and racquetball. Results should not automatically be extended to table tennis, wall practice, or every activity involving a racket.[1,2]

## Do

1. If enjoyable and appropriate for you, use racket sports as one option for regular activity; prioritize sustainable participation rather than a “47%” target or sport ranking.
2. Begin at an intensity appropriate to your fitness and skill, then progress gradually. Beginners, people returning after inactivity, and those with prior joint problems should learn sound technique and footwork.
3. Warm up dynamically and pay attention to the playing surface, footwear, and recovery. Stop and seek assessment for persistent pain, chest pain, fainting, or unusual shortness of breath.
4. People with significant cardiovascular symptoms, recent injury, or severe mobility limitation should obtain individualized medical advice before starting vigorous or competitive play.
