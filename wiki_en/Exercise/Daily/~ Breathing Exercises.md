# Breathing Exercises

**Mortality Impact**: Unknown; no evidence shows that breathing exercises reduce all-cause mortality or cardiovascular events or extend lifespan
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐ (preliminary evidence for short-term surrogate outcomes or self-reported symptoms; clinical outcomes and durability are uncertain)
**Actionability**: High, but evidence from different techniques is not interchangeable

## Conclusion

"Breathing exercises" are not one intervention. Comfortable slow-paced breathing, abdominal/diaphragmatic breathing, device-based inspiratory muscle training (IMT/IMST), yogic breathing (pranayama), and multicomponent programs that also include mindfulness, music, or relaxation differ in intensity, mechanism, control condition, and target population.

Current evidence suggests that some methods can change blood pressure, heart rate variability (HRV), or self-reported stress/anxiety during training or over the short term. These are surrogate outcomes or symptom scales, not proof of fewer cardiovascular events or longer life. Breathing exercises should not replace medication, delay medical care, or be treated as an alternative therapy for hypertension, anxiety disorders, or lung disease.

## Evidence

- **Mixed breathing methods and blood pressure**: A 2024 meta-analysis pooled 15 studies, 1,097 participants, and 11 different breathing interventions. The mean difference in systolic blood pressure was -7.06 mmHg (95% CI -10.20 to -3.92), but heterogeneity was I²=90%, and all 15 studies had unclear or high risk of bias. Interventions ranged from a single 16-minute session to 90 days, so the review cannot establish one frequency or dose. [1]
- **Newer counterevidence and durability**: In a 2025 network meta-analysis, "breathing control" versus passive control in people with hypertension produced a short-term (≤3 months) systolic blood-pressure difference of -6.65 mmHg (95% credible interval -10.39 to -2.93). Overall certainty was "very low" because of risk of bias, potential publication bias, and imprecision. Few studies followed participants beyond three months, and effects appeared to diminish over time. [2]
- **Inspiratory muscle training is not ordinary slow breathing**: A 2023 IMT meta-analysis reported eight RCTs and 215 participants, yet its pooled systolic-pressure section reported seven trials, 228 participants, and -12.55 mmHg—a clear internal inconsistency. Devices, loads, frequencies, and intervention durations varied substantially; with fewer than ten studies, publication bias was not assessed. [3] A more rigorous double-blind, sham-controlled RCT included only 36 adults aged 50–79 with systolic pressure ≥120 mmHg. IMST at 75% of maximal inspiratory pressure, 30 breaths/day, six days/week for six weeks reduced clinic systolic pressure from 135 to 126 mmHg; low-resistance sham training did not change it. This remains a small, short-term surrogate-outcome trial. [4]
- **Slow breathing and HRV**: A systematic review/meta-analysis of 223 studies found that voluntary slow breathing increased vagally mediated HRV measures during practice, immediately after one session, and after multisession interventions. Because breathing rate and respiratory sinus arrhythmia directly alter HRV, this is a physiological response, not evidence of fewer clinical events or longer life. [5]
- **Stress and anxiety**: A 2023 meta-analysis found a small-to-medium effect on self-reported stress across 12 RCTs and 785 participants (Hedges' g=-0.35, 95% CI -0.55 to -0.14), but most studies had moderate risk of bias, and one author had trained as a breath teacher. [6] In a subsequent 400-person RCT with a matched attention-placebo, coherent breathing at about 5.5 breaths/min did not outperform the 12-breaths/min control for stress, anxiety, depression, or wellbeing. [7]
- **Diaphragmatic breathing**: A 2019 systematic review of isolated diaphragmatic breathing for stress found only three studies, one of which was randomized; clinical and methodological heterogeneity prevented pooling. This does not support broadly claiming that diaphragmatic breathing lowers blood pressure, improves lung function, or extends life. [8]
- **Yogic breathing**: A 2026 meta-analysis of pranayama for hypertension included seven RCTs and 683 participants. Heart rate decreased (SMD=-0.43, 95% CI -0.52 to -0.34), and systolic pressure decreased under a fixed-effect model, but the authors still called for larger standardized trials. [9] A 2025 review in people with mental disorders found benefits mainly against passive controls and in per-protocol samples, with high overall risk of bias. Adverse events were more frequent with fast than slow techniques, and pranayama should not replace standard treatment. [10]
- **Chronic/serious respiratory disease**: A 2024 systematic review included 73 RCTs and 5,479 participants, mostly with COPD or asthma. Pursed-lip/diaphragmatic breathing versus usual care improved breathlessness by -0.40 points (95% CI -0.70 to -0.11), which did not exceed the minimal important difference; health-related quality of life improved. No related adverse events were reported, but "not reported" does not prove that every technique is safe for every patient. Techniques should be individualized by pulmonary-rehabilitation professionals. [11]

## Do

1. If the goal is relaxation, try brief, comfortable slow-paced breathing. The emphasis is on breathing more slowly, not taking forcefully deeper breaths. There is no evidence that 4-7-8 breathing, box breathing, or any fixed inhalation-to-exhalation ratio is best for long-term clinical outcomes.
2. If you have hypertension, use breathing practice only as an adjunct. Continue validated blood-pressure monitoring and prescribed treatment; do not change medication based on a reading taken after a single session.
3. IMT/IMST requires a resistance device and an individualized load based on maximal inspiratory pressure; it is not a synonym for "deep breathing." People with cardiopulmonary disease should discuss suitability with a clinician or respiratory/pulmonary-rehabilitation therapist.
4. Avoid unsupervised rapid or forceful breathing and prolonged breath holds. Hyperventilation syndrome is associated with hypocapnia, and organic disease must be excluded before making that diagnosis. [12] Stop immediately if you develop dizziness, tingling around the mouth or in the limbs, marked breathlessness, chest pain, or near-fainting; persistent or severe symptoms require medical assessment.

## Limitations

- Participant and instructor blinding is difficult. Self-reported stress, anxiety, and breathlessness are susceptible to expectancy and attention effects.
- Many trials are small, last only weeks to three months, and differ greatly in technique, training dose, co-medication, and control group.
- Most studies measure clinic blood pressure, HRV, heart rate, pulmonary function, scale scores, or quality of life; none establish improved clinical events or mortality.
- Adverse-event and trial-registry outcome reporting is incomplete; small-study effects and publication bias cannot be excluded.
