# Balance and Stability Training

**Mortality Impact**: There is no direct evidence that balance training extends lifespan or reduces all-cause mortality. Fall-prevention exercise trials in the 2024 USPSTF review did not show a significant reduction in all-cause mortality; a broader meta-analysis of long-term exercise RCTs likewise found no overall mortality difference (RR 0.93, 95% CI 0.83–1.04) [1][5].
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (for fall outcomes in community-dwelling older adults; fracture evidence is uncertain and mortality/lifespan evidence is insufficient)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion

For community-dwelling older adults at increased fall risk, sustained, progressive functional exercise that safely challenges balance reduces falls; many effective programs also include strength training. The evidence does not support treating resistance training alone, improvement on a balance test, or any arbitrary “multicomponent” package as proof of fewer fractures or longer life [1][2].

This conclusion should not be directly extrapolated to care-facility residents, people with stroke or Parkinson disease, people who require another person or a walking aid to walk, or younger adults in the general population [2][3][6].

## Evidence

- The 2024 USPSTF evidence review included 83 fair- or good-quality RCTs (48,839 participants). Across 37 exercise trials (16,117 participants), exercise reduced the **rate of falls** (IRR 0.85, 95% CI 0.75–0.96), the **risk of being a person with at least one fall** (RR 0.92, 95% CI 0.87–0.98), and the **rate of injurious falls** (IRR 0.84, 95% CI 0.74–0.95). It did not significantly reduce the **risk of being a person with at least one injurious fall** (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.79–1.02) [1].
- In the same review, exercise did not significantly reduce the **rate of fall-related fractures** (IRR 0.81, 95% CI 0.57–1.15) or all-cause mortality. Multifactorial interventions reduced fall rate (IRR 0.84, 95% CI 0.74–0.95) but did not significantly reduce the number of fallers, injurious falls, fractures, or all-cause mortality. More components therefore do not automatically mean greater clinical benefit [1][7].
- The 2019 Cochrane review separated exercise types. Programs primarily involving balance and functional exercise reduced fall rate (RaR 0.76, 95% CI 0.70–0.81) and the number of fallers (RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.82–0.91), both with high-certainty evidence. Multiple exercise categories—usually balance/functional plus resistance exercise—also probably reduced falls, but with lower certainty and without a direct head-to-head demonstration of superiority. Effects of resistance training alone, dance, or walking remained uncertain [2].
- The FAMILY cluster RCT enrolled 2,610 community-dwelling adults aged 60 or older at increased fall risk in 128 rural villages across 4 Chinese provinces. The intervention combined **balance and functional exercise with community-engaged health education**; controls received usual health education. At 12 months, 29.7% versus 38.3% reported at least one fall: an absolute difference of −8.63 percentage points and adjusted OR of 0.67 (95% CI 0.48–0.91) [3]. This bundled trial cannot separate the contributions of exercise and community engagement.
- A 2026 network meta-analysis included 423 RCTs and 33,901 adults aged 60 or older who were not recruited for a specific disease. Several exercise modes improved static, dynamic, or reactive balance tests, but the authors stated that the rankings did not establish superiority. These are **functional surrogate outcomes**, not evidence of fewer falls, fractures, or deaths [4].

## Do

1. **Prioritize progressive balance and functional exercise** when fall risk is increased, usually with lower-limb strengthening; begin with assessment or supervision by a physical therapist, primary-care clinician, or trained instructor when instability is marked [2][7].
2. **Practice beside stable support and progress gradually**. Effective programs depend on sustained participation and adequate but safe balance challenge, not a one-off session or test [2].
3. **Seek individualized assessment** after a fall or functional decline. Use fall history, gait and balance, medicines, postural blood pressure, vision, feet and footwear, cognition, and the home environment to select interventions [7].
4. **Place balance work inside a complete activity plan**. It does not replace aerobic and strength activity appropriate to the individual, or evaluation and treatment of osteoporosis, syncope, or neurological disease.

## Don't

1. Do not interpret a better balance-test score as proof of fewer fractures, lower mortality, or longer life [1][4].
2. Do not attribute the FAMILY result to one exercise; it compared exercise plus community-engaged education with usual health education [3].
3. Do not attempt eyes-closed one-leg standing, rapid perturbations, or loaded movement without support or supervision when instability or recurrent falls are present.
4. Do not use calcium or vitamin D alone as a substitute for balance training and individualized risk assessment [7].

## Caveats

- The USPSTF evidence applies to community-dwelling adults aged 65 or older. The Cochrane review included community-dwelling adults aged 60 or older and excluded trials focused on conditions such as stroke. FAMILY excluded neurodegenerative disease and inability to walk without another person or a walking aid [1][2][3].
- Care facilities differ in environment, cognitive impairment, staffing, and implementation. Facility-specific evidence should guide programs; community effect sizes should not simply be imported [6].
- Exercise-related harms were incompletely reported. In the USPSTF review, reported harms were mainly minor musculoskeletal symptoms and serious harms were rare, but wrist and hip fractures during exercise sessions were reported [7].
- FAMILY was open-label, relied mainly on quarterly self-reported falls, could not isolate the effects of exercise from community-engaged education, and should not be directly generalized to hospitals or care facilities [3].
- The 2019 Cochrane review reported a possible fracture benefit with low-certainty evidence, whereas the newer USPSTF analysis found no significant fracture difference. Fracture effects should therefore remain uncertain rather than selectively adopting the more favorable estimate [1][2].
