# Physical Inactivity

**Mortality impact**: The direction is clear, but no single percentage is broadly generalizable. WHO says that insufficiently active people have a 20%–30% higher risk of death, but its fact sheet does not provide one exposure definition, effect measure, baseline absolute risk, or a direct primary source for that range. Cohort meta-analyses produce materially different estimates because they compare no activity, not meeting guidelines, study-specific lowest groups, or device-measured quartiles [1][2][3]

**Evidence level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (WHO strongly recommends regular activity for adults, and large prospective cohorts and meta-analyses are directionally consistent; all-cause mortality evidence is mainly observational, and long-term mortality RCTs have not established a causal effect)

**Actionability**: High (activity can begin at low doses; age, disease, disability, occupational load, and environment determine safe implementation)

---

## Conclusion

Persistently low activity is consistently associated with higher all-cause mortality, and the largest marginal association is usually seen when moving from almost no activity to some activity. However, “physical inactivity” does not have one interchangeable comparison across studies, so `+20–30%` should not be treated as the personal risk increase for everyone who falls short of guidelines [1][2][3].

Adults can use WHO targets as a long-term direction: 150–300 minutes/week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity, 75–150 minutes/week of vigorous activity, or an equivalent combination, plus moderate-or-greater muscle strengthening involving major muscle groups on at least 2 days/week. People who cannot yet meet the target can still benefit and should start with a small, sustainable amount and build gradually [1].

## Evidence

### 1. Why the `+20–30%` prefix was removed

WHO's 2024 fact sheet states that insufficiently active people have a 20%–30% higher risk of death than sufficiently active people. This is an authoritative directional summary, but the fact sheet does not link that range to one clearly defined, directly auditable pooled estimate and does not specify whether it is an RR, HR, or another effect measure [6].

More specific studies show why the range cannot serve as a universal filename prefix:

- A 2023 dose-response meta-analysis covered 94 cohorts and more than 30 million participants; its all-cause mortality analysis included 50 results and 811,616 deaths. Relative to zero non-occupational activity, 8.75 marginal MET-hours/week—converted by the authors to about 150 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity—had an RR of 0.69 (95% CI 0.65–0.73) [2]. This is not a binary comparison of everyone below versus above 150 minutes, and it should not simply be inverted into a personal risk promise.
- A device-measured meta-analysis included 36,383 predominantly middle-aged and older adults. With the lowest study-specific quartile of total activity as reference, HRs for the second, third, and highest quartiles were 0.48, 0.34, and 0.27. For sedentary time, the highest versus lowest quartile HR was 2.63 (95% CI 1.94–3.56) [3]. These extreme-group comparisons are much larger than 20%–30% and do not represent guideline attainment.
- In a 15-cohort step meta-analysis, the lowest quartile median was 3,553 steps/day; 5,801 steps/day in the second quartile had an HR of 0.60 (95% CI 0.51–0.71) relative to that group [4]. Total daily steps are not the same exposure as moderate-to-vigorous minutes.

WHO's 20%–30%, zero-activity contrasts, study-specific extreme groups, and failure to meet a guideline answer different questions and cannot share one precise mortality number.

### 2. Dose-response: moving from none to some matters most, without a universal threshold

Garcia et al. found the largest risk differences between 0 and 8.75 marginal MET-hours/week, with smaller marginal differences above that dose. Their model estimated that if all insufficiently active people reached this level, 15.7% (95% CI 13.1%–18.2%) of premature deaths could have been averted [2]. That is a **population potential-impact fraction**, dependent on the exposure distribution and causal assumptions; it is not an individual RR, absolute-risk reduction, or gain in lifespan.

Device studies also support a nonlinear relationship, but estimates depend on device placement, wear time, study-specific bins, and reference groups. Paluch et al. observed flattening around 6,000–8,000 steps/day among adults aged 60 years or older and around 8,000–10,000 among younger adults. These are not mandatory biological thresholds and do not prove that 10,000 steps is optimal for everyone [4].

### 3. Insufficient activity, sedentary time, and occupational activity are not synonyms

- **Insufficient activity** generally means not reaching recommended moderate-to-vigorous activity; **sedentary behaviour** means waking time spent sitting, reclining, or lying with very low energy expenditure. Someone may meet exercise targets yet sit for long periods, or avoid prolonged sitting without accumulating much moderate-to-vigorous activity [1].
- WHO recommends reducing sedentary time and replacing some of it with activity of any intensity, but evidence was insufficient to set one maximum number of sedentary hours for all adults [1].
- Occupational activity cannot automatically be treated as equivalent to leisure exercise. In an individual-participant meta-analysis of 22 studies and up to 590,497 workers, high versus sedentary occupational activity had an HR of 1.12 (95% CI 1.03–1.23) in men and 0.97 (0.86–1.10) in women; leisure activity was inversely associated with mortality in both sexes. Adjustment for income attenuated estimates, indicating important socioeconomic confounding [5].

### 4. Causality, counter-evidence, and absolute risk

Long-term mortality evidence is almost entirely observational. As indirect counter-evidence, a meta-analysis of exercise RCTs lasting at least 1 year in adults aged 60 years or older found no significant reduction in all-cause mortality across 29 trials and 11,441 participants. These studies generally used multicomponent exercise, often had active controls, and had limited mortality events and statistical power [7]. This does not negate other health benefits of activity; it shows why large cohort associations should not be read as proven causal effects.

Principal limitations include:

- **Reverse causation**: occult disease, pain, frailty, or disability can reduce activity and increase mortality; exclusion of early deaths and lag analyses only partly address this.
- **Residual confounding**: more-active participants can differ in smoking, diet, body weight, health-care access, and socioeconomic conditions.
- **Measurement error**: most large cohorts rely on self-reported leisure activity, while short device wear may not represent long-term habits. Questionnaires, MET conversions, devices, and bins are not interchangeable.
- **Selection and publication bias**: device-study volunteers tend to be healthier; cohorts able to provide harmonized individual data are not the whole literature, and positive findings may still be more likely to appear.
- **Population boundaries**: device evidence is weighted toward middle-aged and older adults, whereas occupational evidence covers working-age populations. People with disease, pregnancy/postpartum status, disability, or frailty need population-specific guidance.
- **Missing absolute risk**: HRs and RRs are relative comparisons. Without an individual's baseline risk, they cannot show an absolute-risk reduction or years of life gained.

### 5. Conflicts of interest

The WHO guideline paper, Garcia 2023, Ekelund 2019, and Coenen 2024 declared no competing interests [1][2][3][5]. Paluch 2022 was supported by the US CDC; several authors disclosed public-agency/foundation funding or professional-society roles, with no step-device manufacturer identified as a principal funder [4]. Two authors of the older-adult RCT meta-analysis disclosed grants or advisory relationships with multiple pharmaceutical, nutrition, and health companies; it is therefore used only as indirect counter-evidence and not to estimate a mortality effect [7].

## Do

1. **Start with a small dose**: if currently very inactive, add repeatable short bouts of walking, cycling, wheeling, or another ability-appropriate activity, then gradually increase frequency, duration, and intensity [1].
2. **Use the guideline range as a long-term direction**: when safe, work toward 150–300 minutes/week of moderate activity, 75–150 minutes/week of vigorous activity, or an equivalent combination [1].
3. **Include strengthening**: train major muscle groups on at least 2 days/week; older adults should also include ability-appropriate multicomponent work emphasizing strength and balance [1].
4. **Break up prolonged sedentary time**: replace some sitting with light-or-greater-intensity activity rather than waiting for a universal sedentary-time cutoff [1].

## Don't

1. **Do not treat `+20–30%` as a personal forecast**, or equate not meeting guidelines, zero activity, the lowest quartile, and the highest sedentary group.
2. **Do not assume there is no benefit below 150 minutes**; observational curves show lower risk with small increases in the low-activity range [2][3].
3. **Do not count heavy occupational work as automatically equivalent protection**; duration, recovery, posture, and socioeconomic context differ from leisure activity [5].
4. **Do not jump directly from long-term inactivity to vigorous training**; progression should reflect ability, particularly with frailty, pregnancy/postpartum status, disability, or chronic disease [1].

## Seek medical or professional assessment for

- Chest pain, syncope, marked palpitations, or breathlessness out of proportion to usual exertion during activity.
- New unilateral weakness, sudden gait change, recurrent falls, or pain and swelling that progressively worsen after activity.
- Unstable cardiopulmonary disease, recent surgery, pregnancy complications, severe neuromuscular disease, or substantial mobility impairment before materially increasing activity.
