# Walking

**Mortality impact**: In two US occupational cohorts, the highest versus lowest quartile of leisure-time walking volume was associated with 17% lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.80–0.85) [1]

**Evidence level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (multiple large prospective cohorts and meta-analyses are directionally consistent; the mortality evidence remains observational and does not prove that walking lowers an individual's risk by 17%)

**Actionability**: High (people can start at low doses, but ability, environment, and underlying disease affect safe implementation)

---

## Conclusion

More walking is consistently associated with lower all-cause mortality, but studies do not measure one interchangeable exposure. Some assess self-reported leisure-time walking energy expenditure, others device-measured total daily steps, walking speed, or walking specifically for commuting. The `-17%` filename refers only to the specific comparison in Han et al.; it does not mean that starting to walk causes a 17% reduction, nor is it the shared effect of 7,000 steps, brisk walking, and walking to work [1].

Device-measured evidence suggests that the curve is generally steepest at low activity levels and then flattens. Seven thousand steps per day can be a practical target, but it is not a universal threshold, and there is no evidence that everyone must reach 10,000 steps to benefit [2][3]. Mortality evidence is mainly observational and remains vulnerable to reverse causation, healthy-user bias, and residual confounding.

---

## Evidence

### 1. What exactly does `-17%` compare?

Han et al. pooled the Nurses' Health Study and Health Professionals Follow-Up Study: 70,725 female nurses and 40,742 male health professionals who in 1986 were free of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer, respiratory disease, and neurological disease. Follow-up continued to 2018/2020, with 38,847 deaths recorded [1].

- Exposure was **leisure-time walking** updated by questionnaire every two years and expressed as MET-hours/week. It was not phone or wearable step count and was not a dedicated active-commuting analysis.
- Walking was divided into cohort-specific quartiles. Median exposure in the lowest versus highest group was 0.7 versus 15.0 MET-hours/week in women and 1.1 versus 20.6 MET-hours/week in men.
- The highest versus lowest group had an all-cause mortality HR of 0.83 (95% CI 0.80–0.85). Models also adjusted for other physical activities and many lifestyle factors.
- The dose-response curve was nonlinear and appeared to flatten around 7.5 MET-hours/week. This is a fitted-curve signal, not a randomized prescription threshold.

The investigators used a four-year lag, excluded major disease at baseline, and stopped updating activity after major disease developed to reduce reverse causation. These steps cannot eliminate the possibility that healthier people can walk more or that unmeasured factors explain part of the association. Participants were mainly White health professionals, limiting generalizability to other occupations, ethnic groups, people with disease, and people with mobility limitations [1]. A 2026 correction clarified the definitions of jogging and running and did not change the walking result.

### 2. Device-measured steps are a separate exposure

A 2025 systematic review included 57 studies from 35 cohorts; its all-cause mortality dose-response synthesis used 14 device-measured studies. Compared with 2,000 steps/day, 7,000 steps/day was associated with 47% lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.46–0.60; I²=36.3%), with an inflection around 5,000–7,000 steps/day. Certainty for this outcome was rated moderate [2]. This was still not a trial that randomized long-term step counts.

A meta-analysis of 15 international cohorts (47,471 adults; 3,013 deaths) reported quartile medians of 3,553, 5,801, 7,842, and 10,901 steps/day. Relative to the lowest quartile, adjusted HRs were 0.60, 0.55, and 0.47 for the next three quartiles [3]. Curves flattened around 6,000–8,000 steps/day in adults aged 60 years or older and 8,000–10,000 in younger adults. After adjustment for total steps, commonly used time-at-cadence measures were no longer significant, suggesting that evidence for **total steps is more stable than a requirement to walk briskly** [3].

Step counts include all ambulatory movement, not only planned walking. Readings from wrist devices, hip devices, and pedometers are also not directly interchangeable. Reviews report substantial variability in effect estimates because devices, reference groups, and models differ [4].

### 3. Duration, speed, and commuting cannot share one number

- **Duration/energy expenditure**: A 2014 meta-analysis of 14 walking cohorts found that a standardized 11.25 MET-hours/week of walking was associated with 11% lower all-cause mortality (95% CI 4%–17%) after adjustment for other physical activity [5]. This is an observational cross-study dose conversion, not Han's highest-versus-lowest comparison.
- **Walking speed**: In a 2024 meta-analysis of 13 cohorts, the fastest versus slowest category had an HR of 0.57 (95% CI 0.48–0.66), and each 0.1 m/s increment had an HR of 0.94 (95% CI 0.92–0.96) [6]. Speed reflects behavior but also cardiorespiratory fitness, strength, neurological status, and underlying disease. Reverse causation is therefore particularly important; forcing a faster pace cannot be assumed to reproduce the observed risk difference.
- **Walking to work**: A meta-analysis of 17 studies and 829,098 workers found that active commuting by walking was associated with 13% lower all-cause mortality (95% CI 1%–25%); the high-exposure category was associated with 19% lower risk (95% CI 8%–30%) [7]. Exposure definitions varied and only employed populations were represented, so these estimates cannot replace leisure-walking or device-step estimates.
- **Total physical activity**: WHO's target of 150–300 minutes/week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity is a broad health guideline. Walking can contribute, but regular muscle-strengthening activity is also recommended [8]. The guideline is not the source of the `-17%` figure.

### 4. Causality, counter-evidence, and limitations

There is no adequate trial that assigns general-population adults to different long-term walking doses with all-cause mortality as the primary outcome. As indirect counter-evidence, a meta-analysis of exercise RCTs lasting at least one year in adults aged 60 years or older found no significant mortality reduction across 29 trials with mortality data from 11,441 participants. Interventions were generally multicomponent rather than walking-only, and event counts and power were limited [9]. This does not negate walking's health value; it shows why large cohort HRs should not be interpreted as causal effects.

Principal limitations are:

- **Reverse causation**: occult disease, pain, frailty, or impaired mobility can both reduce walking and increase mortality; lag analyses only partly address this.
- **Healthy-user bias**: people who walk more often smoke less, eat better, and have more social or material resources; statistical adjustment cannot capture every difference.
- **Measurement differences**: self-reported time has recall error; short device wear may not represent long-term habits; walking speed may be a health marker as much as an exposure.
- **Population differences**: Han's cohorts were mainly White US health professionals. Step reviews span countries and ages but still have limited age-specific evidence; people with disease, frailty, or disability need individualized interpretation.
- **Publication and modelling bias**: reference categories, step bins, and dose-response models differ, producing widely varying estimates. A reported “minimum” or “inflection point” is not a biological switch [4].
- **No absolute-risk estimate**: an HR is relative. It does not state how much one person's absolute mortality risk falls or how many years of life are gained.

### 5. Conflicts of interest

The Han study was supported by US National Institutes of Health grants and an American Cancer Society research professorship; funders had no role in design, analysis, interpretation, or publication decisions [1]. The 2025 device-step review was supported by Australian public/nonprofit research funds and declared no competing interests [2]; the 2024 umbrella review also declared no relevant financial or nonfinancial interests [4]. ## Do

1. **Start with a sustainable increment**: if currently inactive, add short bouts of walking to daily routines; observational curves suggest that the largest marginal association is at the low end [2][3].
2. **Treat 7,000 steps as an optional target, not a pass/fail line**: age, baseline ability, and device differ, and benefits may occur below 7,000 [2][3].
3. **Use total-activity guidance for long-term planning**: when safe, progress toward 150–300 minutes/week of moderate aerobic activity and include muscle strengthening [8].
4. **Adapt to conditions**: choose indoor walking, shorter duration, or lower intensity during extreme heat or cold, poor air quality, slippery conditions, or complex traffic.

## Don't

1. **Do not treat `-17%` as a personal guarantee** or apply it to 7,000 steps, brisk pace, or commuting; those estimates use different exposures and comparison groups.
2. **Do not abruptly increase volume to chase 10,000 steps**. Evidence does not support a universal hard threshold; pain, fatigue, and fall risk take priority.
3. **Do not use walking as a complete substitute for strength, balance work, or medical treatment**. Walking alone may not cover fall-prevention needs in older adults, and WHO also recommends regular strengthening [8].

## Seek medical or professional assessment for

- Chest pain, syncope, marked palpitations, or breathlessness out of proportion to usual effort while walking.
- New unilateral weakness, a sudden gait change, or recurrent falls.
- Joint, foot, or calf pain that progressively worsens, persists after rest, or is accompanied by marked swelling.
- Unstable cardiopulmonary disease, recent surgery, severe peripheral neuropathy, or substantial mobility impairment before increasing activity.
