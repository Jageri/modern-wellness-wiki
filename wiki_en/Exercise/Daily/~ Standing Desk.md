# Standing Desk

**Mortality Impact**: Unknown; no evidence shows that sit-stand desks or more standing reduce all-cause mortality
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐ (reduces occupational sitting; long-term clinical outcomes unproven)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion

A sit-stand desk can help some office workers sit less at work, but it mainly replaces sitting with standing. It is not exercise and has not been shown to reduce cardiovascular events or mortality. A more defensible goal is to avoid maintaining one posture for long periods: alternate sitting and standing according to comfort, add brief walking or other activity breaks to the workday, and do not stand still all day in the name of health.

## Evidence

- A 2025 umbrella review included 36 systematic reviews covering 214 unique studies. Sit-to-stand workstations used alone reduced sedentary time by up to about 75 min/day (95% CI -109 to -41) but did not significantly increase physical activity at any intensity. Environmental-intervention results showed possible publication bias and high heterogeneity (I²=84.8%) [1].
- A 2018 Cochrane review included 34 studies and 3,397 participants, all from high-income countries. Sit-stand desks reduced workplace sitting by a mean 100 min/workday at ≤3 months (95% CI -116 to -84) and 57 min/day at 3–12 months (95% CI -99 to -15). Evidence quality was low, with no longer-term evidence. These results establish behavior change, not disease or mortality benefit [2].
- In a meta-analysis of nine trials involving 877 healthy adults, mean follow-up was only 3.81 months and intervention groups stood 1.33 h/day more than controls. Fasting glucose was 2.53 mg/dL lower (95% CI -4.27 to -0.79) and body fat mass 0.75 kg lower (95% CI -0.91 to -0.59), while fasting insulin, lipids, blood pressure, weight, and waist circumference did not differ significantly. These short-term surrogate outcomes cannot be converted into cardiovascular or longevity benefit [3].
- Standing breaks and activity breaks are not equivalent. A meta-analysis of seven one-day randomized crossover trials found that brief standing interruptions modestly improved postprandial glucose, while light-intensity walking had larger effects on glucose and insulin; neither significantly affected systolic blood pressure. These were acute laboratory outcomes only [4].
- In a device-measured observational study of 83,013 UK Biobank adults (mean age 61.3 years), standing time was not associated with major cardiovascular disease. Above 2 h/day of standing, each additional 30 min was associated with an 11% higher risk of orthostatic circulatory disease. This was not a sit-stand desk trial and cannot show that changing standing time changes risk [5].
- A Danish occupational cohort defined exposure as standing or walking for at least 75% of working time. Over 12 years, the pooled RR for hospitalization due to lower-limb varicose veins was 1.78 (95% CI 1.19–2.68). This observational occupational exposure did not separate standing from walking and should not be directly extrapolated to intermittent sit-stand desk use [6].
- In a meta-analysis of three laboratory studies, prolonged standing desk work did not produce less low-back pain than sitting (SMD 0.60, 95% CI -0.68 to 1.87; I²=90%). Responses varied substantially, so the evidence does not support one universal standing dose [7].
- Standing expended only 0.15 kcal/min more than sitting on average (95% CI 0.12–0.17) across 46 studies and 1,184 participants. This is a short-term energy-expenditure result, not a weight-loss, cardiovascular, or mortality outcome [8].

## Do

1. If you use a sit-stand desk, treat it as a posture-change tool; adjust sitting and standing periods to comfort and task demands.
2. Add actual movement to the workday, such as brief walks, getting water, or walking to speak with a colleague; standing still is not a substitute for activity.
3. Shorten standing and change posture if you develop leg heaviness, swelling, dizziness, or low-back/lower-limb discomfort.

## Don't

1. Do not stand still all day, and do not treat “switch every 30 minutes” as a fixed dose supported by long-term clinical evidence.
2. Do not interpret less workplace sitting as proven reduction in cardiovascular disease or mortality; sit-stand desk trials have not tested those long-term outcomes.
3. Do not replace regular walking, strength training, or other physical activity with standing desk use.

## Individualization and Medical Red Flags

- People with orthostatic hypotension, venous disease, balance/fall risk, or pain aggravated by standing should tailor use according to symptoms and clinical or occupational-health advice.
- Persistent one-sided swelling, marked pain, skin-color change, fainting, or chest pain is not routine desk discomfort and warrants prompt medical assessment.
