# Medical Insurance

**Mortality Impact**: No all-cause mortality data; this entry concerns access to care and household exposure to medical costs
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (Official enrollment statistics, a health-system review, a Chinese catastrophic-health-expenditure review, and a US randomized public-insurance policy experiment; no comparison of Chinese personal commercial-insurance portfolios)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion
First make sure basic medical insurance remains active, then compare commercial contracts against financial risks your household could not absorb. In 2025, China's basic medical insurance covered 1,330.6939 million people and maintained 95% enrollment. This supports treating social medical insurance as the system foundation, but enrollment does not mean that every service is accessible, timely, or fully reimbursed. Nor does it establish one commercial product, benefit level, or purchase order for every household. Premiums, deductibles, exclusions, renewal terms, and payment rules can change; the current contract and the household's actual capacity to absorb loss are decisive.

## Evidence
- China's National Healthcare Security Administration reported 1,330.6939 million basic-medical-insurance enrollees in 2025 and a maintained enrollment rate of 95% [1]
- A Lancet review of China's 2009-2019 reforms described near-universal basic coverage while identifying remaining gaps in quality, cost control, and equity [2]
- A Chinese systematic review of 30 studies and 633,917 participants pooled catastrophic health expenditure at 12.8% (95% CI 12.2%-13.3%) among insured people and 16.2% (15.4%-16.9%) among uninsured people. This was a heterogeneous body of observational evidence with significant publication bias, and only one included study concerned commercial insurance; it cannot establish causality or compare personal products [3]
- The Oregon Medicaid lottery experiment in low-income US adults illustrates why endpoints must remain separate: in year one, utilization rose while out-of-pocket costs and medical debt fell [4]; at two years, financial strain and depression screening outcomes improved, but several measured physical-health outcomes did not improve significantly [5]. This US public-insurance policy experiment did not measure all-cause mortality and cannot be converted into guidance on Chinese personal commercial-insurance portfolios

### Coverage goals that should not be conflated
- **Basic medical insurance** is social medical insurance concerned with enrollment, settlement, and covered medical expenses; evidence about a system reform is not evidence for a specific personal contract
- **Commercial medical insurance** pays under the contract for covered medical-behavior-related expenses or a stated amount. China's current health-insurance rules recognize both expense-reimbursement and fixed-benefit medical insurance; deductibles, provider/drug scope, pre-existing-condition rules, renewal provisions, and exclusions still depend on the contract [6]
- **Critical-illness insurance** generally pays when a contract-defined disease, condition, or procedure meets its definition. It is not reimbursement of actual medical expenses and is not the same as the serious-illness layer within China's basic medical-insurance system [6]
- **Life insurance** covers human life and body and may pay on contract-defined death, survival, disease, disability, age, or term conditions. Its role here is household financial-risk management, not evidence about access to treatment [7]

These are functional distinctions only; product names do not replace contract definitions.

### ⚠️ Limitations
- Enrollment does not mean every disease, medicine, or service is fully reimbursed
- Utilization, delayed care, medical debt, catastrophic health expenditure, clinical measurements, and mortality are different endpoints; improvement in one does not establish improvement in the others
- Chinese pooled catastrophic-expenditure estimates vary with insurance scheme, region, income, age, disease, and outcome definition; differences in who enrolls and who seeks care can also confound comparisons [3]
- Commercial-policy terms vary by region, age, health status, product version, and date; prices and benefits quoted in older articles may be obsolete
- This entry has no evidence that any portfolio of commercial medical, critical-illness, or life insurance lowers all-cause mortality or extends life
- This entry is not individualized insurance, legal, or financial advice and cannot predict whether a claim will be paid

## Do
1. Confirm that each family member's basic medical insurance is active, paid, and usable [1][2]
2. List separately the risks the household could not absorb—medical out-of-pocket costs, cash needs after a diagnosis, prolonged income loss, dependent support, or disabling injury—then match contract type to risk rather than treating them as one problem
3. Compare waiting periods, deductibles or benefit structure, covered services, pre-existing-condition rules, provider networks, renewal terms, exclusions, and total premiums in the full contract [6][7]
4. Answer the insurer's health-disclosure questions truthfully and retain the application record, policy, medical records, itemized bills, and invoices [7]
5. Ask a licensed institution, regulator, or independent legal/financial professional to clarify complex terms

## Don't
1. Don't treat a sales summary, product nickname, or headline coverage limit as the amount actually payable
2. Don't conceal medical history or decide on the insurer's behalf that a condition need not be disclosed
3. Don't use commercial insurance as a substitute for basic insurance, necessary treatment, or emergency savings
4. Don't buy from premium, deductible, or renewal figures copied from an outdated article

## Medical Red Flags
- In an emergency, call emergency services and seek care first; do not delay treatment for preauthorization or claim notification. Contact the insurer after the patient is stabilized
- When hospitalization, surgery, or expensive medicines are anticipated, check provider-network, authorization, and documentation requirements early, while keeping clinical need first
- For disputes over denial, exclusions, or health disclosure, preserve written records and use the insurer's appeal route, regulatory complaint channels, or legal advice
