# Religious Affiliation (Evidence Primarily Concerns Service Attendance)

**Mortality Impact**: ~ (no fixed causal effect generalizable across populations; some observational cohorts report lower all-cause mortality among religious-service attenders)
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (Multiple prospective cohorts support an association, but population and geographic heterogeneity, residual confounding, and reverse causation remain)
**Actionability**: Depends on personal beliefs and circumstances

## Conclusion
The stronger evidence concerns the **frequency of attending religious services or organizations**, not religious affiliation alone, and it does not prove that any belief extends life. Several US prospective cohorts report lower all-cause mortality among regular attenders, but the evidence is observational; associations are weaker in some secular European populations and differ by sex. Relative-risk estimates from US cohorts should not be treated as a guaranteed individual benefit.

## Evidence
- [Li et al. (2016)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27183175/) followed 74,534 US female nurses who were free of cardiovascular disease and cancer at baseline from 1996 to 2012. Compared with never-attenders, once-weekly attenders had an all-cause mortality HR of 0.74 (95% CI 0.70–0.78), and more-than-weekly attenders had an HR of 0.67 (95% CI 0.62–0.71). The exposure was self-reported service attendance, not religious affiliation; participants were predominantly White Christian female nurses. The paper estimated a restricted mean survival difference of 0.43 years (95% CI -0.09–1.54) for attending at least weekly, which does not support a multi-year longevity claim or any certain gain in years.
- [Chen et al. (2021)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32793951/) compared at-least-weekly with never attendance in the US NHSII and HRS cohorts and reported a combined all-cause mortality RR of 0.74 (95% CI 0.65–0.84); mortality was not an applicable outcome in the young GUTS cohort. The NHSII E-value was 2.04. This quantifies how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be to explain away the point estimate; it neither excludes confounding nor proves causality.
- [Chida et al. (2009)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19142047/) systematically reviewed prospective observational studies. Religiosity/spirituality was associated with lower mortality in initially healthy populations (pooled HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.76–0.87), but not in diseased populations (HR 0.98, 95% CI 0.94–1.01). The authors detected publication bias and advised caution.
- [Lindström and Pirouzifard (2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37635991/) studied 24,855 adults in southern Sweden. The association between attendance and all-cause mortality became non-significant after adding 12 measures of social participation (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.73–1.09). This suggests a possible social-network pathway but does not establish that it fully explains the association.
- [Ahrenfeldt et al. (2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36646924/) studied 2,987 Danes aged 40 years or older. The overall estimate was HR 0.70 (95% CI 0.50–0.99); an association was present among women (HR 0.56, 95% CI 0.35–0.89) but not clearly among men (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.59–1.53).
- [VanderWeele et al. (2017)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28338863/) studied 36,613 US Black women. After multivariable adjustment, religious/spiritual self-identification was not clearly associated with mortality (MRR 0.91, 95% CI 0.71–1.18), whereas frequent attendance remained associated (MRR 0.64, 95% CI 0.51–0.80). Affiliation or identity therefore cannot be used interchangeably with attendance.

## Limitations & Counter-Evidence
- **No causal inference**: No mortality trial randomly assigns religious belief or service attendance; all core evidence is observational.
- **Health selection and reverse causation**: Healthier and more mobile people may be more able to attend, while declining health may reduce attendance. Repeated measures, lagged analyses, and sensitivity analyses reduce but do not eliminate this problem.
- **Confounding versus mediation**: Smoking, alcohol use, exercise, social support, marriage, socioeconomic position, depression, and social participation may be confounders, mediators, or both. The Swedish association disappeared after adjustment for social participation.
- **Limited generalizability**: Major US studies disproportionately represent women and Christian settings; the Danish results differed by sex. Estimates cannot simply be transferred to other countries, religions, non-religious people, or clinical populations.
- **Measurement and publication bias**: Attendance is usually self-reported, and definitions of religiosity/spirituality vary. A later systematic review detected publication bias, so early positive estimates may be inflated.
- **Not absolute risk**: HRs and RRs are relative measures. These studies do not provide one absolute risk reduction that applies to everyone.

## What to Do
1. Participation in religion should follow personal belief, culture, and safety—not pursuit of a mortality percentage.
2. People who already hold religious beliefs may, if they wish, view safe and supportive services as one form of social participation; this evidence does not support clinicians prescribing religion.
3. Non-religious people do not need to attend religious services for health. Maintaining genuine, stable social ties is generally reasonable, but current evidence does not show that book clubs, sports clubs, volunteering, or pets have the same mortality effect as religious attendance.
4. If a religious group brings coercion, discrimination, financial exploitation, or obstruction of medical care, prioritize help from trusted people and appropriate professionals.
