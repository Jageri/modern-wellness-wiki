# Music and Dementia

**Mortality Impact**: Unknown; no evidence shows that music listening, instrument playing, or music therapy reduces all-cause mortality or extends life
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐ (Prevention evidence is observational; some RCT evidence addresses short-term symptoms in people with dementia, mostly with low-to-moderate certainty)
**Actionability**: Moderate (Reasonable as preference-based leisure or adjunctive care, not as a dementia-prevention prescription or disease-modifying treatment)

## Conclusion

Among community-dwelling adults aged 70 years or older who were initially free of dementia, one Australian cohort study found that self-reported "always" listening to music and "often/always" playing an instrument were associated with lower dementia incidence. This is an observational lifestyle association, not evidence that listening to music or learning an instrument prevents dementia. It also does not support a dose prescription such as "30 minutes daily."

For people already living with dementia, professionally delivered or therapeutically structured music-based interventions of at least five sessions may slightly improve depressive symptoms and overall behavioural problems at the end of treatment. They have not shown clear benefits for agitation/aggression, cognition, quality of life, or persistence after treatment. These short-term symptom outcomes must not be presented as slower disease progression, dementia prevention, or longer survival.

## First distinguish what "music" means

- **Everyday receptive listening**: A self-reported leisure exposure in the Jaffa cohort, not a randomized intervention and not a reproducible prescription for duration, volume, or genre.
- **Singing or instrument playing**: Active participation that can also mark earlier training, cognitive reserve, income, education, social engagement, and health. It cannot be combined with receptive listening as one causal effect.
- **Music therapy or therapeutic music-based intervention**: The Cochrane definition required a qualified music therapist or a therapeutic relationship meeting at least two criteria involving individualized goals, preference matching, active participation, or clinical indication/referral. Playing a recording without therapeutic interaction and ordinary recreational choir participation were not equivalent to this intervention.
- **Cognitive stimulation, reading, recreation, or multicomponent care**: These can independently provide attention, social contact, and emotional support. Against such active controls, music did not improve most outcomes.

## Prevention evidence: association only

- [Jaffa et al., *International Journal of Geriatric Psychiatry*, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41088732/) included 10,893 community-dwelling Australians aged ≥70 years without dementia at recruitment (PMID: 41088732). Outcomes were assessed from year 3 onward, and models adjusted for age, gender, and education.
- "Always" listening, compared with never/rarely/sometimes, was associated with 39% lower dementia risk (HR approximately 0.61, 95% CI 0.45–0.82) and 17% lower risk of cognitive impairment no dementia (CIND; HR approximately 0.83, 95% CI 0.74–0.92).
- Playing an instrument often/always was associated with 35% lower dementia risk (HR approximately 0.65, 95% CI 0.42–0.99, p=0.047), but not with CIND or change in cognitive test scores. The 35% was not an *additional* reduction on top of listening. Participants who both listened and played had 33% lower dementia risk (HR approximately 0.67, 95% CI 0.51–0.89); these percentages cannot be added.
- Always listening was associated with better global cognition and memory scores over time, but not other cognitive domains or subjective cognitive well-being. Associations were generally stronger among those with more than 16 years of education.
- [Arafa et al., *BMC Neurology*, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36303117/) pooled three prospective cohorts and also found instrument playing associated with lower dementia risk (HR 0.64, 95% CI 0.41–0.98; I²=23.3%; PMID: 36303117). However, there were only three cohorts, follow-up was relatively short, and reverse causation and healthy-participant bias remain possible. A three-study evidence base also cannot reliably exclude publication bias.

## Existing dementia: short-term symptoms are not disease progression

- [van der Steen et al., *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews*, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40049590/) included 30 RCTs with 1,720 participants; 28 studies with 1,366 participants contributed to meta-analysis (PMID: 40049590). Most participants lived in institutions, and most interventions mixed active and receptive musical elements.
- Compared with usual care, depressive symptoms probably improved slightly at the end of treatment (SMD -0.23, 95% CI -0.42 to -0.04; 9 studies, 441 participants; moderate certainty), while overall behavioural problems may have improved slightly (SMD -0.31, 95% CI -0.60 to -0.02; 10 studies, 385 participants; low certainty).
- There was no clear benefit over usual care for agitation/aggression (SMD -0.05, 95% CI -0.27 to 0.17; 11 studies, 503 participants), cognition (SMD 0.19, 95% CI -0.02 to 0.41; 7 studies, 353 participants), emotional well-being/quality of life, anxiety, or social behaviour.
- Compared with other activities such as reading, cooking, discussion, or cognitive stimulation, only social behaviour may have been better at the end of treatment (SMD 0.52, 95% CI 0.08–0.96; 4 studies, 84 participants; low certainty); other main outcomes showed no clear advantage.
- Follow-up from four weeks to six months after treatment found no long-term effect for any outcome. Participants and therapists could not be blinded, some outcome assessment carried bias, and adverse events were reported inconsistently, although no serious adverse events were reported.

## Caregiver outcomes and home-based multicomponent care

[Baker et al., *EClinicalMedicine*, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38106552/) randomized 432 person-with-dementia–caregiver dyads to caregiver-delivered music activities, reading, or usual care (PMID: 38106552). The music program combined singing, instrument playing, movement/dance, and listening; it was a multicomponent care intervention, not passive listening alone. At 90 days, enduring behavioural and psychological symptoms were not better with music than usual care (between-group difference -0.15, 95% CI -1.41 to 1.10, p=0.81). Patient cognition, depression, and quality of life did not differ at 90 or 180 days. Caregiver outcomes showed no advantage at 90 days except a borderline resilience result, and no caregiver secondary outcome differed at 180 days.

## Limitations and counter-evidence

- Prevention data come from a nonrandomized, self-reported secondary cohort analysis whose main models adjusted only for age, gender, and education. Early cognitive decline may reduce listening or instrument playing. Residual confounding by hearing, depression, income, social engagement, other leisure activities, and general health behaviour remains possible.
- ASPREE/ALSOP participants were community-dwelling Australians aged ≥70 years, and findings were stronger in the highly educated. Results should not be directly generalized to middle-aged adults, different cultural or educational groups, or less healthy populations.
- The instrument-playing meta-analysis contained only three cohorts, with a wide confidence interval close to the null. Existing studies do not show whether starting an instrument in later life prevents dementia.
- Therapeutic trials were mostly conducted in long-term-care institutions and varied substantially in content, intensity, individualization, and control conditions. [Lepping et al., *Journal of Alzheimer's Disease*, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38995790/) found that among 48 music-intervention studies, only 10 fully reported more than 5 of 11 intervention-description criteria, limiting replication and implementation (PMID: 38995790).
- All 30 trials in the Cochrane review had high performance-bias risk because participants and therapists could not be blinded; selective-reporting risk was unclear in 27 trials, and a funnel plot suggested some publication bias for overall behavioural problems.

## Do

1. If you already enjoy music, use listening, singing, playing, or music-based social activity as enjoyable leisure without chasing an unvalidated duration or genre.
2. For a person with dementia who has depressive or behavioural symptoms, discuss individualized, goal-directed music-based therapy with the care team and track whether the target symptom actually improves.
3. Use a comfortable volume. Stop if the person becomes distressed, fatigued, painful, or clearly refuses, and respect preference and consent.

## Don't

1. Do not treat 39%, 35%, or 33% as a personal risk reduction achievable by intervention, and do not add these percentages.
2. Do not treat listening, singing/instrument playing, professional music therapy, and multicomponent care as the same intervention.
3. Do not use music in place of dementia assessment, medication review, hearing care, safety planning, or evaluation of treatable triggers such as pain, infection, or sleep disturbance.
4. Do not relabel a short-term mood or behavioural change as cognitive recovery, slower disease progression, dementia prevention, or longer survival.

## Medical Red Flags

- New or progressive changes in memory, orientation, language, judgment, or daily function warrant formal assessment.
- Sudden worsening of confusion, drowsiness, agitation, or aggression in a person with dementia requires evaluation for delirium, infection, pain, dehydration, constipation, and medication adverse effects; simply adding music is not enough.
- New unilateral weakness, facial asymmetry, slurred speech, altered consciousness, or sudden severe headache requires emergency care.
