# Depression

**Mortality Impact**: `~` No single percentage is transportable to an individual; cohort estimates vary substantially with comorbidity, severity, measurement, and confounder control
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Large cohort syntheses support an association with premature mortality; causality is unproven)
**Actionability**: High (depression is treatable; mortality estimates are not individual predictions)

## Conclusion
Depression is not an ordinary short-lived low mood. If depressed mood or loss of interest/pleasure is present most days for at least 2 weeks, with other symptoms or impaired daily functioning, seek a professional assessment. “Low mood + loss of interest for 2 weeks” is a prompt to seek care, not a self-diagnosis.

## How to interpret the mortality evidence
- A 2025 meta-analysis of 268 cohort studies included major depressive disorder or dysthymia, not any single elevated questionnaire score. All-cause mortality was RR=2.10 (95% CI 1.87–2.35) versus unmatched general-population controls and RR=1.29 (1.21–1.37) versus controls matched for physical comorbidities. Heterogeneity was I²=99.9% for both estimates, so these results cannot be reduced to a stable “+50%.”
- In the same analysis, relative risk was higher for suicide mortality (RR=9.89, 95% CI 7.59–12.88) and was also elevated for natural-cause mortality (RR=1.63, 1.51–1.75). These are population relative risks and do not predict whether an individual will die by suicide.
- A 2018 umbrella review synthesized 246 unique studies and about 3.83 million people. All summary associations were statistically significant, but none met its criteria for “convincing” evidence; evidence weakened in analyses using structured diagnostic interviews or stronger confounder adjustment.
- Depression commonly coexists with physical illness. Depression may affect sleep, activity, smoking or alcohol use, treatment adherence, and help-seeking; cancer, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, chronic pain, or their treatment may also cause depressive symptoms or independently raise mortality. Observational studies cannot exclude reverse causation or residual confounding.
- Current evidence supports “depression is associated with higher mortality.” It does not prove that depression itself causes a fixed percentage of deaths, or that treating depressive symptoms necessarily lowers all-cause mortality.

## Recognition and assessment
- A depressive episode usually involves depressed mood or loss of interest/pleasure most of the day, nearly every day, for at least 2 weeks, with changes in concentration, sleep, appetite, energy, self-worth, hope, or thoughts of death/self-harm.
- Symptom scales such as the PHQ-9 can support screening and follow-up but do not establish a diagnosis by themselves. Assessment should also consider functional impairment, severity, suicide risk, past mania or hypomania, substance use, medication effects, and physical illness.
- Chronic stress can be related to depression but is not synonymous with a depressive disorder; this entry does not combine their mortality estimates.

## Effective treatment and its limits
- Psychological treatments and antidepressants have randomized-trial evidence for reducing depressive symptoms. Choice should reflect severity, previous response, comorbidities, risk, and the person’s preferences. A 2023 synthesis of 409 trials supports CBT efficacy; a 2018 network meta-analysis of 522 trials found all 21 studied antidepressants more effective than placebo for acute treatment, with certainty ranging from moderate to very low.
- Exercise can be one treatment option. A 2024 network meta-analysis of 218 randomized trials found symptom improvement with walking/jogging, yoga, and strength training, but nearly all trials had risk-of-bias concerns and confidence was mostly low or very low. Exercise should not replace needed assessment, psychotherapy, or medication.
- Do not routinely use hs-CRP to diagnose “inflammatory depression,” and do not self-treat depression with anti-inflammatory drugs. Trials in people with elevated inflammation remain exploratory, have not shown higher remission rates, and do not justify self-prescribing.

## What to do
1. Arrange an appointment with primary care, a mental-health clinician, or a qualified therapist. Note symptom duration, sleep, appetite, functional changes, and all medicines/substances used.
2. Within your capacity, keep regular waking, meals, activity, and contact with someone you trust; break tasks into very small steps.
3. Work with a clinician to identify and manage chronic pain, cardiovascular or metabolic disease, sleep disorders, and alcohol or substance use.
4. After starting treatment, attend planned reviews and report benefit, adverse effects, and any change in suicidal thoughts.

## What not to do
1. Do not blame depression on weak will or tell someone to “cheer up.”
2. Do not use alcohol or other substances to self-medicate.
3. Do not start antidepressants, anti-inflammatory drugs, or supplements on your own, and do not stop antidepressants abruptly. Medicines can cause adverse effects such as nausea, sedation, weight or sexual-function changes; abrupt stopping can cause withdrawal symptoms. Plan tapering with the prescriber.
4. People aged 18–25, or anyone at increased suicide risk, need earlier and closer review after starting or increasing an antidepressant. This is not a reason to withhold treatment.

## Get urgent help
- If there are thoughts, plans, or preparations for suicide or self-harm, or a risk of acting now: do not remain alone, contact a trusted person and move away from means of harm, and contact local emergency services or go to an emergency department immediately.
- Severe self-neglect, inability to eat or drink, psychotic symptoms, or unusually elevated/irritable mood with markedly reduced need for sleep and risky impulsive behavior (possible mania) also require urgent assessment.
- Red flags are prompts to obtain help; they do not replace diagnosis or prescribing.
