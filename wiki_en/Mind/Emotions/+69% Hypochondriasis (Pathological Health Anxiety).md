# Hypochondriasis (Pathological Health Anxiety)

**Mortality association**: All-cause mortality HR 1.69 (95% CI 1.47–1.93)
**Evidence level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (one Swedish nationwide matched cohort; observational association awaiting independent replication)
**Actionability**: High (assessable and treatable)

> Last updated: 2026-09-06 | Entry author: Longevity Wiki AI

## Bottom line

In a Swedish nationwide matched cohort, 4,129 people with an ICD-10 diagnosis of hypochondriasis recorded in specialist care had a sociodemographically adjusted all-cause mortality HR of 1.69 (95% CI 1.47–1.93) versus 41,290 matched controls [1]. This was an observational register study: it cannot show that hypochondriasis caused the deaths, and its estimate should not be applied directly to ordinary health concerns, subclinical health anxiety, or populations in other countries.

Crude mortality rates were 8.5 versus 5.5 per 1,000 person-years (an absolute difference of about 3.0 per 1,000 person-years); 6.5% versus 4.3% died during the study period [1]. These unadjusted absolute figures help put the HR in context but are not an individual's lifetime risk.

## Evidence

### Swedish nationwide matched cohort

Mataix-Cols and colleagues linked Swedish national patient and cause-of-death registers. They included people first recorded with hypochondriasis during 1997–2020; mean follow-up was 7.7 years in both groups [1].

| Outcome | Adjusted HR (95% CI) | Events (hypochondriasis/control) |
|---|---:|---:|
| **All-cause mortality** | **1.69 (1.47–1.93)** | 268/1,761 |
| Natural causes | 1.60 (1.38–1.85) | 232/1,646 |
| Circulatory disease | 1.52 (1.21–1.92) | 87/598 |
| Respiratory disease | 2.33 (1.50–3.61) | 26/116 |
| Neoplasms | 0.99 (0.74–1.33) | 52/496; no statistically significant difference |
| Unnatural causes | 2.43 (1.61–3.68) | 36/115 |
| Suicide | 4.14 (2.44–7.03) | 29/53 |

An HR of 1.69 is a relative hazard ratio. It does not mean that everyone loses 69% of their lifespan or that absolute mortality rises by 69 percentage points. The model adjusted for birth year, sex, county, country of birth, education, civil status, and family income [1].

### Comorbidity and causal boundaries

- The diagnosed group was 56.7% female and had a median age of 34.5 years at first diagnosis. A total of 85.7% had at least one other recorded lifetime psychiatric diagnosis, including anxiety-related disorders in 78.4% and depressive disorders in 48.4% [1].
- All-cause mortality associations remained statistically significant but attenuated when psychiatric comorbidities were added one at a time: HR 1.23 (1.05–1.44) after further adjustment for depressive disorders and 1.34 (1.12–1.59) after adjustment for anxiety-related disorders [1].
- The suicide association was no longer clear after further adjustment for depressive or anxiety-related disorders (HR 1.37, 95% CI 0.71–2.64; and HR 1.86, 95% CI 1.00–3.44, respectively) [1]. Suicide mortality therefore should not be attributed simply to hypochondriasis itself; comorbidity and modeling choices matter.
- Chronic stress, lifestyle, avoidance of care, under-recognition, and access to treatment were proposed mechanisms, not tested mediators. The study did not show that treating health anxiety reduces mortality [1].

### Contrary evidence and uncertainty

This was the first large study directly examining all-cause and cause-specific mortality in clinically diagnosed hypochondriasis, and a comparable independent replication has not yet been established [1]. The paper also notes that an earlier systematic review suggested suicide attempts might be less frequent in hypochondriasis, although the included studies had methodological limitations. This conflicts with the cohort's suicide-death association and requires further study [1].

## Applicability and limitations

- **Population**: People with a formally recorded ICD-10 hypochondriasis diagnosis in Swedish specialist care—not everyone who worries about health.
- **Selection and misclassification**: Primary-care data were unavailable and hypochondriasis is markedly underdiagnosed in Sweden. The diagnosed group may represent more severe cases, while undiagnosed cases may have entered the control group.
- **Residual confounding**: Register data cannot exclude incompletely measured smoking, alcohol use, physical-disease severity, or patterns of healthcare use. Some socioeconomic covariates were measured at the end of follow-up and may be confounders or mediators.
- **Cause-of-death precision**: Some cause-specific counts were small, and classifying deaths of undetermined intent as suicide can introduce misclassification. Generalizability across cultures and healthcare systems is uncertain.
- **Publication and replication**: The mortality conclusion currently rests mainly on one study, so publication bias cannot be assessed well and independent national replication is still needed.

## What can help

Pathological health anxiety is treatable. A 2019 systematic review of 19 randomized trials found a pooled between-group effect of CBT on health anxiety of Hedges' g 0.79 (95% CI 0.57–1.01), falling to 0.62 after adjustment for publication bias; benefits were largely sustained for 12–18 months [2]. A 2026 network meta-analysis of 35 trials and 3,263 adults supported CBT and third-wave CBT as reasonable first-line psychological treatments, but these trials assessed symptoms—not mortality [3].

1. If worry consumes substantial time, drives repeated checking or medical visits, leads to avoidance of care, or impairs daily life, seek assessment from a mental-health professional or clinician familiar with health anxiety.
2. Agree on a planned follow-up and testing approach with one clinician while still obtaining appropriate medical evaluation for new, persistent, or worsening physical symptoms. Do not automatically label every symptom as anxiety.
3. Discuss CBT that includes components such as exposure and response prevention and cognitive restructuring. Treatment does not help everyone: a review of unwanted outcomes found that about 10% reported at least one adverse event and 17% dropped out, with substantial heterogeneity across studies [4].
4. If self-harm or suicidal thoughts occur, contact local emergency or crisis services immediately, or ask a trusted person to accompany you to urgent care.

## Avoid

1. Do not treat this cohort HR as an individual's destiny or as proof of causation.
2. Do not rely on repeated body checking, repeated non-indicated tests, or endless symptom searching for short-lived reassurance; a therapist can help plan a gradual reduction in safety behaviors.
3. Do not ignore new red-flag symptoms because of a health-anxiety diagnosis, and do not dismiss genuine distress because tests have been normal so far.
