# Insufficient Sleep

**Mortality Impact**: ~
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (observational association; insufficient causal evidence for mortality benefit from intervention)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion

Adults should prioritize sufficient, regular, restorative sleep. The AASM/SRS recommends that adults aged 18–60 regularly sleep at least 7 hours per night. CDC reference ranges are ≥7 hours for ages 18–60, 7–9 hours for ages 61–64, and 7–8 hours for ages 65 and older. Sleep need varies; "6 hours" should not be treated as a universal safe floor.

## Evidence

### Observational association

- **Cappuccio et al. meta-analysis (2010)**: 16 prospective studies with 1,382,999 participants. Definitions of "short sleep" varied across studies and sleep duration was questionnaire-reported. Short sleep was associated with all-cause mortality (RR=1.12, 95% CI 1.06–1.18), with between-study heterogeneity (I²=39%). This is a relative risk, not a 12-percentage-point increase in absolute risk. [PMID: 20469800](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20469800/)
- **UK Biobank prospective cohort (2021)**: Among 407,500 participants, self-reported ≤5 hours versus 7 hours was associated with all-cause mortality (HR=1.25, 95% CI 1.16–1.34). This estimate applies to that specific contrast; it cannot be generalized to every person sleeping <6 or <7 hours. [PMID: 33819843](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33819843/)
- **Age differences**: In a meta-analysis of 28 articles and 95,259 older adults, the pooled estimate for short sleep was HR=1.04 (95% CI 1.00–1.09), only marginally significant. The association was significant in men but not women. Region, measurement method, baseline age, and follow-up interval were possible sources of heterogeneity. [PMID: 32723316](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32723316/)
- **Long sleep is not proven to be a harmful intervention**: Cohorts often associate long sleep with higher mortality, but underlying disease, fragmented sleep, reduced functional status, and employment or social factors can cause both longer sleep and higher mortality. Reverse causation and residual confounding remain plausible.

> **Why the headline number was withdrawn**: "+12–24%" combined different short-sleep thresholds, ages, reference groups, and effect measures into an apparently universal all-cause mortality effect. That claim is not supportable. Large population studies are mainly observational and mostly use self-reported sleep; they do not provide a universally applicable absolute risk difference.

### Intervention evidence

- A single-center RCT included 80 adults aged 21–40 with BMI 25.0–29.9 who habitually slept <6.5 hours. Individualized sleep-hygiene counseling increased actigraphy-measured sleep by about 1.2 hours/night versus control and reduced energy intake by 270 kcal/day. The intervention lasted only 2 weeks and mortality was not an outcome. It supports the feasibility of extending sleep and a short-term metabolic-behavioral effect, not a mortality reduction. [PMID: 35129580](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35129580/)

## Do

1. Allow a stable opportunity for at least 7 hours of sleep and record bedtime, rise time, awakenings, and daytime alertness for 1–2 weeks.
2. Keep a broadly consistent rise time and reduce pre-bed bright light, caffeine, and alcohol interference.
3. If adequate opportunity is available but sleep remains difficult, do not simply extend time in bed; assessment for insomnia or another sleep disorder is more useful.

## Medical and Safety Boundaries

- **Insomnia**: Difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, or poor sleep quality, despite adequate opportunity, together with daytime impairment. Symptoms ≥3 times/week for ≥3 months meet the time/frequency boundary for chronic insomnia and warrant clinical assessment. Multicomponent cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is first-line; sleep hygiene should not be the sole treatment. [AASM insomnia resources](https://aasm.org/clinical-resources/insomnia-toolkit/)
- **Sleepiness / apnea clues**: Excessive daytime sleepiness despite adequate sleep, especially with frequent loud snoring, witnessed breathing pauses, or gasping, should be discussed with a clinician and may require a sleep study. [NHLBI sleep-apnea symptoms](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep-apnea/symptoms)
- **Immediate safety**: Do not drive or operate hazardous equipment when sleepy.
