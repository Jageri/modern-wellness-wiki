# Blue Light Exposure

**Mortality Impact**: No direct evidence that screen blue light or a specific blue-light spectrum changes all-cause mortality or lifespan
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐ (The circadian mechanism of nighttime light is reasonably established; evidence for screens, glasses, eye disease, and lifespan outcomes is insufficient or inconsistent)
**Actionability**: Moderate

## Conclusion
"Blue light" is not a single on/off exposure. Circadian effects depend jointly on spectrum, intensity at the eye, duration, timing, and an individual's prior light history. Higher melanopic-weighted light input at night can suppress or delay the melatonin rhythm and prolong sleep latency in a controlled experiment, but this does not establish that all screen use causes insomnia [1][2].

If pre-sleep screen use displaces sleep, is arousing, or worsens your own sleep, prioritize shortening use, lowering brightness, and reducing stimulating content. Evidence does not establish a universal requirement to avoid screens for 2 hours before bed, nor does it show that night mode or blue-light-blocking glasses reliably improves objective sleep outcomes [3][4][5][6][9].

## Evidence
- **Spectrum is not the only variable**: A 2019 systematic review included 128 reports, but only 15 met quality criteria requiring at least 20 participants and adequate reporting of light parameters. Evening exposure at 460 nm suppressed melatonin, but the strongest suppression in the review occurred at the shorter 424 nm wavelength; 631 nm red light and low-level nighttime light could also produce circadian responses [1].
- **Controlled evidence supports dose-and-timing effects**: In a 2023 randomized experiment, 72 healthy men were exposed during the 4 hours before habitual bedtime to display light varying in luminance and melanopic irradiance. Higher melanopic irradiance was associated with longer sleep latency, lower melatonin concentrations, and later melatonin onset. The findings cannot be assumed to apply to women, older adults, or unconstrained everyday screen use [2].
- **Objective sleep benefits from blue-light-blocking glasses are unestablished**: A 2025 meta-analysis included only 3 double-blind crossover RCTs with 49 participants in total. Pooled effects were nonsignificant for sleep-onset latency (MD −4.86 min, 95% CI −20.23 to 10.52), total sleep time (MD +8.75 min, 95% CI −35.31 to 52.82), sleep efficiency (MD −0.61 percentage points, 95% CI −7.58 to 6.35), and wake after sleep onset (MD −1.47 min, 95% CI −14.94 to 11.99). Samples were small, interventions lasted about 1 week, and no study reported actual melanopic dose at the eye [3].
- **Subjective sleep and ocular outcomes are also uncertain**: Cochrane 2023 included 17 RCTs (5–156 participants each; follow-up less than 1 day to 5 weeks). Six sleep-quality RCTs with 148 participants were split: 3 reported improvement and 3 no difference; certainty was very low. The review found no RCT data that evaluated macular health or serum melatonin, so it cannot support claims either that routine screen blue light causes macular disease or that filtering lenses prevent it [4].
- **Screen use is not equivalent to blue-light exposure**: In a randomized pilot trial of 38 people, avoiding mobile phones for 30 minutes before bed for 4 weeks improved self-reported sleep latency and duration. The intervention could not separate spectrum and brightness from content arousal, notifications, or bedtime displacement [5]. A 2024 National Sleep Foundation panel reached consensus that general screen use and pre-sleep content impair sleep in children and adolescents, but did not reach consensus for adults or for harm from pre-sleep screen light [6].
- **Mortality studies concern ambient light, not screen blue light**: In an observational UK Biobank analysis, 88,905 people wore a light sensor for only 1 week and were followed for a mean of 8 years. Brighter personal nighttime light was associated with higher all-cause mortality, but the study did not identify screens, spectrum, or blue-light dose and cannot prove causation; several authors disclosed lighting-related patents, companies, or industry funding [7]. Counterevidence comes from another UK Biobank analysis of 273,335 people: when outdoor nighttime light was estimated by satellite, the natural-cause mortality association disappeared after further adjustment for PM2.5 and evening noise (highest vs lowest quartile HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.97–1.08) [8].
- **Night-mode evidence is mainly optical and modeled, not a sleep outcome**: One study measured 64 smartphones and 4 lenses, then modeled melatonin suppression from spectra and previous data. The warmest night-mode settings reduced the modeled value, but no participants used the phones and neither melatonin nor sleep was measured [9].

## Do
1. If screen use delays bedtime, set a practical stopping point. "30 minutes earlier" can be a personal trial starting point, not a universal medical threshold.
2. Lower screen and room brightness at night. Treat night mode as an adjunct that reduces short-wavelength content, not as a way to cancel the effects of prolonged use.
3. Maintain a regular light-dark pattern: obtain outdoor/daylight exposure during the day and keep the sleep period as dark as practical. There is no need to filter daytime light continuously because of generic "blue-light" concerns.
4. To test whether a change helps you, keep wake time stable, record bedtime, sleep onset, and awakenings for 1–2 weeks, and change one factor at a time.

## Don't
1. Do not treat studies of "screen time" as studies of blue-light spectrum, or short-term melatonin changes as proof of chronic disease or lifespan effects.
2. Do not buy expensive blue-light-blocking glasses on promises of preventing macular disease or guaranteeing sleep; current RCTs have not established those benefits.
3. Do not treat phone night mode as insomnia therapy, and do not ignore notifications, content arousal, or sleep time displaced by continued use.

## Medical Red Flags
- Seek clinical assessment when insomnia persists and impairs daytime function, or occurs with loud snoring/gasping, restless legs, or mood symptoms; do not attribute these problems only to blue light.
- Seek prompt eye care for sudden vision loss, a visual-field defect, a sudden increase in flashes/floaters, or eye pain; these are not symptoms to self-treat with blue-light-blocking glasses.
