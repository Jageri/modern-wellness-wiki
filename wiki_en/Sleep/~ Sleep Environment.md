# Sleep Environment

**Mortality Impact**: No direct evidence was found that changing bedroom temperature, noise, light, ventilation, or bedding improves all-cause mortality or lifespan; available studies mainly assess sleep stages, nocturnal awakenings, sleep duration, and self-reported sleep disturbance
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (Noise has systematic-review and exposure-response evidence; evidence for temperature, light, ventilation, and bedding is mostly small, short-term, or observational)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion

Excess heat or humid heat, transportation noise, and inadequate ventilation can impair some sleep outcomes. The timing and intensity of light may also affect sleep-wake rhythms, but findings from habitual settings are inconsistent. The most defensible approach is to make the bedroom thermally comfortable, as quiet and dark as practical, and adequately ventilated when outdoor air is safe. Current evidence does not support treating `18–22°C`, `CO2 <1000 ppm`, or one bedding type as universal thresholds for everyone.

## Evidence

- **Temperature and humidity**: A 2012 review reported that, in real-life settings with bedding and sleepwear, heat exposure can increase wakefulness and reduce slow-wave and REM sleep; humid heat adds thermal strain, while bedding modifies the effects of cold exposure [1]. The reviewed experiments varied substantially in conditions, clothing, and population, so they do not establish one universally optimal bedroom temperature.
- **Noise**: A 2022 update of the WHO evidence review included 36 transportation-noise studies with 173,160 responses. When survey questions named noise, each 10-dB increase in nighttime noise was associated with high sleep disturbance with ORs of 2.18 (95% CI 2.01–2.36) for aircraft, 2.52 (2.28–2.79) for roads, and 2.97 (2.57–3.43) for rail, with moderate-certainty evidence. Effects were smaller and evidence was low to very low certainty when questions did not mention noise [2]. These are associations between outdoor nighttime transportation noise and self-reported disturbance, not a universal conversion such as “each minute above a threshold adds a fixed number of awake minutes.”
- **Light**: A 2021 systematic review included 25 habitual-light studies in healthy adults; all were cross-sectional, most measured light at the wrist, and overall quality was low. There was limited evidence linking the amount and timing of light with rest-activity rhythm and some sleep-architecture estimates, but findings for circadian phase, sleep estimates, sleep quality, and mood were conflicting [3]. Reducing unnecessary bright light at night is reasonable, but blackout conditions have not been shown to improve longevity or long-term health.
- **Ventilation and CO2**: A 2023 single-blind field intervention followed 29 mechanically ventilated bedrooms for four weeks. In subsets with clear ventilation contrasts, wearable-estimated deep sleep was shorter under low ventilation; morning cognitive performance did not differ [4]. CO2 served as an indicator of ventilation and changed alongside relative humidity. This small study does not establish CO2 itself as the cause or validate `1000 ppm` as a universal sleep-outcome cutoff.
- **Air pollution**: A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis pooled observational studies in middle-aged and older adults. Each 10 μg/m³ increase in long-term PM2.5 exposure was associated with a composite “poor sleep health” outcome (OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.12–1.43), whereas ozone was not significantly associated (OR 1.04, 0.88–1.24) [5]. The analysis combined heterogeneous outcomes such as poor sleep quality, low efficiency, and OSA, and was largely observational; it does not prove that air cleaning improves sleep or lifespan.
- **Mattresses and bedding**: A 2015 review of 24 controlled trials concluded that subjectively defined medium-firm or self-adjusted mattresses may improve comfort, sleep quality, and spinal alignment [6]. However, a 2025 meta-analysis of nine RCTs in healthy adults found no clear differences between body-cooling bedding strategies and usual bedding in sleep latency, sleep efficiency, sleep stages, wake after sleep onset, or total sleep time; certainty was very low to low [7]. A separate 2024 review of fibre types included only nine heterogeneous studies, found effects depended on population and temperature, and disclosed that two authors received research funding or a scholarship from Australian Wool Innovation [8].

### Limitations and causal boundary

- Environmental conditions, sleepwear, bedding, age, comorbidity, and sensitivity to noise and light differ between people; a single cutoff cannot be generalized to everyone.
- Most interventions covered few nights, enrolled small samples, and relied on self-report or wearables. They did not test cardiovascular events, all-cause mortality, or lifespan.
- Noise and air-pollution evidence is largely observational and may be confounded by housing, socioeconomic conditions, and baseline health. Association is not causation.
- Bedding research is heterogeneous and some of the field is industry-funded; no single fibre should be promoted as universally superior.

## Do

1. Adjust room temperature, sleepwear, and bedding for thermal comfort. In humid heat, prioritize cooling and heat dissipation rather than chasing one fixed temperature.
2. Reduce audible nighttime transportation noise by first closing windows, changing bedroom location, or sealing sound leaks. Earplugs are an individual option, but preserve awareness of smoke alarms and emergency signals.
3. Reduce unnecessary bright and direct light at night. If you need to get up, use lighting bright enough to walk safely.
4. Ventilate when outdoor air quality, noise, security, and temperature allow. Do not lower CO2 by bringing in heavy outdoor pollution or noise.
5. Choose a mattress based on comfort, absence of persistent pain, and suitable support. “Medium-firm” is a subjective category, not a standardized engineering value.

## Don't

1. Do not treat a consumer CO2 reading as a diagnosis of sleep quality or health risk.
2. Do not turn one small short-term experiment into temperature, decibel, or bedding cutoffs that everyone must follow.
3. Do not market light blocking, added ventilation, or a bedding purchase as proven to extend life.

## Medical Red Flags

- Loud snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, nocturnal choking, or excessive daytime sleepiness: seek evaluation for sleep apnea rather than only modifying the bedroom.
- Persistent insomnia or insomnia that materially impairs daytime function: seek clinical assessment; environmental changes do not replace insomnia treatment.
- Suspected carbon monoxide, smoke, or other harmful exposure at home or work: leave the exposure and contact local emergency/medical services; do not assume the symptoms are ordinary poor sleep.
