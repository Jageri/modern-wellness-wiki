# Sun Exposure

**Mortality Impact**: Uncertain; observational studies of long-term sunlight exposure and all-cause mortality are inconsistent, and no eligible randomized trial has shown that sun exposure extends life [1]
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐ (Limited and conflicting evidence)
**Actionability**: Moderate

## Conclusion
Outdoor activity can provide bright daytime light and opportunities for exercise, but deliberately exposing oneself to ultraviolet (UV) radiation for longevity is not supported by reliable clinical evidence. Associations between sunlight and lower mortality may be influenced by exercise, baseline health, socioeconomic conditions, travel, and healthy-volunteer bias; UV damage to skin and eyes is established.

## Evidence
- A 2025 NIHR systematic review searched through November 2023. Of 55 main articles, only eight assessed all-cause mortality; among nine analyses, five pointed toward benefit and four toward harm. It found no eligible randomized trials, and the relevant studies had high risk of bias, heterogeneous exposure measures, and insufficient data by skin type [1].
- The Swedish MISS cohort followed 29,518 women aged 25–64 years at baseline for 20 years. The highest self-reported sun-exposure group had an all-cause mortality HR of 0.62 (95% CI 0.50–0.80) versus the lowest group, but the systematic review judged this result at high risk of bias [1,2]. A later analysis of the same cohort estimated 0.6–2.1 fewer years of life expectancy among sun avoiders, not that people with skin cancer lived six years longer [3]. These figures are not individual causal effects.
- A UK Biobank cohort restricted to participants of White European ancestry used solarium use and residential shortwave radiation as exposure proxies; both were associated with lower all-cause mortality [4]. The findings remain observational: solarium use may represent other sun-seeking behaviors, and residential radiation is not a personal dose.
- Daytime light is an environmental signal for the circadian system, but a systematic review of habitual light exposure in healthy adults found that all 25 included studies were cross-sectional, overall quality was low, and associations with circadian phase, sleep, and mood were inconsistent [5]. Bright daytime light does not require unprotected skin exposure to UV.
- UVB supports cutaneous vitamin D synthesis, but an association between low 25(OH)D and mortality does not show that sun exposure or supplements extend life. In the D-Health randomized trial of 21,315 Australians aged 60 years or older, monthly vitamin D₃ 60,000 IU versus placebo did not reduce all-cause mortality (HR 1.04, 95% CI 0.93–1.18) [6].

## Risks and Boundaries
- IARC classifies solar radiation as carcinogenic to humans. WHO identifies UV as a primary cause of skin cancer and a cause of eye damage, and recommends sun protection when the UV Index (UVI) is 3 or higher [7,8].
- Sunburn, especially during childhood and adolescence, increases later skin-cancer risk. Sunbeds should not be used to obtain vitamin D [7].
- Skin pigmentation, latitude, season, cloud cover, altitude, medications, and baseline skin-cancer risk all change the benefit–harm balance. Current mortality evidence cannot define a universal number of "safe unprotected minutes."

## Do
1. Spend time outdoors during the day, but do not look directly at the sun or aim for tanning, redness, or sunburn.
2. Check the local UVI. At 3 or higher, limit exposure around solar noon; prioritize shade, covering clothing, a broad-brimmed hat, and UV-protective eyewear, and use broad-spectrum sunscreen on uncovered skin. Do not use sunscreen to extend time in intense sun [7].
3. Do not use sunbeds or tanning lamps.
4. Do not self-prescribe a 25(OH)D target above 30 ng/mL or 1,000–4,000 IU/day for longevity. The 2024 Endocrine Society guideline advises against routine 25(OH)D screening in healthy adults, identifies no universal prevention target, and advises against empiric supplementation above current dietary reference intakes in healthy adults younger than 75 years [9].
5. People who are housebound, cover nearly all skin, have deeply pigmented skin at high latitude, or have established risk factors such as bone disease or malabsorption should discuss food, supplementation, and testing with a clinician rather than use intense sun exposure as a substitute for assessment [7,9].
