# Noise Pollution

**Mortality Impact**: No stable, broadly generalizable all-cause mortality effect estimate
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (Long-term observational studies; mortality findings are inconsistent)
**Actionability**: Medium

## Conclusion
Long-term transportation noise is associated with higher cardiovascular risk, but pooled all-cause mortality estimates are inconsistent and causality has not been established by randomized trials. This entry therefore does not use a numeric mortality prefix. Noise reduction can still reduce annoyance and sleep disruption; whether it extends life remains uncertain.

## First clarify what the decibel metric means
- `Lden` in these studies is the A-weighted long-term day-evening-night sound level, usually averaged over a year at the most-exposed residential façade; evening and nighttime periods receive 5 dB and 10 dB penalties. It is not a one-time phone reading beside the bed.
- `Lnight` is the long-term nighttime average. WHO 2018 recommends reducing road, railway, and aircraft noise below 45, 44, and 40 dB `Lnight`, respectively; the corresponding `Lden` guideline values are 53, 54, and 45 dB. These are outdoor long-term exposure metrics in a European guideline, not a universal rule that every bedroom must be below 40 dB.

## Evidence
- A 2021 mortality systematic review included 13 studies. Per 10 dB higher road-traffic `Lden`, the RR was 1.01 (95% CI 0.98–1.05; 5 studies, I²=78%) for non-accidental mortality and 1.01 (0.98–1.05) for cardiovascular mortality; the authors judged the mortality evidence weak overall. [Cai et al., 2021; PMID 33307398](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33307398/)
- A different 2022 UK Biobank analysis and cohort meta-analysis reported a pooled all-cause mortality RR of 1.05 (1.02–1.07) per 10 dB higher road-traffic noise. In UK Biobank the association was more apparent in men and was not significant in women; this remains observational evidence. [Hao et al., 2022; PMID 35358546](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35358546/)
- A 2025 Danish prospective cohort included only 26,723 men aged 65–74. An interquartile-range increase of 14.9 dB road-traffic `Lden` was associated with all-cause mortality HR 1.081 (1.027–1.137). This cannot be directly generalized to women, younger people, or other settings, and it is not an 8.1% increase per 10 dB. [Mayntz et al., 2025; PMID 40403531](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40403531/)
- For cardiovascular incidence, a 2025 systematic review included 20 longitudinal studies and more than 8.4 million people, mostly in Europe. Per 10 dB higher road-noise `Lden`, pooled RRs for IHD, MI, and stroke were 1.017 (0.990–1.044), 1.029 (1.011–1.048), and 1.025 (1.009–1.041), respectively; the overall linear IHD estimate was not statistically significant, while MI and stroke estimates were. [Pershagen et al., 2025; PMID 40444274](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40444274/)
- A 2023 systematic review found that associations between transportation noise and cardiovascular outcomes persisted after air-pollution adjustment in most, but not all, studies; evidence for noise-air-pollution interaction remained unclear. [Eminson et al., 2023; PMID 37182833](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37182833/)
- A 2026 review judged evidence for auditory injury well established and noted growing epidemiological and mechanistic evidence for non-auditory outcomes, while also describing causal inference as difficult for several outcomes. Its first author disclosed consulting and paid-expert-testimony relationships; the other authors reported no relevant relationships. [Basner et al., 2026; PMID 42081987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42081987/)
- A 2026 randomized crossover laboratory study included only 25 healthy young adults over seven nights. Earplugs mitigated most sleep effects of intermittent 45–65 dBA environmental noise but began to fail at the highest level; continuous pink noise reduced REM sleep. The trial did not test long-term cardiovascular outcomes or mortality. [Basner et al., 2026; PMID 41627391](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41627391/)

## Limitations
- Most evidence concerns modeled residential transportation noise in Europe and other high-income settings; it cannot automatically be generalized to occupational noise, neighbor noise, headphone exposure, or every country.
- Address-based models may miss bedroom orientation, insulation, open windows, moving, and personal mobility; exposure misclassification can alter estimates.
- Transportation noise correlates with air pollution, socioeconomic conditions, and residential selection; statistical adjustment cannot eliminate residual confounding.
- Mortality meta-analyses disagree, some include few studies, and heterogeneity can be high. There is no reliable estimate of years of life gained or lost.
- An intervention systematic review found evidence thinly distributed across interventions and outcomes, with diverse designs and high risk of bias in most studies; it could not pool noise reduction against health outcomes. There is no evidence that earplugs or home soundproofing reduce mortality. [Brown & van Kamp, 2017; PMID 28771220](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28771220/)

## Do
1. Identify the main source and timing first. For a persistent problem, prefer assessment by a qualified service or a standards-based sound-level meter; do not equate a single phone reading with `Lden` or `Lnight`.
2. Reduce noise along the source-path-receiver chain: control the source where possible; at home, consider sealing door and window gaps, moving the bedroom to a quieter façade, or professionally assessed sound insulation.
3. If nighttime noise cannot be removed promptly, properly fitted earplugs may be tried. Existing randomized evidence supports only short-term laboratory sleep effects, not long-term cardiovascular or longevity benefit.

## Don't
1. Do not mask environmental noise with loud headphones or continuously loud “white noise”; that adds sound exposure.
2. Do not treat one generic dB value as a safety threshold for every source, indoor/outdoor location, and averaging period.
3. Do not claim that earplugs, thick curtains, or double glazing have been proven to prevent cardiovascular events or extend life.
