# Microplastics

**Mortality Impact**: Unknown; there is no reliable human causal evidence from which to estimate all-cause mortality or lifespan change
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐ (Human detection and association studies are growing, but bias and measurement concerns are substantial)
**Actionability**: Low (no direct evidence that an everyday behavior lowers particle burden or that any intervention improves clinical outcomes)

## Conclusion

Microplastics and nanoplastics have been reported in multiple types of human samples, and some studies associate detected burden with inflammatory markers or clinical events. These findings do not establish that plastic particles cause disease, death, or shorter lifespan. Measurement validity, contamination control, reverse causation, and residual confounding remain central concerns. Low-cost precautionary choices are reasonable when they do not compromise safe water, food hygiene, or nutrition, but “plastic detox” products and unvalidated exposure tests are not evidence-based.[1][5]

## Human evidence: detection is not disease

- A 2026 systematic review of in-vivo human evidence included 25 studies: 14 cross-sectional, 9 case-control, 1 prospective cohort, and 1 quasi-experimental study. Five cardiovascular studies included 454 participants. Detection methods and results were inconsistent, confounding and exposure-measurement bias were commonly moderate to high, and heterogeneity precluded meta-analysis.[1]
- A 2024 NEJM prospective observational study enrolled 304 patients undergoing carotid endarterectomy for asymptomatic carotid disease; 257 completed a mean 33.7 months of follow-up. The composite of myocardial infarction, stroke, or all-cause death occurred in 30/150 (20.0%) with plaque MNPs detected and 8/107 (7.5%) without detection; adjusted HR 4.53 (95% CI 2.00–10.27). This is a composite association in a selected, high-risk surgical population—not “4.5-fold higher all-cause mortality”—and it cannot be translated into a general-population lifespan effect.[2]
- An independent 2025 prospective observational study enrolled 142 patients with myocardial infarction; 110 completed 31.5 months of follow-up. Coronary-blood PVC concentration was associated with major adverse cardiac events. This remains an observational association in a selected patient group, and PVC was measured using Py-GC-MS, so the matrix-interference concern applies directly.[8]
- A 2026 cross-sectional study of 61 patients undergoing coronary angiography reported a higher detection frequency in STEMI than in chronic coronary syndrome and normal-coronary controls. However, smoking history was the only independent predictor of MNP detection in multivariable analysis, highlighting correlated exposures and confounding.[9]
- A 2024 Korean cross-sectional study used micro-FTIR to report blood particles and associations with selected coagulation and inflammatory markers in 36 healthy adults. Procedural blanks also contained 0–5 particles (mean 1.67), and blank values were not subtracted. The small sample, single measurement, and cross-sectional design cannot establish temporality or causation.[3]

## Experimental mechanisms and systematic reviews

- A 2024 rapid systematic review included only three human observational studies; much of its hazard conclusion relied on 28 animal studies. Animal and in-vitro studies suggest possible pathways including oxidative stress, inflammation, and barrier injury, but commonly use selected polymers, regular particle shapes, and experimental doses that cannot be converted directly into real-world human risk.[4]
- Mechanistic evidence adds biological plausibility to possible harm, but it does not replace human dose-response data, long-term follow-up, or randomized intervention evidence. No evidence currently shows that a consumer behavior reduces clinical events or extends lifespan by reducing microplastic exposure.[1][4]

## Major measurement uncertainties

- A 2025 methods study found that Py-GC-MS measurement of polyethylene (PE) and polyvinyl chloride (PVC) in biological matrices is affected by matrix interference and nonspecific pyrolysis products. Recoveries ranged from 7% to 109%, and realistic detection limits accounting for matrix effects and recovery were up to 20 times nominal limits. The authors concluded that current Py-GC-MS methods were unsuitable for reporting PE and PVC in biological matrices.[5]
- This finding reduces confidence in mass concentrations produced by similar pyrolysis methods, but it does not by itself prove that every prior human detection is false. Studies using orthogonal spectroscopy, microscopy, or isotope methods still require independent replication.[2][5]
- Spectroscopic methods retain particle size and shape information but often miss smaller particles. Every approach is vulnerable to contamination from sampling, reagents, air, and equipment. Particle counts or mass concentrations generally cannot be compared directly across studies.[1]

## Bottled water, food contact, and the intervention boundary

- A 2020 drinking-water systematic review included 12 studies and found reports in both bottled and tap water. Between-study heterogeneity exceeded 95%, and minimum detectable particle size ranged from 1 to 100 micrometers. A single “particles per liter” figure should therefore not be treated as a universal exposure value or health threshold.[6]
- The current US FDA position is that available evidence does not demonstrate that MNP levels detected in foods pose a human-health risk, and detection methods are not yet standardized.[11]
- The 2026 PERTH randomized trial tested 7-day low-plastic-contact programs in 60 adults. It measured urinary phthalate and bisphenol metabolites—plastic-associated chemicals, not microplastic particles—and did not assess symptoms, disease, death, or lifespan. It therefore does not demonstrate “microplastic clearance” or lower clinical risk.[7]
- A 2026 nonrandomized before-and-after study analyzed 174 therapeutic plasma-exchange procedures in 114 patients and reported lower circulating MP counts when starting counts were high. It used the commercial PlasticTox assay, for which validation data were not public; it had no untreated control or health outcome, and the plastic tubing itself introduced particles. It does not support plasma exchange for “plastic detox” in healthy people or establish clinical benefit.[10]

## Practical guidance

1. If convenient, use glass, ceramic, or stainless steel for hot food and use plastic products only within manufacturer-specified temperatures and purposes. This is a low-cost precaution, not a proven disease-prevention intervention.
2. Follow local microbiological and chemical water-safety guidance first; do not replace bottled water with tap water that is not known to be safe. Whether a filter lowers MNP exposure and whether that matters clinically depend on the technology and source water; there is no universal clinical conclusion.
3. Avoid “microplastic testing packages,” detox supplements, and “plastic-cleansing” therapies; do not seek invasive plasma exchange for “plastic detox.” There are no validated clinical thresholds or evidence of benefit.[10]
4. People with cardiovascular disease should continue to prioritize established risks such as smoking, blood pressure, lipids, diabetes, and physical inactivity. Microplastic testing is not a substitute for standard care.

## Conflicts of interest and currency

- The NEJM study reported government research-program funding and directs readers to journal disclosure forms. A smaller prospective study in myocardial-infarction patients has since been published, but there is still no large general-population replication using independently validated measurement methods.[2][8]
- The 2026 human systematic review was funded by SingHealth Duke-NUS academic programs, and its authors declared no competing interests. Its search ended on December 26, 2024, so it cannot include every later study.[1]
- The PERTH trial was funded by the Minderoo Foundation, and several authors were foundation employees; the authors stated that the foundation and its benefactors did not influence conduct or findings. This does not invalidate the results, but the endpoint remained exposure to plastic-associated chemicals rather than microplastic particles or clinical outcomes.[7]
- Seven plasma-exchange-study authors were associated with Circulate Health, a company providing contract plasma-exchange services to private clinics; the other authors were officers of the four clinics where data were gathered. This is a direct commercial conflict relevant to the study's conclusion.[10]
