# Finnish-Style Dry Sauna

**Mortality impact**: Unknown. Frequent use was associated with lower mortality in an Eastern Finnish observational cohort, but sauna use has not been shown to cause the reduction
**Evidence level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (Long-term observational association; no independent-population replication or mortality-endpoint randomized trial)
**Actionability**: Medium (Requires suitable facilities and entails heat-exposure risks)

## Conclusion

A traditional Finnish sauna is a hot, low-humidity environment in which water may also be poured onto heated rocks to increase humidity briefly. The Eastern Finnish KIHD cohort found lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among frequent users. However, sauna habits were self-reported only at baseline, the frequent-use group was small, the main reference group used a sauna once weekly rather than not at all, and subsequent analyses remained within the same KIHD study system. The evidence supports an **observational signal**, not a causal prescription that four to seven sessions per week reduces mortality by 40% in the general population.

These results cannot be directly applied to infrared saunas, steam rooms, hot-water bathing, different temperature-humidity protocols, or people unaccustomed to sauna bathing. No fixed temperature, duration, or frequency has been shown to maximize lifespan.

## Key Evidence

- **All-cause mortality (Finnish men)**: 2,315 Eastern Finnish men aged 42–60 years were followed for a median of 20.7 years, with 929 all-cause deaths. The four-to-seven sessions/week group included 201 men and 62 deaths (30.8%); the once-weekly group included 601 men and 295 deaths (49.1%). The multivariable-adjusted all-cause mortality HR for four to seven sessions versus one session weekly was 0.60 (95% CI 0.46–0.80). This is the source of the former “-40%” label: it is a conditional relative-hazard association, not a randomized treatment effect, and the crude event proportions should not be converted into treatment absolute benefit or an NNT.
- **Cardiovascular mortality (same male cohort)**: Four to seven sessions/week versus one session/week was associated with HR 0.50 (95% CI 0.33–0.77) for fatal CVD and HR 0.37 (0.18–0.75) for sudden cardiac death. Sessions lasting >19 versus <11 minutes were associated with sudden cardiac death HR 0.48 (0.31–0.75), but session duration did not show a significant trend with all-cause mortality (P for trend = 0.93).
- **The later analysis including women was not an independent replication**: Women joined at the 11-year follow-up of the original KIHD male cohort, producing an analysis sample of 1,688 adults aged 53.4–73.8 years (867 women and 821 men). During a median 15.0-year follow-up, 181 cardiovascular deaths occurred; the overall adjusted HR for four to seven sessions/week versus one was 0.36 (0.17–0.77). However, all eight cardiovascular deaths in the high-frequency group occurred in men; among the 73 high-frequency women, there were no events and that HR could not be estimated. The study also did not report an all-cause mortality effect, so it did not validate “40% lower all-cause mortality” in women.
- **Sauna type and comparator**: KIHD described traditional Finnish sauna as approximately 10%–20% relative humidity, with temporary increases after water was poured on heated rocks; mean temperature in the 2015 male study was 78.9°C. All men used saunas, and the main comparator was once-weekly use, not non-use.
- **Randomized evidence and inconsistency**: A 2025 systematic review included 20 randomized passive-heating trials lasting 2–15 weeks and mixed Finnish sauna, far-infrared dry sauna (Waon), hot-water bathing, hot yoga, and local heating. Most pooled cardiometabolic and vascular outcomes did not improve significantly, and the overall systolic blood-pressure difference was -2.46 mmHg (95% CI -5.02 to 0.10); however, the whole-body heating subgroup was -4.11 mmHg (-7.36 to -0.86). Most trials had moderate-to-high risk of bias, with heterogeneity and signs of small-study/publication bias, and none established reduced mortality or longer life from sauna use. A contemporaneous 2015 commentary also specifically raised healthy-user selection, self-selection, and reverse causation as explanations for part of the association.

## Causality, Population, and Currency Boundaries

- Direct mortality evidence comes from the KIHD study in Finland's Kuopio region. The 2018 analysis included follow-up participants from the original cohort and was not a replication across countries, ethnic groups, or a wholly independent cohort. Evidence is particularly limited for women, younger adults, and people unaccustomed to sauna bathing.
- Baseline self-report creates recall and exposure-classification error, and habits may change during long follow-up. Residual confounding, illness-related avoidance, and healthy-user bias cannot be excluded.
- The frequent-use groups had few events, and sex-stratified analyses were underpowered. Repeated positive analyses of the same cohort cannot replace independent replication or permit a formal publication-bias assessment for mortality outcomes; effect sizes may be overestimated.
- The 2025 randomized evidence addresses only short-term surrogate outcomes. Through its search date of November 4, 2024, there was no adequately powered sauna trial with prespecified all-cause mortality or lifespan endpoints. This audit's updated search also found no independent mortality evidence sufficient to restore a numeric prefix.
- The 2015 and 2018 KIHD analyses were supported by Finnish nonprofit foundations and reported no relevant conflicts of interest. The 2025 review disclosed that one author owned Everyone Cohort Inc. and another had nutrition-supplement/food-industry research support or honoraria; no direct sauna-industry funding was identified, but these disclosures remain relevant context.

## Safety and Practice

1. Do not treat four to seven sessions/week as a proven longevity dose or force higher temperature or longer duration to match an observational category.
2. If you choose to use a sauna, use a shorter, milder exposure that you already tolerate. Leave immediately and seek help for chest pain, marked palpitations, shortness of breath, dizziness, confusion, or near-fainting.
3. Avoid alcohol before or during sauna use; maintain hydration and guard against hypotension, dehydration, syncope/falls, heat illness, and burns. A 2018 systematic review of dry sauna found that the available trials were mostly small and that high-quality data on the frequency and severity of adverse effects remain limited; few severe events in published trials does not establish safety for everyone.
4. People with unstable angina, recent myocardial infarction, or severe aortic stenosis should not initiate sauna use on their own. Those with cardiovascular disease, syncope/orthostatic hypotension, high-risk pregnancy, or illnesses or medicines affecting thermoregulation should first obtain individualized clinical advice.
5. Infrared saunas, steam rooms, and hot baths are not the same exposure studied in these mortality data; their results should not inherit the HRs above.
