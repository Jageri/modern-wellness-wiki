# Cold Showers

**Longevity endpoint**: No direct human evidence shows that cold showers reduce all-cause mortality or extend lifespan

**Health outcome**: One open-label randomized trial found 29% fewer self-reported sickness-absence days, but no significant difference in self-reported illness days

**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐ (only one direct shower trial with open-label delivery and self-reported outcomes; long-term and clinical-population evidence is lacking)

**Actionability**: Low (reasonable as a personal hygiene preference, not as immune, cardiovascular, or longevity therapy)

## Conclusion

Cold showers have not been shown to prevent infection, treat disease, or extend life. The only published, assessable large randomized trial directly testing clinical outcomes from routine cold showers found fewer reported days off work, but not fewer reported days of illness. The trial was unblinded and relied entirely on self-reported outcomes and adherence, so the result should not be interpreted as “boosted immunity.” Cold-water immersion, ice baths, cold air, and cold weather are different exposures; their mechanistic or risk data cannot be converted directly into benefits or harms from showering.

## Direct Shower Evidence

- The Dutch trial enrolled 3,018 employed adults aged 18–65 who had no severe comorbidity and no routine cold-shower experience. Intervention groups ended their usual warm shower with 30, 60, or 90 seconds at the coldest available water temperature for 30 consecutive days. Average local cold-water temperature during the study was about 10–12°C, but individual shower temperatures were not measured each time. Participants then chose whether to continue for another 60 days.
- At 90 days, the three cold-shower groups combined had an incidence rate ratio of 0.71 for self-reported sickness-absence days (29% fewer, P=0.003), but there was no significant group effect on self-reported illness days and no dose-response difference among 30, 60, and 90 seconds. This does not establish fewer infections, milder disease, or improved immune function.
- Seventy-nine percent of the intervention groups completed the first 30-day protocol; loss to follow-up was 19.6% at 90 days. Recruitment was online, the study was unblinded, prior illness, outcomes, and adherence were self-reported, and actual compliance could not be verified. A small difference in the mental component of quality of life at 30 days was considered clinically irrelevant by the authors and was no longer significant at 90 days.
- A 2025 systematic review included 11 randomized trials with 3,177 participants, but only the trial above used showers; the other 10 used cold-water immersion. Temperatures ranged from 7°C to 15°C and exposure from 30 seconds to 2 hours. Its findings of increased inflammation shortly after exposure, lower stress scores only at the 12-hour time point, and no significant short-term immune effect therefore should not be presented as effects of routine cold showers. The review did not assess mortality, cardiovascular events, or long-term disease outcomes and noted few trials, limited population diversity, and highly varied protocols.
- A Swiss randomized trial intended to replicate the sickness-absence and illness-day findings enrolled 350 people and was completed in 2023. As of 2026-09-09, ClinicalTrials.gov still showed “No Results Posted.” It cannot count as replication until results are public and peer reviewed.

## Showering Is Not Immersion

- In an experiment involving only 18 healthy adults, participants took six cold showers over four days before a second head-out, seated immersion for 3 minutes at 10°C. The 10°C shower groups had a 21% smaller respiratory-frequency response during the first 30 seconds of the later immersion, whereas the 15°C shower group did not; immersion-induced tachycardia was not reduced in most groups. This shows limited habituation of one component of cold shock under specific conditions, not a health benefit or proof that ice baths are safe.
- A 2024 systematic review of the cold-shock response studied cold-water immersion. Immersion can cause gasping or hyperventilation, heart-rate changes, arrhythmias, and drowning risk. Some habituation appeared after about four repeated immersions on average, but results varied and outcome-specific samples were small. A shower does not produce the same whole-body submersion or drowning setting, so these data cannot quantify the risk of a routine cold shower. Conversely, shower-trial safety data do not establish that immersion is safe.
- Studies of brown fat, catecholamines, exercise recovery, cold-weather mortality, or lifespan under sustained cold in animals are not direct clinical evidence about routine cold showers. They cannot support claims that cold showers “burn fat,” increase alertness, enhance immunity, or extend life.

## Safety Boundaries

- The large shower trial found no intervention-related serious adverse events, but it excluded people who self-reported severe cardiac, pulmonary, or other systemic disease, so it does not establish safety for those groups. The most frequent related symptom was persistent cold sensation in the body, hands, or feet (up to about 13%); a small number reported muscle pain or cramps, insomnia, dizziness, or palpitations, and three participants with Raynaud's phenomenon reported persistent cold sensation.
- Current evidence supports neither “all cardiovascular disease is an absolute contraindication” nor “gradual exposure is always safe.” People with cardiac or pulmonary disease, a history of syncope, marked Raynaud's phenomenon, or another condition worsened by cold should ask a clinician familiar with their condition before trying very cold showers or whole-body immersion.
- Stop exposure immediately for chest pain, fainting, marked breathlessness, persistent palpitations, or altered consciousness, and seek urgent medical help according to symptom severity.

## What to Do

1. There is no need to start cold showers for “immune boosting” or “longevity,” and they should not replace vaccination, exercise, sleep, or standard care.
2. If a healthy adult tries them as a personal preference, the direct trial used 30 seconds of cold water at the end of a warm shower; 60 or 90 seconds was not better. This was a study protocol, not a validated optimal dose or medical prescription.
3. Do not extrapolate shower evidence to ice baths, cold-water swimming, or open water. Those settings add stronger cold shock and drowning hazards and require a separate safety assessment.
4. Stop rather than pursuing colder or longer exposure if cold sensation persists, dizziness or palpitations occur, sleep is disturbed, or an existing symptom worsens.
