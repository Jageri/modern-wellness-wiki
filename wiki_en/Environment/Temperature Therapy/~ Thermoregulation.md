# Thermoregulation (Cold/Heat Exposure)

**Longevity endpoint**: No evidence shows that deliberately lowering normal body temperature, "strengthening" the temperature rhythm, or using a fixed dose of sauna/cold exposure extends human lifespan

**Health outcomes**: Avoiding hypothermia, heat stroke, and prolonged non-optimal ambient temperatures has clear safety value; deliberate heat/cold adaptation evidence mainly concerns short-term physiology, not mortality or lifespan

**Evidence level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (Systematic reviews and large models support measurement ranges, age-related changes, and ambient-temperature harms; long-term clinical benefits from deliberate thermal exposure remain unestablished)

**Actionability**: Medium (focus on consistent measurement, changes from personal baseline, and avoiding extreme exposure)

## Conclusion

Thermoregulation is the process by which the body uses vasoconstriction/vasodilation, sweating, shivering, metabolic heat production, and behavior to maintain core temperature. "37°C" is not a universal cutoff for every person, time, or measurement site. Oral, axillary, tympanic, and rectal readings are not directly interchangeable.

The slightly lower resting temperature observed in older adults should not be interpreted as greater longevity. Physiological changes after heat or cold adaptation are not proven lifespan effects. The most certain actions are to avoid temperature extremes and interpret readings with symptoms and personal baseline rather than pursuing an "optimal body temperature."

## Definitions, measurement, and rhythm

- A 2019 systematic review included 36 studies, 7,636 healthy adults, and 9,227 measurement sites. Ranges calculated as mean ± 2 standard deviations were 36.32–37.76°C rectal, 35.76–37.52°C tympanic, 35.73–37.41°C oral, and 35.01–36.93°C axillary. These are site-specific reference distributions, not a single self-diagnostic threshold.
- In the same review, adults aged ≥60 years averaged 0.23°C lower than those aged <60. A separate meta-analysis of 16 studies estimated that older adults' overall temperature was 0.17°C lower (95% CI -0.30 to -0.03); the core-temperature difference of -0.13°C (95% CI -0.27 to 0.01) was not statistically significant. Differences were small and affected by site and heterogeneity.
- Core temperature has a near-24-hour rhythm, generally lower in the rest/sleep phase and higher in the active phase. Aging can accompany reduced amplitude or a phase shift. Temperature rhythm is both an output of the circadian clock and affected by sleep, activity, light, and illness. No clinical trial shows that independently "optimizing the temperature curve" prevents disease or extends life.

## Fever is not hyperthermia

- **Fever** involves an inflammatory upward shift in the hypothalamic set point; **hyperthermia** results from inadequate heat dissipation or excessive heat production without that mechanism. Heat stroke is an organ-threatening emergency and cannot be managed as ordinary fever.
- A 2025 systematic review synthesized 132 studies of adults hospitalized with bacterial infection. Older adults were less likely to be febrile (RR 0.89, 95% CI 0.83–0.95) and averaged only 0.14°C lower (95% CI -0.26 to -0.02); 129/132 studies were at high risk of bias. Absence of fever therefore does not rule out infection in an older adult.
- A 2022 review included 42 randomized fever-therapy trials and 5,140 adults. Antipyretics, physical cooling, or both showed no clear effect on all-cause mortality (RR 1.04, 95% CI 0.90–1.19) or serious adverse events (RR 1.02, 0.89–1.17). This does not mean fever needs no assessment and does not apply to heat stroke; all included trials were judged at high risk of bias.

## Ambient temperature and mortality

- A 2021 three-stage model used 2000–2019 time-series data from 750 locations in 43 countries. It estimated that 5.08 million deaths/year (95% empirical CI 4.09–5.97 million), or 9.43% of deaths (7.58%–11.07%), were associated with non-optimal ambient temperature: 8.52% cold-related and 0.91% heat-related.
- This was a location-level association with grid extrapolation, not an individual randomized trial; "cold-related" is also not clinical hypothermia. Curves, lags, and adaptation through heating or air conditioning vary across place and time. These numbers cannot be converted into personal lifespan benefit from sauna, cold-water immersion, or a thermostat setting.

## Deliberate exposure: adaptation without a longevity conclusion

### Heat adaptation and passive heating

- A 2019 heat-acclimation meta-analysis included only 11 randomized trials and 215 participants; mean age was 26.09 years, 91% were male, and participants were athletes. Time-trial performance improved modestly (SMD 0.50, 95% CI 0.03–0.97), but core/skin temperature, exercise heart rate, plasma volume, and VO₂max did not differ significantly. This cannot be extrapolated to older adults, patients, or lifespan.
- A 2025 meta-analysis included 20 randomized trials of at least one week of passive heating (hot-water bathing, sauna, hot yoga, or local heating), lasting 2–15 weeks. Most pooled cardiometabolic/vascular outcomes did not improve significantly. Overall systolic blood pressure was -2.46 mmHg (95% CI -5.02 to 0.10), not significant. A whole-body-heating subgroup was -4.11 mmHg (-7.36 to -0.86), but heterogeneity and subgroup analysis constrain causal interpretation. Mortality and lifespan benefit were not established.

### Cold adaptation and cold-water immersion

- Cold can trigger cutaneous vasoconstriction, shivering, and non-shivering thermogenesis. Habituation, metabolic adaptation, and insulative adaptation are distinct; "more brown fat" does not describe the full response.
- A 2024 cold-shock review found that initial cold-water immersion can trigger hyperventilation, heart-rate changes, arrhythmia, and drowning risk. Some habituation appeared after about four immersions, but pooled samples per outcome were only 46–145 and between-study variation was substantial. Habituation does not mean risk has disappeared.
- Another 2024 meta-analysis synthesized 24 cold-water-immersion/cryostimulation studies in healthy adults. It found short-lived changes in heart-rate variability, a small heart-rate decrease, and a small mean-blood-pressure increase. These are acute surrogates, not cardiovascular events, mortality, or lifespan benefits.

## Boundary of the "lower temperature extends life" claim

A 2025 *Nature Aging* Perspective summarized cross-species associations between lower core temperature and longevity and experimental lifespan extension from lowered temperature in rodents. It was not a randomized human longevity trial and does not justify deliberately lowering core temperature in healthy people. One author disclosed scientific-advisory roles with Biophysical Therapeutics and Invictus Biopharm; this should be considered alongside the absence of direct human evidence.

## Safety and actions

1. For day-to-day tracking, use the same thermometer, site, and similar time. Interpret readings with symptoms and personal baseline.
2. There is no need to begin fixed sauna, ice-bath, or cold-water-immersion routines for "hormesis" or longevity. If trying one for preference, do not enter cold water alone, escalate to colder/hotter/longer exposure, or expose yourself while drinking alcohol, dehydrated, or ill.
3. Older adults, people unable to leave an extreme environment unaided, and those with cardiovascular disease, syncope, impaired sweating/thermoregulation, or medications affecting temperature/volume status should not treat healthy-adult experiments as proof of personal safety.
4. Fainting, delirium, loss of consciousness, or cessation of sweating in heat should be treated as possible heat stroke: call emergency services, move to shade/air conditioning, and begin aggressive cooling. Do not wait for a particular thermometer number.
5. Persistent shivering, slurred speech, clumsiness, drowsiness, or confusion after cold exposure, or core temperature <35°C, signals a hypothermia emergency: leave the cold/wet environment, remove wet clothes, warm the trunk first, and seek emergency help.
6. Fever with breathing difficulty, persistent chest pain, new confusion, inability to wake, seizure, or marked dehydration warrants urgent assessment. In older or immunocompromised people, absence of high fever does not exclude serious infection.
