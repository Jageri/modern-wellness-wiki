# Air Pollution

**Long-term mortality association**: In an updated global meta-analysis, each 10 μg/m³ increase in long-term average PM2.5 was associated with all-cause mortality RR=1.095 (95% CI 1.064–1.127). This is not a 9.5% increase in an individual's absolute probability of death and should not be linearly extrapolated across arbitrary concentration differences.
**Evidence level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (strong association with long-term mortality; evidence is mainly observational and between-study heterogeneity is very high)
**Actionability**: Moderate (individuals can reduce exposure, but source control and public policy are primary)

## Conclusion

Long-term exposure to outdoor PM2.5 is consistently associated with higher all-cause and cause-specific mortality. Concentration-response studies have not identified a threshold that can be considered risk-free, but this does not mean the slope is identical at every concentration or that any personal protective measure has been shown to extend life. Follow local air-quality alerts, adjust the timing and route of activities, reduce exposure during high-pollution periods, and control indoor particle sources.

## Evidence

- **Updated global systematic review** (Orellano et al., 2024, PMID 39399882): 106 studies were included; 52 studies contributed 53 PM2.5–all-cause mortality estimates. Per 10 μg/m³, pooled RR=1.095 (95% CI 1.064–1.127). The median of study mean/median PM2.5 levels was 16.58 μg/m³, with a range of 4.49–72.4 μg/m³. Heterogeneity was very high (I²=99.6%), and the 80% prediction interval crossed 1 (0.966–1.241), so the pooled estimate is not a fixed effect for every setting or person. Funnel-plot asymmetry was not observed and Egger's test gave P=0.45, but these findings cannot exclude all publication bias.
- **Asia-Pacific systematic review** (Oh et al., 2025, PMID 40662655): among 71 articles, 19 studies entered the PM2.5–all-cause mortality meta-analysis; per 10 μg/m³, RR=1.11 (95% CI 1.05–1.17). Mean PM2.5 concentrations in included studies ranged from 11.6 μg/m³ in Japan to 80.6 μg/m³ in China. Most outcomes had I²>60% except PM2.5–lung cancer. In the elderly subgroup, RR=1.09 (95% CI 0.99–1.21), which did not meet the usual threshold for statistical significance.
- **Huai River policy quasi-experiment** (Chen et al., 2013, PMID 23836630): in the historical high-pollution setting studied, TSP was 184 μg/m³ higher north of the river (95% CI 61–307) and life expectancy at birth was 5.5 years lower (95% CI 0.8–10.2). The model associated each additional 100 μg/m³ TSP with about 3.0 years lower life expectancy (95% CI 0.4–5.6). This finding concerns TSP, a specific policy boundary, and a historical population; it cannot be converted into a universal life-years estimate per 10 μg/m³ PM2.5.
- **Concentration-response boundary**: studies in the 2024 update reported linear, supralinear, and sublinear curves. Several European, Canadian, and Chinese studies found no threshold, whereas a U.S. Medicare study estimated lower relative risks below about 10 μg/m³. “No clear safe threshold has been identified” is more accurate than claiming that every very low concentration has been proven equally harmful.

## Do

1. Check official local air-quality alerts. When pollution rises, move commuting and exercise to cleaner times, routes, or indoor settings, especially for people with cardiopulmonary disease, older adults, and children.
2. Limit entry of obvious outdoor pollution while reducing indoor sources such as smoking, cooking smoke, and solid fuels; also account for ventilation, carbon dioxide, and combustion safety.
3. If indoor particle reduction is needed, choose a cleaner sized for the room, with a stated clean-air delivery rate and no intentional ozone generation, and replace filters as directed. Portable cleaners can lower indoor PM2.5, but have not been shown to reduce long-term mortality.
4. If going outside is unavoidable during a severe particle-pollution episode, consider a certified, well-fitting N95/KN95/FFP2-class respirator. Correct fit, continuous use during exposure, and timely replacement matter.

## Don't

1. Do not treat AQI values from different countries or platforms as interchangeable. AQI algorithms and categories differ; follow local health or environmental guidance.
2. Do not stop exercising indefinitely because of air pollution. WHO's evidence review concludes that pollution attenuates but generally does not eliminate the benefits of physical activity; first adjust timing, location, and intensity.
3. Do not interpret “95% filtration efficiency” as a guaranteed 95% reduction in real-world inhaled dose or health risk. Fit, pollutant type, and use affect performance, and particle respirators do not remove all gaseous pollutants.
4. Do not treat a small short-term trial as mortality evidence. A randomized crossover trial in 52 healthy Beijing college students (Jiang et al., 2021, PMID 34265631) measured lung function and biomarkers for several hours after a 2-hour walk. It did not test cardiovascular events, mortality, or lifespan and cannot be directly generalized to children, older adults, or people with cardiopulmonary disease.

## Controversies & Limitations

- **Causal boundary**: long-term mortality evidence is mainly observational. Consistency, concentration-response analyses, and quasi-experiments strengthen causal interpretation, but residual confounding, selection bias, and exposure measurement error remain possible.
- **Population and exposure differences**: particle composition, exposure methods, baseline disease, socioeconomic conditions, and healthcare access vary by setting. Adults were the focus of 96/106 studies in the global update, and low- and middle-income settings remain relatively underrepresented.
- **Heterogeneity**: the global all-cause mortality result had I²=99.6% and a prediction interval crossing 1; the Asia-Pacific estimate was also heterogeneous. A single RR should not be used to predict an individual's risk.
- **Personal-protection evidence**: air-cleaner and respirator studies are generally small, short, and focused on surrogate outcomes. They have not established reductions in long-term clinical events or mortality. People with respiratory or cardiovascular disease should consider individual tolerance and professional advice before using a tight-fitting respirator.
- **Conflicts of interest**: the 2024 global update was supported by the WHO Regional Office for Europe and multiple public bodies; one author was WHO-affiliated, and the authors declared no conflicts. The 2025 Asia-Pacific review received Korean environmental-health and government support, with no identified air-cleaner or respirator manufacturer funding, although public disclosures cannot rule out every unreported conflict.
