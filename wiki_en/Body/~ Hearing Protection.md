# Hearing Protection

**Mortality impact**: Unknown; no randomized trial has shown that noise protection, hearing screening, or hearing aids reduce all-cause mortality or extend life
**Evidence level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (direct evidence supports noise reduction and communication benefits; mortality and long-term cognitive benefits remain uncertain)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion

The established value of hearing protection is reducing hazardous noise exposure and, after hearing loss develops, improving speech understanding, communication, and hearing-related handicap. Current evidence cannot support a `+20~40%` mortality or lifespan label: the roughly 21% figure is an **observational association between hearing loss and all-cause mortality**, not an intervention effect of protection, screening, or hearing aids.

Hearing loss is also associated with dementia risk, but association does not mean treatment reverses that risk. Hearing-aid decisions should primarily reflect communication difficulty, personal goals, and professional assessment—not a promise to prevent dementia or prolong life.

## Evidence boundaries

### Mortality: only an observational association with hearing loss

- A 2025 meta-analysis included 36 cohorts and about 6.4 million people. Hearing loss was associated with all-cause mortality (HR 1.21, 95% CI 1.13–1.31). Heterogeneity was very high (I²=95.7%), and European and Oceanian subgroups were not significant; every included study was observational.
- This HR compares hearing loss with normal hearing. It cannot be converted into “hearing protection lowers mortality by 21%.” No RCT was identified that tested noise protection, screening, or hearing aids using all-cause mortality or lifespan as an outcome.

### Noise prevention: exposure can be reduced, but longevity is unproven

- Noise-induced hearing loss can follow one extremely loud event or repeated exposure and may be permanent. Risk depends on intensity, distance, and duration. NIOSH's 85 dBA/8-hour value is an occupational recommended exposure limit, not a universal personal “safe line.” [NIOSH](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/noise/about/noise.html)
- In a Cochrane review, hearing-protection devices reduced exposure by about 20 dB(A) in one RCT and three controlled studies (57 participants), but certainty was low. Fitting instruction improved attenuation by 8.59 dB (95% CI 6.92–10.25; two RCTs, 140 participants) versus no instruction. Evidence that programs avert long-term hearing loss remained uncertain.
- Engineering controls, shorter duration, and greater distance come first. When those are insufficient, use properly selected and fitted earplugs or earmuffs. Active-noise-cancelling headphones should be treated as hearing protection only when labeled with a noise reduction rating (NRR). [NIOSH](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/noise/prevent/ppe.html)

### Hearing aids: communication benefits are more secure than cognitive benefits

- A 2025 umbrella review of 11 systematic reviews found relatively consistent benefits for speech perception, communication function, hearing handicap, and self-reported benefit. Evidence was insufficient for cognition, depression, loneliness, and social isolation; quality-of-life findings conflicted.
- A 2026 Cochrane update included 16 RCTs and 2,261 participants. Hearing aids likely improve hearing-specific quality of life and listening ability in adults with mild-to-moderate loss, may have little or no effect on loneliness, and have very uncertain effects on working memory. A separate 2026 review of RCTs was directionally consistent. Neither review establishes a mortality benefit or durable cognitive protection. [PMID 42644645](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42644645/); [PMID 42053356](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42053356/)
- The ACHIEVE RCT enrolled 977 adults aged 70–84. Its 3-year primary analysis was negative: between-group cognitive change was 0.002 SD (95% CI -0.077 to 0.081; p=0.96).
- The reported “48% slowing” came only from a prespecified stratified sensitivity analysis: the 238-person higher-risk ARIC-source subgroup differed, while 739 newly recruited volunteers did not. It was not an overall treatment effect or a 48% reduction in dementia or mortality.
- A 2026 target-trial emulation (observational) of about 2,777 adults aged 70 or older found a 7-year overall cognition difference of 0.03 SD (95% CI -0.14 to 0.21). Estimated dementia risk was lower, but the authors could not exclude residual confounding; this does not replace a long-term RCT.

### Hearing loss and dementia: a risk marker is not necessarily a reversible cause

- In a 2011 cohort of 639 people, dementia HR was 1.27 (95% CI 1.06–1.50) per 10 dB greater loss. HRs for moderate and severe loss were 3.00 and 4.94, but only six participants had severe loss and the interval was wide. The authors stated that whether loss is an early marker or modifiable cause remained unresolved.
- The 2024 Lancet Commission lists hearing loss among potentially modifiable dementia risk factors. A population-attributable fraction is a population model based on prevalence and observational association—not an individual's risk, a hearing-aid treatment effect, or a directly observed fraction of preventable cases.
- The original entry's 2023 UK Biobank hearing-aid/dementia paper ([retracted, PMID 37062296](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37062296/)) was retracted. Its result is no longer used as evidence.

### Screening: symptom-triggered assessment is not universal screening

- People who notice difficulty hearing, have tinnitus, receive family reports of communication problems, or have substantial noise exposure have a clear clinical reason for assessment.
- For asymptomatic adults aged 50 or older, the USPSTF finds evidence insufficient to determine the benefits and harms of universal screening (I statement). “Regular screening after age 50” is not a universal prescription proven to improve long-term outcomes.

## What to do

1. **Reduce noise first**: lower the source, shorten exposure, increase distance, and take quiet breaks; follow occupational hearing-conservation programs at work.
2. **Use protection correctly**: wear appropriate, well-fitted earplugs or earmuffs at concerts, construction sites, around gunfire, or in other high-noise settings.
3. **Do not treat a fixed “60/60 rule” as a guarantee**: devices, fit, and background noise vary. If you must raise volume to overpower the environment, reduce it or leave the setting.
4. **Seek assessment for communication difficulty**: evaluation can distinguish wax, conductive disease, age-related loss, and noise-related loss, and address hearing aids, assistive devices, and communication training.
5. **Seek prompt care for sudden one-sided or rapidly worsening hearing loss**; vertigo, neurologic symptoms, ear pain or discharge, and marked asymmetry also warrant assessment.

## Limitations and applicability

- The mortality meta-analysis combined self-report and audiometry, age groups, and regions; residual confounding and reverse causation remain possible.
- ACHIEVE mainly applies to US adults aged 70–84 with mild-to-moderate loss and no substantial cognitive impairment; 88% were White. It does not establish effects in younger adults, people with dementia, severe or sudden loss, or general-population mortality.
- Protection studies largely concern occupational or single-event recreational noise. Real-world attenuation depends heavily on selection, fit, and consistent use.
- ACHIEVE was NIH-funded, but some authors reported hearing-industry consulting, research contracts, honoraria, or donated devices. This belongs alongside its negative primary result and limited applicability.
