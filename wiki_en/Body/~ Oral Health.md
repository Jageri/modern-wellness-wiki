# Oral Health

**Mortality Impact**: `~` Observational studies associate tooth loss and lower toothbrushing frequency with higher all-cause mortality; no randomized trial has shown that oral care extends lifespan
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (Mortality associations are supported by cohort data and a systematic review; causality and a longevity benefit are unproven)
**Actionability**: High (to prevent caries, gingivitis, and periodontitis, not as a proven life-extension intervention)

## Conclusion
Brush twice daily for about two minutes with fluoride toothpaste, and clean between the teeth daily using a device suited to the spaces and the user's abilities. Dental recall and professional-cleaning intervals should be individualized according to caries risk, periodontal status, and previous treatment rather than fixed at every six months for everyone.

## Evidence

### Association with all-cause mortality
- Tooth loss is the cumulative result of caries, periodontitis, smoking, diabetes, nutrition, socioeconomic conditions, and access to care. A systematic review included 75 cohort articles, with 44 in meta-analysis; fewer teeth were associated with all-cause mortality, but multivariable-adjusted HRs across different exposure definitions ranged from **1.02–1.30**. This is not a causal estimate that retaining teeth reduces mortality by 2–30%. [[PMID 34026863]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34026863/)
- The Guangzhou Biobank Cohort followed 28,006 middle-aged and older adults for a mean 14.3 years. Compared with brushing ≥2 times/day, brushing once/day was associated with all-cause mortality HR **1.16 (95% CI 1.10–1.22)**, and brushing <once/day with HR **1.27 (95% CI 1.00–1.61)**. Oral health was self-reported in interviews, and healthy-behavior and socioeconomic confounding may remain; the study cannot show that brushing itself lowers mortality. [[PMID 38968002]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38968002/)

### Periodontal disease and cardiovascular disease
- A meta-analysis of 30 longitudinal cohorts found periodontal disease associated with higher incident cardiovascular disease risk (RR **1.20, 95% CI 1.14–1.26**). This is an observational association, not a treatment effect. [[PMID 33124761]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33124761/)
- A 2026 American Heart Association scientific statement recognizes the association and plausible mechanisms, but causality is unconfirmed and there is no direct evidence that periodontal treatment prevents cardiovascular disease. [[AHA official summary]](https://newsroom.heart.org/news/gum-disease-may-be-linked-to-plaque-buildup-in-arteries-higher-risk-of-major-cvd-events) [[PMID 41399933]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41399933/)
- A Cochrane review used death and cardiovascular events as clinical endpoints but found only two high-risk-of-bias RCTs. Evidence was very uncertain, so it could not determine whether periodontal treatment reduces all-cause death or cardiovascular events. Improvements in CRP, blood pressure, or endothelial function are surrogate outcomes and do not substitute for myocardial infarction, stroke, or death. [[PMID 31887786]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31887786/)

### Daily care and recall intervals
- The American Dental Association recommends that most people brush twice daily for two minutes with fluoride toothpaste and clean between the teeth daily. [[ADA Home Oral Care]](https://www.ada.org/resources/ada-library/oral-health-topics/home-oral-care/)
- Floss is one interdental-cleaning option; one device need not be prescribed to everyone. A 2019 Cochrane review found low- to very-low-certainty evidence for gingivitis outcomes, mostly from short studies, with insufficient evidence for caries or periodontitis clinical endpoints. [[PMID 30968949]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30968949/)
- In adults who regularly attended primary-care dental practices, a Cochrane review found little to no difference over four years between risk-based and fixed six-month recall for caries, gingival bleeding, or oral-health-related quality of life. Among adults eligible for 24-month recall, 24-month and six-month recall also produced little to no difference. These findings do not apply to patients receiving supportive periodontal care after active periodontitis treatment. [[PMID 33053198]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33053198/)

## Do
1. Brush morning and evening for about two minutes with fluoride toothpaste, using a soft-bristle brush and gentle cleaning along the gumline.
2. Clean between the teeth daily. Floss may suit tight contacts; ask a dental professional about appropriately sized interdental brushes or other devices for larger spaces or periodontal problems.
3. Schedule examinations and professional cleaning according to individual risk; after periodontal treatment, follow the prescribed supportive-care plan.
4. Stop smoking, and if you have diabetes, combine glycemic management with periodontal assessment.

## Don't
1. Do not treat brushing, flossing, or professional cleaning as proven life-extension or cardiovascular preventive therapy.
2. Do not scrub hard with a hard-bristle brush or scrape calculus yourself.
3. Do not ignore persistent bleeding gums, bad breath, loose teeth, or changes in chewing.

## Medical Red Flags
- Recurrent bleeding, swollen or suppurating gums; loose teeth or receding gums.
- Toothache, facial swelling, fever, difficulty swallowing, or difficulty breathing; the last two require urgent care.
- An oral ulcer, lump, or red/white patch that has not healed after two weeks.
