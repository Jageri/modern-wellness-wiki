# Tooth Brushing

**Mortality Impact**: No reliable causal estimate shows that a toothbrushing intervention reduces all-cause mortality. In the observational Guangzhou Biobank cohort, compared with brushing ≥2 times/day, brushing once/day was associated with all-cause mortality HR 1.16 (95% CI 1.10–1.22), and brushing <1 time/day with HR 1.27 (95% CI 1.00–1.61).
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (Mortality and cardiovascular evidence is mainly observational; evidence for local oral-health benefits is stronger)
**Actionability**: Very High

## Conclusion

Correct twice-daily brushing with fluoride toothpaste prevents caries and helps control plaque and gingivitis. Less frequent brushing is associated with higher all-cause mortality and some cardiovascular outcomes, but this does not establish that brushing more often prolongs life or prevents cardiovascular events. Brushing frequency may also reflect smoking, income, education, access to care, and other health behaviours.

## Evidence

### Evidence related to mortality and cardiovascular outcomes

- **Direct all-cause mortality evidence**: The Guangzhou Biobank study included 28,006 middle-aged and older adults followed for a mean of 14.3 years; brushing frequency was self-reported. Compared with ≥2 times/day, adjusted HR was 1.16 (95% CI 1.10–1.22) for once/day and 1.27 (95% CI 1.00–1.61) for <1 time/day. This is an observational association in one population, not an intervention showing “25% lower mortality from brushing” (Zhou et al., 2024).
- **Cardiovascular outcomes**: The Scottish Health Survey included 11,869 adults followed for a mean of 8.1 years. Participants reporting “never/rarely” brushing had an adjusted HR of 1.70 (95% CI 1.30–2.30) for fatal or non-fatal cardiovascular events compared with those brushing twice/day. Brushing was self-reported, and the authors stated that causality remained undetermined (de Oliveira et al., 2010).
- **Periodontitis is not the same exposure as brushing**: A meta-analysis of 39 prospective cohort studies found periodontitis associated with all-cause mortality RR 1.31 (95% CI 1.07–1.61). The exposure was diagnosed/defined periodontal disease, not toothbrushing alone, so the result cannot be inverted into a “31% risk reduction from brushing” (Guo et al., 2023).
- **Overall evidence landscape**: A 2025 scoping review included 21 studies. Lower brushing frequency was generally associated with higher risks of cardiovascular events, stroke, hypertension, metabolic syndrome, and chronic kidney disease, but the evidence was predominantly observational; longitudinal and interventional studies are still needed to establish direction and causal pathways (Park et al., 2025).

### ⚠️ Counter-evidence and limitations

- **No longevity RCT**: No randomized trial has shown that increasing brushing frequency or treating periodontitis reduces all-cause mortality. The updated AHA scientific statement treats the periodontitis–atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease literature as an association with important uncertainties still requiring clarification (Tran et al., 2026).
- **Confounding and selection bias**: Frequent brushers often differ in smoking, diet, income, education, and healthcare use. In an NHANES III analysis of older adults, periodontitis was associated with mortality in conventional Cox models but became non-significant after inverse-probability-weighted regression adjustment for selection bias (Yu et al., 2023).
- **Exposures cannot be combined indiscriminately**: Brushing frequency, flossing/interdental cleaning, dental attendance, periodontitis, and missing teeth are distinct exposures. Results for a composite “good oral hygiene” pattern cannot be attributed to brushing alone.
- **These are not absolute risks**: The reported HRs and RRs are relative effect measures. The cited studies do not provide a generalisable estimate of the absolute mortality reduction after changing brushing behaviour.
- **Limited generalisability**: The direct all-cause mortality estimates come from middle-aged and older adults in Guangzhou, while the Scottish study measured cardiovascular events. They should not be directly extrapolated to every age group, region, or healthcare setting.

## Do

1. Brush twice daily for about 2 minutes with fluoride toothpaste, gently cleaning every tooth surface and the gumline.
2. Clean between teeth daily. Choose floss, interdental brushes, or an oral irrigator according to spacing, dexterity, and dental advice; evidence does not support one universally best device.
3. See a dentist regularly, but tailor recall and professional-cleaning intervals to caries, periodontal, and individual risk. Evidence does not support a fixed one-to-two cleanings per year for everyone.
4. Recurrent gum bleeding may indicate gingivitis/periodontal disease, but can also be related to overly forceful brushing, medicines, or other conditions. Do not stop brushing completely; use a soft brush, clean gently, and arrange a dental assessment.
5. Manual and powered brushes can both clean effectively. A Cochrane review found that powered brushes reduced plaque and gingivitis somewhat more on average, but the clinical importance was uncertain; they may be particularly useful for people with limited dexterity or difficulty brushing thoroughly.
6. The local oral-health benefits of brushing are established, but brushing is not a substitute for smoking cessation, exercise, diet, or indicated medical care, and should not be marketed as a “longevity shortcut.”
