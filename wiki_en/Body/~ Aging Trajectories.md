# Aging Trajectories

**Mortality Impact**: Unknown. Omics “waves” do not provide a direct all-cause mortality effect; epigenetic age and functional decline are associated with mortality, but changing a “biological age” score has not been shown to extend life
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐ (exploratory observational evidence; exact peak ages have not replicated consistently, and the central 44/60-year analysis is under journal scrutiny)
**Actionability**: Medium (useful for correcting the fixed “aging cliff” misconception; no anti-aging treatment window has been established)

## Conclusion

Molecular markers and physical function do not all change at a constant rate, but there is no universal timetable of aging cliffs. Peaks at 44, 60, or other ages come from different datasets, models, and statistical windows. They do not show that whole-body function, disease risk, and mortality suddenly change together on a birthday.

“Biological age” is not a single directly measured truth. It is a model score determined by its inputs and training target. Some clocks predict mortality or frailty risk at the population level, but they are not validated personal longevity endpoints, and a one-time commercial result should not determine the use of hormones, supplements, or other “anti-aging” treatments.

## Evidence

### Proposed waves and inflection points

- The 44/60 claim mainly comes from Shen et al. Repeated multi-omics samples were collected from 108 California adults aged 25–75, with a median follow-up of only 1.7 years (maximum 6.8), and more molecular or microbial changes were reported near ages 44 and 60 [1]. Repeated sampling does not provide a within-person life-course trajectory from youth to old age; the peaks still rely heavily on comparisons among participants of different ages and sliding-window analysis.
- On July 15, 2026, *Nature Aging* added an **Editor’s Note** to the article—not a retraction or a formal “Expression of Concern.” After further review by the authors and independent researchers, the reliability of some analyses used to detect peaks around particular ages was in question, and the scope of correction remained under assessment [1]. Ages 44 and 60 therefore cannot be treated as settled findings.
- Ding et al. analyzed 516 samples from 13 tissues in 76 Chinese donors aged 14–68 and reported greater proteomic remodeling around age 50, especially 45–55, with a prominent vascular signal [2]. This was a cross-sectional tissue atlas across different donors, not continuous observation of the same people. One author was employed by a proteomics company.
- Other studies report different ages. Cross-sectional plasma proteomics in 4,263 people found waves around ages 34, 60, and 78 [3]. Liu et al. estimated a brain-age gap from imaging and associated it with plasma proteins in 4,696 people, reporting peaks at 57, 70, and 78 [4]. Zhang et al. built a mortality-associated “metabolic age” model and reported peaks at 44, 51, and 63 in a 7,092-person sliding-window analysis [5]. The latter two concern model-derived brain or metabolic age, not birthdays marking whole-body physiological collapse.
- Together, these studies support possible nonlinearity in some molecular markers. They do not consistently replicate exact ages across tissues, platforms, populations, age ranges, or algorithms, and they do not establish a causal intervention window.

### Epigenetic clocks and “biological age”

- A 2024 multidisciplinary framework found no consensus for validating aging biomarkers before clinical use. Technical reliability, generalizability, longitudinal change, clinical outcome prediction, and qualification as an intervention surrogate must be tested separately [6]. Accurate chronological-age prediction is not proof that a clock measures a reversible rate of aging.
- A 2019 systematic review included 23 studies and 41,607 participants. Each 5 years of DNA-methylation age acceleration was associated with 8%–15% higher relative all-cause mortality risk [7]. However, the evidence base was small and heterogeneous, disease and longevity findings were inconsistent, and positive publication bias was a concern. This does not mean “5 fewer years of life,” nor does it show that lowering a clock reduces mortality.
- A 2025 frailty meta-analysis included 24 studies and 28,325 participants. Raw DNA-methylation age and age deviation were not associated with frailty. Several later-generation clocks had small cross-sectional associations (standardized β approximately 0.06–0.11) with high heterogeneity (I² approximately 71%–91%). In longitudinal analyses, only GrimAge had a very small borderline association with increasing frailty; PhenoAge and pace-of-aging measures were not significant [8]. Different clocks do not measure one interchangeable construct.

### Functional trajectories closer to personal health

- A Japanese community cohort repeatedly measured grip strength, gait speed, and one-leg standing in 1,048 adults aged 65 and older, averaging 4.5 assessments. Function generally declined with age but formed different high, middle, and low trajectories; grip and gait trajectories were often parallel rather than showing one shared cliff. Lower grip and one-leg-standing trajectories were associated with mortality [9].
- In a 2026 Italian community cohort of 1,967 older adults, decline in any one of grip strength, gait speed, or chair-stand performance was associated with subsequent mortality: HR 1.73 (95% CI 1.20–2.51) at 2 years and 1.49 (1.26–1.78) at 8 years, compared with no decline [10]. These are prognostic associations, not intervention effects; disease, early frailty, and other factors may contribute to both decline and death.

## Caveats

- Most molecular peaks come from cross-sectional age differences, short repeated sampling, exploratory clustering, or sliding windows. Analytic choices, batch effects, cohort composition, and age distributions can move a peak.
- Blood, one tissue, brain-age models, metabolic age, and whole-body function are not interchangeable “total aging scores.” Organs within one person may age differently.
- Mortality prediction does not establish causation or validate a surrogate endpoint. No evidence shows that treatment guided by a commercial clock prevents disease, disability, or death.
- Cohorts come from selected populations in the United States, United Kingdom, China, Japan, and Italy. Ancestry, social environment, disease burden, and survivor selection limit generalizability.
- Clustering divides continuous variation into trajectory groups; the number and boundaries depend on modeling choices and should not be treated as fixed natural types.

## Do

1. Follow personal trends in blood pressure, waist circumference, glucose, lipids, grip, gait, exercise tolerance, sleep, and daily function rather than one birthday or one “biological age” result.
2. If function is persistently declining, repeat the measurement and look for treatable causes such as anemia, thyroid disease, cardiopulmonary disease, medication effects, undernutrition, depression, pain, or sleep disorders.
3. Continue evidence-based basics at every age: do not smoke, stay active and strength-train, manage blood pressure and metabolic risks, receive recommended vaccines, and use risk-appropriate screening.
4. When a headline names an exact aging age, ask whether it followed the same people for decades, measured a clinical outcome rather than a model score or molecular signal, and replicated in an independent cohort.

## Don't

1. Do not buy anti-aging tests, supplements, hormones, or infusions because you are approaching 44, 50, 60, or another proposed peak.
2. Do not treat one epigenetic-clock or commercial biological-age result as a diagnosis, lifespan countdown, or treatment target.
3. Do not treat a group-average curve or exploratory cluster as personal destiny, and do not delay routine risk management because you are “not at the cliff yet.”

## Medical Red Flags

- Chest pain, shortness of breath, fainting, or new neurological symptoms require prompt assessment; do not attribute them to an “aging transition.”
- Unintentional weight loss, persistent fatigue, or a marked decline in grip, gait, chair rising, or daily function over weeks to months.
- Progressive memory, gait, or self-care decline, or recurrent falls.
