# Obesity

**Mortality Impact**: Cannot be summarized by one fixed percentage; risk varies with obesity class, age, sex, fat distribution, smoking, and pre-existing disease
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Large prospective cohorts show a strong association; causal evidence for treatment varies by population and intervention)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion
BMI is a population screening measure, not a stand-alone measure of an individual's fat mass, health status, or indication for treatment. Assessment should combine BMI with waist circumference or waist-to-height ratio, weight history, organ function, and obesity-related conditions. Health, function, and sustainability should guide shared choices among dietary, activity, behavioral, medication, and metabolic/bariatric surgical options. No single calorie deficit, protein dose, or waist target is appropriate for everyone.

## Mortality evidence: the numbers have strict boundaries
- The [Global BMI Mortality Collaboration, *Lancet*, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27423262/) pooled 239 prospective studies. To reduce confounding by smoking and reverse causation from illness-related weight loss, the primary analysis was restricted to 3.95 million never-smokers without chronic disease at baseline who survived the first 5 years, using BMI 22.5–<25 kg/m² as the reference. All-cause mortality HR was 1.45 (95% CI 1.41–1.48) for class I obesity (30–<35), 1.94 (1.87–2.01) for class II (35–<40), and 2.76 (2.60–2.92) for class III (40–<60).
- In the same analysis, the HR per 5 kg/m² higher BMI above 25 declined with age at measurement, from 1.52 (1.47–1.56) at ages 35–49 to 1.21 (1.17–1.25) at ages 70–89, and was higher in men than women. Therefore, “obesity increases mortality by 20%–30%” is not generalizable across obesity classes or populations and is not an individual's absolute risk.
- [Cerhan et al., *Mayo Clinic Proceedings*, 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24582192/) pooled 11 cohorts of 650,386 White adults. After adjustment for BMI and other factors, each 5 cm greater waist circumference was associated with all-cause mortality HR 1.07 (1.06–1.08) in men and 1.09 (1.08–1.09) in women. Waist circumference adds information beyond BMI, but this remains observational and does not mean that losing 5 cm necessarily reduces mortality by the same proportion.

## Weight, fat distribution, and the “obesity paradox”
- BMI does not distinguish fat from muscle or show fat distribution. A 2025 Lancet Commission recommended using BMI for screening and, except when BMI is >40 kg/m², confirming excess adiposity with direct body-fat measurement or at least one appropriate anthropometric measure, then assessing organ function and limitations in daily activities ([Rubino et al., *Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology*, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39824205/)). This is an expert commission's diagnostic framework, not a mortality-endpoint RCT.
- In some older populations or people with conditions such as heart failure or chronic kidney disease, higher BMI has been associated with lower short-term mortality—the “obesity paradox.” Illness-related weight loss, smoking, selection bias, and failure of BMI to capture muscle and fat distribution can contribute. It does not establish that gaining fat is protective, and HRs from healthy cohorts should not be applied directly to patients.
- Unintentional weight loss and intentional weight management are different exposures. When observational studies find higher mortality among people who lost weight, occult disease may cause both weight loss and death; this does not establish harm from intentional weight loss.

## Weight-loss interventions and hard outcomes
- A 2017 systematic review of 54 dietary weight-loss RCTs involving 30,206 adults with obesity found all-cause mortality RR 0.82 (0.71–0.95), an absolute difference of about 6 fewer deaths per 1,000 participants (2–10 fewer). Cardiovascular mortality RR 0.93 (0.67–1.31) and incident cardiovascular events RR 0.93 (0.83–1.04) remained uncertain ([Ma et al., *BMJ*, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29138133/)). These findings do not establish one calorie deficit as suitable for everyone.
- Look AHEAD randomized 5,145 adults with type 2 diabetes and overweight or obesity. Intensive lifestyle intervention produced more weight loss, but at a median 9.6 years the cardiovascular composite HR was 0.95 (0.83–1.09), not significantly lower ([Wing et al., *NEJM*, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23796131/)). This does not negate benefits in weight, fitness, and some metabolic measures, and it should not be extrapolated to people without diabetes.
- SELECT compared semaglutide 2.4 mg with placebo in 17,604 adults aged ≥45 years with BMI≥27, established cardiovascular disease, and no diabetes. The primary cardiovascular composite occurred in 6.5% versus 8.0%, HR 0.80 (0.72–0.90), while adverse events leading to permanent discontinuation occurred in 16.6% versus 8.2%; Novo Nordisk funded the trial ([Lincoff et al., *NEJM*, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37952131/)). This supports the relevant high-risk population and prescribing context—not universal treatment—and the benefit cannot be attributed entirely to weight loss.
- In the prospective, nonrandomized Swedish SOS controlled study, the surgery group included 2,007 people and usual-care group 2,040; over median follow-up of about 22–24 years, all-cause mortality HR was 0.77 (0.68–0.87), but residual confounding remains possible ([Carlsson et al., *NEJM*, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33053284/)). Surgery requires individualized assessment of severity, complications, risks, and preferences.

## Do
1. After obtaining permission to discuss weight, record weight history and BMI with reliable methods and add waist circumference or waist-to-height ratio. Interpret thresholds cautiously in people with high muscle mass, frailty, or different ethnic backgrounds.
2. With a clinician, assess blood pressure, glucose or HbA1c, lipids, sleep apnea, fatty liver disease, joint problems, and functional limitations. Set goals around health and function, not only the scale.
3. Choose a dietary and activity pattern that is sustainable and compatible with culture and resources; include ability-appropriate resistance activity to support strength and function. Seek a registered dietitian or structured behavioral support when useful.
4. If lifestyle support is insufficient or health risk is high, discuss approved medication or surgery with a qualified clinician, including indications, contraindications, cost, adverse effects, long-term follow-up, and plans for discontinuation or weight recurrence. WHO's recommendation for long-term GLP-1 obesity therapy in adults is conditional and emphasizes comprehensive care ([WHO, 2025](https://www.who.int/news/item/01-12-2025-who-issues-global-guideline-on-the-use-of-glp-1-medicines-in-treating-obesity)).

## Don't
1. Do not treat a fixed calorie deficit, minimum calorie intake, protein dose, or waist cutoff as a universal prescription.
2. Do not buy unregulated weight-loss products or independently escalate, combine, or abruptly stop prescription medicines.
3. Do not stigmatize yourself or others based on one BMI or weight change. Obesity is multifactorial and often relapsing; durable support matters more than crash dieting.
4. Do not interpret the “obesity paradox” as evidence that weight gain is beneficial. People with serious illness, frailty, or recent unintentional weight loss need assessment first.

## Seek professional assessment
- Rapid unintentional weight gain or loss, marked swelling, breathlessness, or other new symptoms.
- Snoring with witnessed apnea, daytime sleepiness, or abnormal blood pressure, glucose, or lipids.
- Binge eating, purging, severe dietary restriction, intense fear of weight gain, or weight management that markedly affects mood or daily life.
- Pregnancy or plans for pregnancy, childhood or adolescence, older-age frailty, complex chronic disease, or use of medicines that can affect weight requires a tailored plan.
