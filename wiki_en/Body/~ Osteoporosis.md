# Osteoporosis

**Mortality Impact**: No validated direct “±1% all-cause mortality” estimate; 1-year mortality is high after hip fracture, but this is not the mortality effect of osteoporosis itself
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (screening and drug fracture prevention are supported by RCTs/systematic reviews; mortality evidence is mainly observational after fracture)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion
Osteoporosis is a disorder of reduced bone mass, bone fragility, and increased fracture risk. “Low bone density,” “osteoporosis by DXA T-score ≤−2.5,” and “fragility fracture” are not interchangeable. Preventing serious outcomes requires risk-based screening, assessment of prior fragility fractures and falls, and antifracture medication when indicated. Calcium, vitamin D, and exercise are supportive measures, not substitutes for indicated drug treatment.

## Evidence
- **Keep mortality outcomes separate**: A review of 70 hip-fracture surgical trials found mean 1-year mortality of 24% in the 1980s, 23% in the 1990s, and 21% after 1999, with no significant difference. This is post-fracture case fatality—not evidence that “osteoporosis increases all-cause mortality by 20%”—and is influenced by age, comorbidity, and fracture severity ([Mundi et al., 2014, PMID: 24397744](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24397744/)).
- **Screening**: The 2025 USPSTF recommends screening women aged 65 years or older. Younger postmenopausal women with risk factors should first undergo clinical risk assessment to decide whether DXA is warranted. Evidence is insufficient for routine screening in men. Across 3 RCTs in older, higher-risk women, screening versus usual care reduced hip fractures (RR 0.83, 95% CI 0.73–0.93; about 5 fewer per 1,000 screened) and major osteoporotic fractures (RR 0.94, 0.88–0.99; about 6 fewer per 1,000) over 3.7–5 years ([Kahwati et al., 2025, PMID: 39808441](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39808441/)).
- **Diagnosis is not the same as total risk**: Central-DXA T-score ≤−2.5 is a commonly used diagnostic threshold in postmenopausal women and men aged 50 or older, but many fragility fractures occur above that threshold. FRAX estimates 10-year hip and major-osteoporotic-fracture probability for ages 40–90 using a country-specific model. It should be interpreted with prior fractures, falls, medications, and diseases; FRAX alone is not a diagnosis.
- **Drugs prevent fractures**: In the USPSTF evidence review, bisphosphonates reduced vertebral fractures (RR 0.51, 0.39–0.66; about 18 fewer per 1,000 treated) and hip fractures (RR 0.67, 0.45–1.00; about 3 fewer per 1,000). FREEDOM randomized 7,868 women aged 60–90 with postmenopausal osteoporosis; over 3 years, denosumab reduced new radiographic vertebral fractures from 7.2% to 2.3% and hip fractures from 1.2% to 0.7%. Generalization to men or other populations requires caution ([Cummings et al., 2009, PMID: 19671655](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19671655/)).
- **Calcium and vitamin D**: A 2026 systematic review of 69 RCTs (153,902 participants) found little to no clinically important fracture benefit among adults not receiving osteoporosis drugs, most of whom were not at high fracture risk: calcium alone RR 0.91 (0.81–1.01), vitamin D alone RR 1.00 (0.95–1.06), and combined supplementation RR 0.91 (0.84–0.99) for any fracture. These findings do not mean that people with osteoporosis should ignore deficiency or adequate intake ([Massé et al., 2026, PMID: 42161415](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42161415/)).
- **Exercise has limits**: A network meta-analysis in community-dwelling older adults associated exercise with fewer falls (RR 0.79, 0.73–0.86) and fall-related fractures (RR 0.62, 0.42–0.90). However, a pragmatic RCT of 9,803 older adults found that screening plus targeted exercise did not reduce fractures over 18 months (rate ratio 1.20, 0.91–1.59). Exercise should therefore be framed as improving strength, balance, and fall risk—not as guaranteed BMD gain or a medication substitute ([Dautzenberg et al., 2021, PMID: 34318929](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34318929/); [Lamb et al., 2020, PMID: 33211928](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33211928/)).

## Do
1. After a fracture from standing height or less, marked height loss, or worsening kyphosis, seek assessment for vertebral fracture, secondary causes, and imminent refracture risk.
2. Discuss DXA screening from age 65 for women. Younger postmenopausal women should first assess risks such as low body weight, parental hip fracture, smoking, and excess alcohol. Men and people using glucocorticoids need individualized assessment; “routine DXA for everyone over 50” is not supported.
3. Meet age- and sex-appropriate calcium needs primarily through food. Vitamin D testing and supplementation should reflect diet, sunlight exposure, 25(OH)D, kidney function, and treatment. There is no universal number of daily sun-exposure minutes.
4. Use progressive weight-bearing, resistance, and balance exercise; obtain tailored advice first if vertebral fracture, marked kyphosis, or high fall risk is present.
5. When medication is indicated, choose it with a clinician. Oral bisphosphonates are commonly given for at least 5 years and IV zoledronate for at least 3 years before risk reassessment—not automatically stopped in everyone. Do not delay or stop denosumab without a planned subsequent antiresorptive ([NOGG 2024](https://www.nogg.org.uk/full-guideline/section-7-strategies-management-osteoporosis-and-fracture-risk)).

## Don't
1. Do not treat low BMD, osteoporosis, and fragility fracture as synonyms or let one T-score define total risk.
2. Do not use calcium, vitamin D, or exercise as a substitute for indicated antifracture medication, and do not self-prescribe high doses.
3. Do not stop bisphosphonates or denosumab on your own. Long-term bisphosphonates carry rare atypical-femur-fracture and osteonecrosis-of-the-jaw risks and require reassessment; stopping denosumab causes rapid rebound bone turnover and can lead to multiple vertebral fractures ([NOGG 2024](https://www.nogg.org.uk/full-guideline/section-6-pharmacological-treatment-options)).

## Medical Red Flags
- Low-trauma fracture, especially of the hip or vertebra
- New persistent back pain, height loss, or worsening kyphosis
- Current or planned long-term glucocorticoid, aromatase-inhibitor, or androgen-deprivation therapy
- New fracture during treatment, or a denosumab injection that may be delayed or interrupted
