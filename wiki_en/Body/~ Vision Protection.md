# Vision Protection

**Mortality Impact**: Unknown; evidence endpoints are myopia incidence/progression, visual-field progression, visual acuity, symptoms, and quality of life—not mortality or lifespan
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (disease-specific; some conclusions have RCT/systematic-review evidence)
**Actionability**: High, but screening, supplements, and treatment must be matched to the person and diagnosis

## Conclusion

"Vision protection" is not one intervention. Refractive error, cataract, age-related macular degeneration (AMD), glaucoma, and diabetic retinopathy have different mechanisms, screening pathways, and treatments. Digital eye strain is mainly a short-term symptom syndrome; it is not equivalent to retinal injury.

For school-age children who are not yet myopic, increasing outdoor time may reduce myopia onset. Evidence that it slows progression in children who are already myopic is uncertain [2]. A blanket annual fundus examination or a single intraocular-pressure measurement for every asymptomatic adult is not a universal evidence-based prescription; people with symptoms, diabetes, known eye disease, or other high-risk features need condition-specific follow-up [5][6][8].

## Evidence: outcomes that must not be combined

- **Myopia onset**: A 2024 Cochrane review included 5 RCTs and 10,733 participants, mostly primary-school children aged 6–9. At 2 years, incidence was 22.5% with outdoor-time interventions versus 26.7% with control (RR 0.84, 95% CI 0.72–0.98; moderate certainty); at 3 years it was 30.5% versus 39.8% (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.59–1.01) [2]. Interventions varied, so these data do not establish one universal daily-minute threshold.
- **Progression of existing myopia**: The same review rated evidence for progression as low certainty. Outdoor activity should not replace a professional myopia-control plan [2]. Spectacles correct refractive blur but do not remove risks associated with axial myopia.
- **Myopia complications**: An observational meta-analysis associated high myopia (spherical equivalent ≤−6.00 D) with retinal detachment (OR 12.62, 95% CI 6.65–23.94) and posterior subcapsular cataract (OR 4.55, 2.66–7.75); moderate/high myopia was associated with open-angle glaucoma (OR 2.92, 1.89–4.52) [1]. These associations do not show that ordinary spectacles or a lifestyle measure reduces risk by the same amounts.
- **Glaucoma**: Direct evidence that screening asymptomatic adults improves vision or quality of life is insufficient. Tonometry alone had sensitivity 0.48 (95% CI 0.31–0.66). In ocular hypertension or diagnosed glaucoma, pressure-lowering treatment reduced progression (RR 0.68, 95% CI 0.49–0.96; absolute difference −4.2%), while the review did not establish improvement in visual acuity, function, or quality of life [6]. In an early open-angle glaucoma RCT, median 6-year progression was 45% with treatment versus 62% with control, but nuclear lens-opacity grading increased with treatment [7].
- **Diabetic retinopathy**: The 2026 ADA guideline recommends an initial dilated comprehensive examination 5 years after onset for adults with type 1 diabetes and at diagnosis for type 2 diabetes. After one or more annual examinations show no retinopathy and glycemia is within target, every 1–2 years may be considered; any retinopathy requires at least annual, and progressive disease more frequent, examinations [8]. This schedule must not be generalized to everyone.
- **AMD and supplements**: AREDS2 is disease-progression management for people with bilateral or unilateral intermediate AMD, not a general "eye vitamin." In 10-year follow-up, lutein/zeaxanthin versus beta-carotene had HR 0.85 (95% CI 0.73–0.98) for late AMD; beta-carotene assignment had OR 1.82 (1.06–3.12) for lung cancer [9]. A 2023 Cochrane review likewise confines the main potential benefit to selected people who already have AMD [12].
- **Cataract and nutrition**: A Cochrane review of 9 RCTs and 117,272 people found no evidence that beta-carotene or vitamins C/E prevent or slow age-related cataract or reduce surgery. Beta-carotene produced RR 0.99 (0.91–1.08) for cataract and RR 1.00 (0.91–1.10) for cataract extraction [11]. Functional impairment warrants surgical assessment rather than delaying care with supplements.
- **Screens, blue light, and eye strain**: A Cochrane review of 17 adult RCTs found that blue-light-filtering lenses may not reduce short-term digital eye strain, sleep findings were indeterminate, and no RCT assessed macular health [4]. The 20-20-20 study had only 29 symptomatic computer users, 2 weeks of follow-up, and no parallel control: symptoms temporarily improved, were not sustained one week after reminders stopped, and tear-film/ocular-surface signs did not improve [3]. It is a comfort strategy, not treatment to prevent blindness or myopia.

## Do

1. For school-age children who are not yet myopic, build regular outdoor time into the day while avoiding sunburn; children with myopia still need professional progression assessment [2].
2. Seek refraction and eye assessment for blur, prescription change, eye pain, or persistent ocular-surface symptoms. Spectacles/contact lenses address refractive error, not AMD, glaucoma, cataract, or retinal disease.
3. With diabetes, diagnosed glaucoma/AMD/high myopia, or another high-risk condition, follow a condition- and risk-specific schedule [6][8].
4. With diagnosed intermediate AMD, ask whether you meet AREDS2 criteria. Neovascular AMD requires timely specialist treatment; anti-VEGF RCT endpoints concern best-corrected visual acuity, not longevity [10].
5. For screen discomfort, adjust text size, distance, and lighting and try short breaks, with comfort as the endpoint [3][4].

## Don't

1. Do not buy blue-light-filtering lenses to prevent macular injury: existing RCTs did not evaluate that clinical endpoint [4].
2. Do not use AREDS2, beta-carotene, or high-dose antioxidant vitamins as general-purpose eye supplements [9][11][12].
3. Do not interpret temporary eye-strain relief as prevention of myopia, cataract, AMD, or glaucoma.
4. Do not use a visual-acuity chart, refraction, or one pressure reading as a substitute for dilated retinal examination, visual fields, OCT, or other disease-directed tests [6].

## Urgent warning signs

- Sudden vision or visual-field loss; a sudden shower of floaters, flashes, or a curtain-like shadow.
- Sudden painful red eye with headache, nausea/vomiting, or halos around lights.
- New distortion of straight lines, a central dark spot, or new blur in a person with diabetes.
