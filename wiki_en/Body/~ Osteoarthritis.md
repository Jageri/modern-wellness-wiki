# Osteoarthritis

**Mortality impact**: Unknown. Direct evidence concerns pain, function, gait, quality of life, imaging structure, and treatment adverse events; it does not establish lower all-cause mortality or longer life
**Evidence level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (multiple guidelines, systematic reviews, and randomized trials support symptom and function management; long-term structural protection and lifespan effects remain unproven)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion

Osteoarthritis is a clinical syndrome centered on activity-related joint pain and impaired function, not a synonym for "degeneration" seen on imaging. With typical features, diagnosis and non-surgical management are generally guided by symptoms and function rather than repeated imaging to "track wear." Individualized therapeutic exercise is core treatment across sites; people with knee or hip osteoarthritis and overweight or obesity can add weight management. Medicines, aids, injections, and surgery must be stratified by site—knee, hip, and hand are not interchangeable—and by comorbidity.

## Evidence

- **Diagnosis is not the same as imaging degeneration**: NICE 2022 advises clinical diagnosis in people aged 45 or older who have activity-related joint pain and either no morning stiffness or stiffness lasting no longer than 30 minutes; routine imaging is not advised unless features are atypical or suggest another condition. In a systematic review, radiographic knee osteoarthritis was found in 15%-76% of people with knee pain, while pain was present in 15%-81% of people with radiographic knee osteoarthritis. An x-ray should therefore not explain an individual's pain in isolation [1,2].
- **Exercise is core treatment, but not proven "cartilage regeneration"**: ACR/Arthritis Foundation recommends exercise for hand, knee, and hip osteoarthritis, tailored to site, ability, and preference [3]. A 2025 knee osteoarthritis network meta-analysis included 217 RCTs and 15,684 participants. Compared with control, aerobic exercise improved pain at short term (SMD -1.10, 95% CI -1.68 to -0.52) and mid term (-1.19, -1.59 to -0.79), with moderate certainty. Follow-up nodes were approximately 4, 12, and 24 weeks, so these data do not show longer-term structural protection [4].
- **Weight management targets symptoms and function**: ACR strongly recommends weight loss for people with knee or hip osteoarthritis and overweight or obesity; NICE says any loss is likely beneficial and that 10% may be better than 5% [1,3]. However, in a community RCT of 823 people aged 50 or older with knee osteoarthritis and BMI≥27, the 18-month diet-plus-exercise versus attention-control difference in WOMAC pain was -0.6 (95% CI -1.0 to -0.1), smaller than the trial's prespecified 1.6-point minimal clinically important difference. Mean weight fell 8% versus 2% [5].
- **Use medicines at the lowest effective dose for the shortest necessary time**: NICE offers topical NSAIDs first for knee osteoarthritis and considers them at other sites. If topical treatment is ineffective or unsuitable, oral NSAIDs may be considered after gastrointestinal, renal, liver, and cardiovascular risk assessment, with gastroprotection [1]. A network meta-analysis of 192 RCTs and 102,829 participants supports topical diclofenac as a first-line pharmacological option for knee osteoarthritis and found that opioid benefit did not outweigh harm [6]. Because the hip is deep, evidence for topical treatment at the knee or hand should not be treated as equivalent hip evidence.
- **Injection is a short-term symptom strategy, not a lifespan or structure-preserving treatment**: NICE considers intra-articular corticosteroid when other medicines are ineffective or unsuitable, or to support therapeutic exercise, while explaining that relief is generally limited to 2-10 weeks [1]. In a 140-person, two-year RCT, triamcinolone every 12 weeks caused greater cartilage-thickness loss than saline (between-group difference -0.11 mm, 95% CI -0.20 to -0.03) without a significant pain difference. This does not support fixed-frequency long-term repeat injection [7].
- **Hyaluronic acid should not be routine**: In a meta-analysis of 169 trials, large placebo-controlled trials found only a 2.0 mm pain difference on a 100 mm scale, below the minimal clinically important difference. Serious adverse events were more frequent (RR 1.49, 95% CI 1.12-1.98), and small-study effects and publication bias were present [8].
- **Surgery follows an appropriate symptom and function threshold**: NICE considers referral for joint replacement when knee, hip, or shoulder pain, stiffness, impaired function, or progressive deformity substantially affects quality of life and non-surgical management is ineffective or unsuitable [1]. Among 100 people with moderate-to-severe knee osteoarthritis already eligible for unilateral total knee replacement, replacement plus non-surgical care produced 15.8 points more KOOS4 improvement at 12 months (95% CI 10.0-21.5) than non-surgical care alone, but serious adverse events numbered 24 versus 6 [9]. NICE does not offer arthroscopic lavage or debridement for osteoarthritis; a BMJ guideline also strongly recommends against arthroscopy for nearly all degenerative knee disease [10].

### ⚠️ Evidence boundaries and counter-evidence

- The strongest exercise outcomes are pain, function, gait, and quality of life—not cartilage thickness, joint replacement, or death. Only 40 trials (18%) in the 2025 review reported safety; "no clear between-group difference" is not proof that every modality is risk-free [4].
- Average effects do not replace individual response. Initial discomfort can occur, and exercise should progress according to tolerance and functional goals rather than a universal fixed dose.
- Knee, hip, and hand osteoarthritis differ in mechanical loading, topical drug penetration, aids, and operations. A first carpometacarpal hand orthosis and a tibiofemoral knee brace are site-specific options, not universal joint treatments [3].
- No evidence cited here shows that oral collagen changes osteoarthritis structure or extends life. It should not displace exercise, weight management, and effective analgesic strategies.

## Do

1. Define the affected joint, pain pattern, functional goal, and comorbidities first. Judge response by pain and real-world function rather than repeated imaging.
2. Individualize aerobic, local strengthening, aquatic, or neuromuscular exercise with a physiotherapist or clinician familiar with osteoarthritis; start at a tolerable level and progress.
3. If you have knee or hip osteoarthritis and overweight or obesity, include sustainable weight management while setting realistic pain and activity goals.
4. If medication is needed, choose it with a clinician according to joint site and gastrointestinal, renal, liver, and cardiovascular risks; topical NSAIDs are usually considered first for the knee.
5. If symptoms substantially impair quality of life despite adequate non-surgical care, discuss the benefits, risks, and recovery burden of referral for joint replacement.

## Don't

1. Do not stop activity or decide on surgery solely because imaging looks "severe"; imaging and pain/function do not map one-to-one.
2. Do not self-treat with long-term oral NSAIDs, and do not treat acetaminophen, opioids, glucosamine, or collagen as disease-modifying therapy.
3. Do not treat hyaluronic acid or fixed-frequency long-term corticosteroid injections as routine "cartilage nourishment."
4. Do not undergo routine arthroscopic lavage or debridement to treat osteoarthritis.

## When to seek care

- A hot, markedly red or swollen joint with fever or systemic illness requires prompt assessment for septic or crystal arthritis.
- Inability to bear weight after recent trauma, sudden true joint locking, rapid deterioration, or progressive deformity.
- Prolonged morning stiffness, symmetric swelling of multiple joints, or rest/night pain unlike typical activity-related pain warrants evaluation for inflammatory arthritis, infection, malignancy, or another diagnosis.
- Persistent major limitation of walking, sleep, or daily activity despite individualized exercise, weight management, and appropriate pain treatment merits specialist review.
