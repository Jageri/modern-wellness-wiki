# Caloric Restriction

**Mortality Impact**: No randomized trial has shown that caloric restriction extends human lifespan or lowers all-cause mortality; human evidence is limited mainly to metabolic and aging biomarkers over 2 years
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐ (Insufficient human hard-outcome evidence; rhesus-monkey survival results depend on study design, diet, and age at initiation and cannot be directly extrapolated)
**Actionability**: Low to medium (long-term adherence is limited, and nutritional, bone, and lean-tissue safety must be protected)

## Conclusion

Caloric restriction means reducing total energy intake without causing malnutrition. It extends lifespan in several laboratory-animal models, but **it has not been shown to extend human lifespan or reduce human all-cause mortality**. The best human randomized evidence comes from CALERIE-2: 220 healthy adults without obesity were randomized and 218 began the intervention; published reports give an observed age range of 21–51 years. The prescribed 25% caloric-restriction group achieved an average energy reduction of about 11.9% over 2 years and improved several cardiometabolic risk factors. The trial was neither long enough nor powered to test mortality, cardiovascular events, or lifespan.

The same trial also found reductions in fat-free mass and bone mineral density at several sites. A post hoc DNA-methylation analysis found a small change only in DunedinPACE, while PhenoAge and GrimAge estimates did not change significantly; surrogate biomarkers cannot be converted into “years of life gained.” Long-term, substantial self-imposed restriction is therefore not justified as a proven longevity intervention.

## Evidence

- **Human randomized trial (CALERIE-2)**: 220 participants were randomized and 218 began the intervention. The prescribed restriction was 25%, but participants achieved an average 11.7%–11.9% over 2 years; 82% of the restriction group and 95% of controls completed the trial. Published reports give an observed age range of 21–51 years and BMI 21.9–28.0 kg/m². Results from these healthy adults without obesity cannot be directly extrapolated to older adults, people with low body weight, chronic disease, or other high-risk groups.[4][5]
- **Cardiometabolic surrogate outcomes**: Relative to the ad libitum group, the restriction group lost an average 7.5 kg and improved blood pressure, LDL cholesterol, total-to-HDL cholesterol ratio, CRP, insulin sensitivity, and metabolic-syndrome score. These were risk factors, not clinical events, mortality, or lifespan outcomes, and most were exploratory analyses.[5]
- **Lean tissue and bone**: At 2 years, fat-free mass decreased by about 2.0 kg relative to control. Bone mineral density fell more at the lumbar spine, total hip, and femoral neck; whether this increases long-term fracture risk remains unknown.[6][7]
- **Aging biomarkers**: A post hoc CALERIE analysis found a small slowing in DunedinPACE but no significant change in PhenoAge or GrimAge. The authors stated that long-term follow-up is still needed for chronic-disease and mortality endpoints. Some DunedinPACE authors were inventors on the measure, which had been licensed to a commercial entity.[8]
- **Rhesus-monkey survival studies conflict**: In the Wisconsin study, controls versus the approximately 30%-restricted group had an all-cause mortality HR of 1.78 (95% CI 1.04–3.04), while the NIA study found no survival improvement. A joint comparison identified diet composition, control intake, sex, and age at restriction onset as possible modifiers. It supports the possibility that some health effects translate across primates, not proof of human life extension. Authors of the 2017 joint analysis disclosed company employment, board, or nutrition/obesity consulting relationships, while stating that these activities did not directly benefit from the work and that there were no direct competing financial interests.[1][2][3]
- **Current review of harms**: A 2025 review concluded that human lifespan benefits remain uncertain. Potential downsides include hunger, cold sensitivity, bone effects, wound healing, reproduction, and infection vulnerability, although much of this evidence comes from animals and long-term human risks remain incompletely documented.[9]

## What to Do

1. **Do not treat caloric restriction as a proven longevity prescription**: CALERIE established 2-year feasibility and changes in surrogate outcomes, not life extension.
2. **If the goal is weight loss or metabolic-risk reduction, design the plan for that clinical goal**: Preserve protein, vitamins, minerals, and overall diet quality, and individualize the energy deficit to body weight, activity, illness, and medication rather than mechanically cutting 25%.
3. **Monitor adverse changes during prolonged restriction**: Watch for unintended continued weight loss, fatigue, cold intolerance, menstrual or sexual changes, impaired physical performance, loss of lean tissue, and bone-health concerns. Do not intensify self-restriction if these occur; seek clinical assessment.
4. **Do not extrapolate to populations CALERIE did not study**: Children and adolescents, pregnant or breastfeeding people, those who are underweight, older or frail, anyone with a current or prior eating disorder, and people needing disease-specific nutrition management or glucose-lowering medication review should obtain clinician or registered-dietitian assessment first.
5. **Do not merge distinct interventions**: “Eating until 80% full,” 16:8, 5:2, smallest-dinner patterns, and continuous caloric restriction are not the same tested intervention. CALERIE does not validate those practices.

## Limitations

- Core human evidence comes mainly from one relatively small, 2-year US trial of younger, healthy adults without obesity.
- The prescribed restriction was 25%, but the achieved reduction was about 12%; the net benefit and safety of larger long-term restriction cannot be inferred.
- Cardiometabolic measures, energy metabolism, and DNA methylation are surrogate outcomes. There is no randomized human evidence for all-cause mortality, lifespan, or long-term clinical events.
- Rhesus-monkey studies were small and differed in diet, feeding practice, control intake, and age at restriction onset; survival findings were inconsistent.
- Multiple CALERIE publications analyze the same participants and are not independent replications. Post hoc and multi-outcome analyses also increase the chance of positive findings.
- Long-term diet trials cannot readily be blinded and face adherence and selection bias. Human lifespan trials also face long follow-up, high cost, and potential nonpublication of null findings.
