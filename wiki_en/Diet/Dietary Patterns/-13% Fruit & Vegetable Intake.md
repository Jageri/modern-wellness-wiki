# Fruit & Vegetable Intake

**Mortality impact**: 5 servings/day versus 2 servings/day was associated with 13% lower relative all-cause mortality (HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.85–0.90)
**Evidence level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (consistent large prospective cohorts and meta-analyses; observational association, not proof of causality)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion

WHO recommends at least 400 g of fruit and vegetables per day for people older than 10 years. In a 2021 dose-response meta-analysis, about 5 servings/day (2 fruit and 3 vegetable servings) versus 2 servings/day was associated with 13% lower all-cause mortality. This is a relative-risk result, not a 13-percentage-point reduction in every individual's absolute mortality risk.

There is no proven universal “optimal maximum.” The newer meta-analysis found no further decline above about 5 servings/day; a 2017 meta-analysis found that most benefit occurred at lower intakes but suggested a smaller additional association up to about 800 g/day. Both rely on observational cohorts. They do not prove life extension or require everyone to force intake to 800 g to chase a percentage.

## Evidence

- **Wang DD et al., Circulation 2021 ([PMID 33641343](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33641343/))**: Two US cohorts plus 24 other prospective cohorts, totaling 1,892,885 participants and 145,015 deaths. The pooled RR for 5 versus 2 servings/day was 0.87 (0.85–0.88); the HR in the two US cohorts was 0.87 (0.85–0.90). Mortality was lowest around 5 servings/day, with no additional decline above that level. Cause-specific outcomes must not be substituted for all-cause mortality: HRs for CVD, cancer, and respiratory mortality in the same study were 0.88, 0.90, and 0.65.
- **Aune et al., International Journal of Epidemiology 2017 ([PMID 28338764](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28338764/))**: Systematic review of 95 prospective studies. Each 200 g/day increment in total fruit and vegetables was associated with all-cause mortality RR 0.90 (0.87–0.93), with high heterogeneity (I²=83%). The curve suggested reductions up to about 800 g/day. Its global attributable-death estimates explicitly assume that the association is causal and are not proven individual benefits.
- **Wang X et al., BMJ 2014 ([PMID 25073782](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25073782/))**: Sixteen prospective cohorts, 833,234 participants, and 56,423 deaths. Each additional serving/day was associated with all-cause mortality HR 0.95 (0.92–0.98), with no further reduction above about 5 servings/day. Cancer mortality was not appreciably associated, so all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality cannot be collapsed into one effect size.
- **Du et al., International Journal of Epidemiology 2017 ([PMID 28449053](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28449053/))**: 462,342 adults aged 30–79 without major chronic disease at baseline from 10 Chinese regions, followed for about 7 years. Eating fresh fruit on at least 4 days/week versus never/rarely was associated with all-cause mortality RR 0.73 (0.70–0.76). This is a fresh-fruit-only result against an extremely low-intake reference in China; it cannot support a `-27%` title for total fruit and vegetable intake.
- **Onni et al., Advances in Nutrition 2025 ([PMID 39956388](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39956388/))**: An umbrella review of 41 food-group meta-analyses involving more than 1 million participants. Higher fruit and vegetable intake was consistently associated with lower all-cause mortality, but many analyses had significant heterogeneity and AMSTAR-2 quality ranged from high to critically low. It supports the direction of association, not a universal percentage without a defined dose and reference.

## What “5 servings” and the food groups mean

- WHO's public-health target is at least 400 g/day. Definitions and weights for a “serving” vary across studies, so servings in every cohort cannot be mechanically converted to one gram value.
- The 2021 US cohort exposure excluded fruit juice, French fries, whole or mashed potatoes, tofu, and soybeans. Juice and potatoes were not associated with total mortality. That does not mean they are necessarily harmful; it means they cannot replace the whole fruits and non-starchy vegetables underlying the headline effect.
- Legumes are a nutritious separate food group. Some international cohorts combine legumes with fruit and vegetables, while others do not. Any effect estimate must retain the source study's definition.
- Fresh, frozen, and canned produce without added sugars or excess sodium can all contribute. Most fruit juices, including products without added sugar, contain substantial free sugars and WHO advises limiting them rather than treating them as whole fruit.

## Limitations, absolute risk, and funding

- The evidence is almost entirely observational. People with higher fruit and vegetable intake often smoke less, exercise more, and differ in overall diet and socioeconomic conditions. Residual confounding and reverse causation can remain after adjustment.
- Intake was usually self-reported using food-frequency questionnaires; serving definitions, classification, and measurement frequency differed across studies. The 2021 US cohorts repeated diet measurement every 2–4 years, but reporting error remains possible.
- The `13%` is a relative-risk difference. The studies do not provide one absolute risk difference transferable to a general reader; absolute benefit depends on age, health, follow-up duration, and baseline mortality risk.
- US nurses and male health professionals do not represent every population. The Chinese fresh-fruit study used an unusually low-intake reference. Portions and energy needs should be individualized for children, older adults, and people with chronic disease.
- The 2017 Aune meta-analysis was supported by a Norwegian medical research foundation, Norwegian regional health/university bodies, and the UK NIHR; authors declared no conflicts. The 2021 study was NIH-supported. The Chinese cohort had charitable and UK/Chinese public research support, funders had no research role, and authors declared no conflicts. The 2025 umbrella review reported no conflicts. No direct produce-industry funding was identified, but non-industry funding does not remove design bias.
- Aune 2017 detected publication bias in some analyses, and the 2021 analysis also assessed small-study bias. The 2025 umbrella review still found heterogeneity and variable review quality, so the association should not be presented as certain causation.

## Do

1. Use at least 400 g/day as a practical starting target, emphasizing whole fruit and varied non-starchy vegetables; there is no need to chase 800 g or a slogan that “more colors guarantee longer life.”
2. A 2-fruit plus 3-vegetable structure is a useful guide, adjusted for energy needs, local foods, and tolerance. Do not substitute juice for whole fruit.
3. Increase gradually if your usual fiber intake is low or you develop bloating or diarrhea. People with kidney disease, potassium restrictions, or a prescribed diet should individualize intake with their clinical team.
4. Food safety matters: rinse produce under running water, keep it separate from raw animal foods, refrigerate cut produce promptly, and do not wash food with soap, detergent, or bleach.
5. Pregnant people, adults 65 or older, immunocompromised people, and young children face greater risk of severe foodborne illness. Avoid raw or undercooked sprouts and unpasteurized juice; cook sprouts until steaming hot.
