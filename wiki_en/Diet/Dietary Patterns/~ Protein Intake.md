# Protein Intake

**Mortality Impact**: No reliable single number; findings for total protein conflict, while replacing animal with plant protein is associated with lower all-cause mortality
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (multiple large prospective cohorts and meta-analyses; no long-term mortality RCT)
**Actionability**: Moderate (protein foods can be changed, but quantity should reflect age, nutritional status, and kidney function)

## Bottom Line

For adults in general, current evidence does not establish that either more or less total protein extends life. The more consistent observational signal is that **replacing some animal-protein foods with plant-protein foods in isocaloric models is associated with lower all-cause mortality**. This is not randomized evidence of causation, and the protein source cannot be separated cleanly from the rest of the food pattern.

## Mortality Evidence

- **Barrantes-Espinola et al., 2026**: A meta-analysis of 9 prospective cohorts (1,036,799 participants) found that isocaloric substitution of plant for animal protein at 3% or 5% of total energy was associated with lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.87–0.94). Heterogeneity was high (I²=77%), and there was no significant difference between the 3% and 5% subgroups. These were observational substitution models, not randomized food-replacement trials. [PMID: 41996864](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41996864/)
- **Naghshi et al., 2020**: Across 32 prospective cohorts (715,128 participants; 113,039 deaths), the highest versus lowest total-protein category was associated with lower all-cause mortality (pooled effect size 0.94, 0.89–0.99), as was plant protein (0.92, 0.87–0.97); animal protein was not significantly associated with all-cause mortality. Each additional 3% of energy from plant protein was associated with 5% lower relative risk of all-cause death. [PMID: 32699048](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32699048/)
- **Chen et al., 2020**: A different meta-analysis of 11 cohorts (350,452 participants; 64,306 deaths) found the opposite association for total protein (highest versus lowest RR 1.05, 1.01–1.10) and an inverse association for plant protein (RR 0.93, 0.87–0.99). In the Rotterdam cohort, the adverse signal for animal protein mainly concerned cardiovascular mortality rather than a stable animal-protein effect on all-cause mortality. [PMID: 32076944](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32076944/)
- **Huang et al., 2020**: In the NIH-AARP cohort (416,104 participants; 77,614 deaths), each additional 10 g/1000 kcal of plant protein was associated with all-cause mortality HRs of 0.88 in men and 0.86 in women. Adjusted 16-year absolute risk differences were -0.95 and -0.86 percentage points, respectively. Replacing 3% of energy from animal protein with plant protein was associated with 10% lower relative all-cause mortality in both sexes. The cohort's mean baseline age was about 62, limiting generalization to all ages and settings. [PMID: 32658243](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32658243/)

> **Interpretation**: The two 2020 meta-analyses point in opposite directions for total protein, so no longevity number is assigned to total intake. Substitution estimates depend on what replaces what and may reflect fiber, fat quality, processing, and the overall dietary pattern rather than an isolated effect of protein molecules.

## Age, Muscle Outcomes, and Kidney Function

- **Protein targets in older adults are conditional**: The ESPEN expert group recommended at least 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day for healthy older adults and 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day for those who are malnourished or at risk because of acute or chronic illness. The higher range is not a blanket target for everyone aged 65 or older. [PMID: 24814383](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24814383/)
- **Muscle outcomes are not lifespan outcomes**: A 2024 meta-analysis of 30 RCTs found no significant effect of whey supplementation on grip strength, gait speed, 6-minute walk, or lean body mass. With resistance exercise, lower-body strength improved modestly (SMD 0.25, 0.05–0.45). These are muscle or body-composition surrogate outcomes and do not establish lower all-cause mortality. [PMID: 39303495](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39303495/)
- **High-protein advice should not be copied into chronic kidney disease**: KDIGO 2024 suggests about 0.8 g/kg/day for adults with CKD G3–G5 and advises those at risk of progression to avoid >1.3 g/kg/day. Frailty, malnutrition, dialysis status, and comorbidity require individualized renal nutrition care. [PMID: 38519239](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38519239/)

## Do

1. **Change food sources before chasing a "longevity gram target"**: with energy roughly unchanged, replace some animal-protein foods, particularly red meat, with legumes, nuts, and whole-grain protein foods.
2. **For older adults, assess protein together with resistance exercise, total energy intake, and nutritional status** rather than relying on supplements alone.
3. **Individualize intake in CKD, substantial unintentional weight loss, or suspected sarcopenia** with a clinician or registered dietitian using kidney function, albuminuria, body weight, and activity level.

## Don't

1. Do not present observational HRs or RRs as a guaranteed longevity benefit from changing foods.
2. Do not treat "animal protein" as one exposure: fish, dairy, eggs, unprocessed meat, and processed meat differ substantially as foods.
3. Do not automatically prescribe 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day solely because someone is over 65, and do not self-prescribe a high-protein diet in CKD.

## Evidence Limitations

- Mortality evidence relies mainly on food-frequency questionnaires and one or a few dietary assessments, leaving measurement error, residual confounding, and healthy-user bias.
- Studies differ in the displaced nutrient, protein source, baseline intake, and population. All-cause mortality heterogeneity in the 2026 substitution meta-analysis was 77%.
- Absolute risks are rarely reported. Huang's absolute risk differences cannot be transported directly to younger, non-US, or different-baseline-risk populations.
- One author of the 2026 substitution meta-analysis disclosed consulting fees from Danone outside the submitted work; the other authors declared no relevant financial or nonfinancial interests.
