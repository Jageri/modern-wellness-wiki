# Ultra-Processed Foods

**Mortality impact**: All-cause mortality relative risk +15–21% (highest vs lowest intake groups; observational syntheses)

**Evidence level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (multiple prospective cohorts point in the same direction, but mortality causality is unproven)

**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion

Move ultra-processed foods from dietary staples to occasional choices, starting with sugar-sweetened drinks, processed meats, and heavily consumed ready-to-eat or ready-to-heat products. Do not use “more than five ingredients” as a stand-alone rule: NOVA classification concerns the industrial formulation and purpose of processing, not merely ingredient count.

## How to interpret the numbers

- A 2025 updated meta-analysis included 18 prospective cohorts, 1,148,387 participants, and 173,107 deaths. Fifteen studies contributed to the highest-versus-lowest analysis: HR 1.15 (95% CI 1.09–1.22). The category cutoffs differed, so the “highest group” cannot be translated into one universal number of grams or servings; heterogeneity was high (I²=83%). In its dose-response model, every **10-percentage-point** increase in UPF share of total energy/weight was associated with HR 1.10 (95% CI 1.04–1.16; I²=91%). The model treated energy and weight shares as equivalent and converted 50 g to one serving, adding uncertainty.
- A 2024 BMJ umbrella review reported RR 1.21 (95% CI 1.15–1.27) for higher versus lower UPF exposure and all-cause mortality. It called the association highly suggestive, but rated it low certainty by GRADE. Another umbrella review reported RR 1.02 (95% CI 1.01–1.03) per 50 g/day and rated this outcome moderate certainty. Estimates based on different exposure units are not interchangeable.
- Important limiting evidence tempers the headline. In US nurses and health professionals, the top versus bottom intake quartile had only 4% higher all-cause mortality (HR 1.04, 95% CI 1.01–1.07), with no association for cancer or cardiovascular mortality and no consistent association within strata of overall diet quality. Unadjusted all-cause mortality rates were 1,472 versus 1,536 per 100,000 person-years, a crude difference of 64 per 100,000 person-years; this is not a portable individual absolute risk. A 2026 PLCO analysis reported HR 1.10 (95% CI 1.06–1.14) for the top versus bottom quartile, but again found no consistent association within most overall-diet-quality strata.

## NOVA classification and exposure

NOVA group 4 comprises industrial formulations made largely from substances extracted from foods, modified from food constituents, or synthesized industrially, usually with little intact group-1 food and often with flavorings, colors, emulsifiers, sweeteners, or other additives used for flavor or texture. Common examples include sugar-sweetened beverages, some ready-to-eat or ready-to-heat mixed dishes, processed meats, packaged snacks, and desserts. “Processed” is not synonymous with “ultra-processed”: frozen vegetables and pasteurized milk, for example, can remain minimally processed.

Most cohorts estimated intake from self-reported food-frequency questionnaires or 24-hour recalls and then assigned NOVA categories retrospectively. Older questionnaires often lacked brand, formulation, or processing detail, creating classification error; product composition also differs across countries.

## Causality, populations, and limitations

- All mortality evidence is observational. Residual confounding by smoking, income, education, healthcare access, physical activity, and overall diet quality, plus reverse causation, cannot be excluded. Relative risks cannot be converted directly into years of life lost.
- The updated meta-analysis was dominated by US and European cohorts, with few Korean, Brazilian, and multinational cohorts. Mean baseline age was about 52 years and mean follow-up 14.5 years. Generalization to younger people, lower-income countries, and other food cultures is uncertain.
- Cause-specific findings are inconsistent. Some syntheses report cardiovascular-mortality associations, but the Fang cohort found none for cardiovascular or cancer mortality, and associations varied substantially by UPF subtype. The headline therefore refers only to all-cause mortality association; it does not mean every cause of death or every NOVA-4 food carries a 15–21% increase.
- Liang was funded by the Beijing Nova Program and the authors declared no competing interests. Lane disclosed that some authors' center had previously received research support from food companies and that some authors were investigators on a trial partly funded by Be Fit Food. This does not invalidate the review, but it warrants transparent disclosure.

## What randomized feeding trials can establish

In a 2019 NIH inpatient randomized crossover trial, 20 adults received two weeks each of ad libitum ultra-processed and unprocessed diets. Energy intake was 508±106 kcal/day greater on the ultra-processed diet; participants gained 0.9±0.3 kg during that phase and lost 0.9±0.3 kg during the unprocessed phase. A 2025 UK outpatient crossover trial designed both diets to meet the Eatwell Guide: 55 adults were randomized, 50 entered the primary analysis, and each diet lasted eight weeks. Both diets produced weight loss, but the minimally processed phase produced 1.01 percentage points more weight loss (95% CI 0.14–1.87).

These trials support a causal claim that the tested dietary packages can alter short-term energy intake or weight. They do not establish effects on mortality, lifespan, or any specific additive. Samples were small and follow-up short, and each diet changed food structure, texture, eating rate, and multiple ingredients together. The 2025 trial also disclosed several author interests, including obesity-drug-company consulting/shareholding and royalties from UPF-related books; PubMed lists public/charitable funding and no food-industry grant for the trial.

## Do

1. Identify the one or two UPF categories you consume most often. Replace sugar-sweetened drinks with water or unsweetened drinks and processed meats with fresh or minimally processed protein sources.
2. Protect overall diet quality at the same time: emphasize vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, and nuts rather than assuming that a “high protein” or “zero sugar” claim makes a product healthy.
3. When NOVA classification is unclear, use more robust decisions: fewer sugary drinks, processed meats, and high-salt/high-energy-density snacks; more recognizable whole foods.
