# Eggs

**Mortality Impact**: Observational syntheses do not show a stable, generalizable all-cause mortality effect; “1–2 eggs daily” is not a universal longevity or safety prescription
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐ (mortality and cardiovascular outcomes come mainly from heterogeneous observational studies; randomized trials cover only short-term surrogate outcomes such as blood lipids)
**Actionability**: Medium

## Conclusion

Eggs can be one protein source in a healthy diet, but the statement that “1–2 eggs daily is safe for every healthy adult” goes beyond the evidence. Associations between roughly one egg per day and higher or lower all-cause mortality vary by analysis, region, and modeling method. No long-term randomized trial has shown that a fixed egg intake extends life or reduces mortality.

Whether and how much to eat should be judged in the context of the whole diet, the displaced food, and an individual's LDL-C/non-HDL-C level. There is no reason to discard the yolk universally because of a generalized fear of cholesterol, but the yolk's nutrient content does not erase its cholesterol load.

## Evidence

- **The latest umbrella review does not support a fixed mortality effect**: A 2025 review included 14 meta-analyses published since 2020. For high intake (median about 1.07 eggs/day) versus low intake (about 0.03 egg/day), the all-cause mortality RR was 1.03 (95% CI 0.97–1.09; I²=85.3%), showing no clear association. Modeling each additional egg/day gave RR 1.07 (1.02–1.12; I²=84.8%), but the evidence was weak. Most included reviews were of critically low methodological quality, so these findings prove neither that 1–2 eggs/day is safe nor that it is harmful [1].
- **Another mortality meta-analysis shows that the comparison changes the conclusion**: In a 2022 synthesis of 55 prospective studies, high versus low egg intake gave an all-cause mortality RR of 1.03 (0.97–1.09; I²=85.3%). The linear model per additional egg/day gave RR 1.07 (1.02–1.12; I²=84.8%), while the nonlinear analysis found no clear association from the lowest intake through one egg/day; higher risk appeared mainly around 1.5 eggs/day or more. All data were observational and vulnerable to dietary measurement error, residual confounding, and regional differences [2].
- **Cardiovascular incidence is not mortality or lifespan**: In three U.S. cohorts and an updated 2020 meta-analysis, each additional egg/day was not significantly associated with incident cardiovascular disease (RR 0.98, 0.93–1.03; I²=62.3%). Asian cohorts showed a lower risk (RR 0.92, 0.85–0.99), whereas U.S. and European cohorts did not; the region interaction was P=0.07, so the Asian subgroup cannot be interpreted as proof that eggs protect the cardiovascular system [3].
- **Randomized trials show that lipid responses are not negligible**: In a meta-analysis of 17 randomized trials in healthy participants, higher egg intake increased LDL-C by a mean 8.14 mg/dL (95% CI 4.46–11.82) and the LDL-C/HDL-C ratio by 0.14 (0.05–0.22). Trials lasted only 21–84 days, used varied comparators and egg doses, and did not measure cardiovascular events or mortality. They establish only short-term changes in surrogate markers [4].
- **Authoritative guidance emphasizes dietary patterns, not a single-food permission slip**: An American Heart Association science advisory found that multinational observational evidence generally did not show a significant association between dietary cholesterol and cardiovascular disease, while intervention studies above current average cholesterol intake usually increased total cholesterol or LDL-C. Its emphasis was a healthy pattern rich in vegetables, fruit, whole grains, nuts, and appropriate protein sources, not a fixed egg target for everyone [5].
- **Conflicts of interest matter**: A systematic review of 211 egg-and-cholesterol studies found industry funding in 60% of studies from 2010–2019. Conclusions were discordant with objective findings in 49% of industry-funded intervention studies versus 13% of non-industry-funded studies. The review authors also disclosed institutional roles, compensation, or royalties tied to advocacy for plant-based diets, so this paper is evidence of bias risk rather than a stand-alone verdict that eggs are harmful [6]. The 2025 umbrella review reported no direct study funding and no commercial conflicts, but acknowledged an unconditional institutional-activities contribution from food company Fileni Alimentare S.p.A. to the Italian Society of Human Nutrition, to which the authors belong [1].

## How to Use Them

1. Treat eggs as one part of the whole diet. Do not use “1–2 daily” as a longevity target, and do not add eggs solely for health promotion.
2. Consider the replacement: substituting eggs for processed meat is different from adding eggs to a diet already high in animal foods and saturated fat.
3. If eating eggs regularly, use cooking methods with modest added oil and salt. This manages the whole meal; it does not prove that boiled eggs lower mortality.
4. People with elevated LDL-C or non-HDL-C, familial hypercholesterolemia, diabetes, or cardiovascular disease should not automatically apply average-population observational findings. Adjust intake with a clinician or registered dietitian in the context of the full diet and follow-up laboratory results.

## Uncertainty and Limitations

- There is no large trial that randomized long-term egg intake and measured mortality or major cardiovascular events.
- Cohorts mostly relied on one or a few food-frequency questionnaires and cannot fully separate bacon, processed meat, refined carbohydrates, cooking fats, or socioeconomic factors.
- “High” and “low” intake and egg size differ across regions. U.S., European, and Asian findings are not automatically interchangeable.
- Relative risks cannot be converted into an individual's absolute risk without age, baseline risk, follow-up time, and information about the replaced food.
- High heterogeneity, overlapping cohorts, and selective publication can destabilize small associations. Short-term lipid trials also cannot be translated directly into mortality risk.

## Safety

- Raw or undercooked eggs can carry foodborne pathogens such as *Salmonella*. Pregnant people, young children, older adults, and immunocompromised people should especially favor fully cooked eggs or pasteurized egg products [7].
- People with diagnosed egg allergy should avoid the relevant food. Breathing difficulty, throat or tongue swelling, generalized hives, dizziness, or shock after eating requires emergency care [8].
- This entry does not replace lipid assessment or individualized nutrition therapy. A null overall mortality association is not a reason to stop lipid-lowering medication.
