# High Salt / High Sodium

**Mortality Impact**: No single all-cause mortality effect estimate is representative of the general population
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (sodium reduction lowers blood pressure; mortality evidence is limited by measurement and population)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion

General adults can use the World Health Organization target of less than 2 g sodium per day, equivalent to about 5 g salt, as an overall dietary direction [1]. This is a **total sodium** target, not only salt added during cooking; sodium from soy sauce, soup bases, pickled foods, restaurant meals, and processed foods also counts. One gram of sodium is equivalent to about 2.54 g salt.

This target does not mean that “high salt increases mortality by 20%.” All-cause mortality evidence is mainly observational and changes with the method used to measure sodium intake. Randomized evidence reliably supports blood-pressure reduction, but mortality evidence in the general population remains insufficient.

## Evidence

- **All-cause mortality with a stronger exposure measure**: TOHP collected three to seven 24-hour urine samples over 1–3 years from US adults aged 30–54 years with high-normal blood pressure but without hypertension. In the observational analysis, each additional 1,000 mg/24 h sodium was associated with all-cause mortality HR 1.12 (95% CI 1.00–1.26). The randomized sodium-reduction group had mortality HR 0.85 (0.66–1.09), which was not statistically significant [2]. This is not a universal absolute risk and does not support a `+20%` title.
- **J/U-shaped counter-evidence and measurement controversy**: PURE used one morning spot urine sample and an equation to estimate 24-hour sodium excretion. Among 101,945 people in 17 countries, estimated sodium of 3–6 g/day was associated with lower risk of a composite of death and major cardiovascular events than lower or higher estimates [3]. However, spot-urine equations have large random and systematic error at the individual level; applying one to TOHP data changed an originally linear sodium–mortality association into a J shape. Relevant professional organizations advise against using spot-urine equations to study individual sodium intake and health outcomes [4,5]. Reverse causation—illness or medical advice causing lower intake—can also make low sodium appear harmful.
- **Blood pressure is a surrogate outcome**: A systematic review of randomized trials found that lower sodium reduced adult systolic blood pressure by a mean 3.39 mmHg (95% CI 2.46–4.31) and diastolic pressure by 1.54 mmHg (0.98–2.11) [6]. Effects vary by baseline blood pressure, age, and achieved intake change. This cannot be summarized as a 5–10 mmHg reduction for everyone or directly converted into mortality.
- **Mortality in randomized trials**: A Cochrane review found no confirmed all-cause mortality reduction from long-term salt-reduction advice in normotensive or hypertensive participants. At the longest follow-up, the respective RRs were 0.90 (0.58–1.40) and 0.99 (0.87–1.14), with too few events and insufficient power [7].
- **Potassium-enriched salt substitute is not simply “eating less salt”**: SSaSS compared salt containing 75% sodium chloride and 25% potassium chloride with regular salt in 20,995 rural Chinese adults who had prior stroke or were aged ≥60 years with hypertension. Over 4.74 years, all-cause mortality rates were 39.28 versus 44.61 per 1,000 person-years, rate ratio 0.88 (0.82–0.95) [8]. This open-label, village-cluster randomized trial in a high-risk population cannot be directly generalized to young, low-risk adults and cannot separate the effects of less sodium from more potassium.
- **Intake in China**: A nationally representative 2015 survey collected complete 24-hour urine samples from 10,114 adults. Mean sodium excretion was 4,121 mg/day, equivalent to about 10.5 g salt and more than twice the WHO limit [9]. This was a cross-sectional intake survey, not a mortality study.

## Do

1. Read “sodium” on nutrition labels, convert with 1 g sodium ≈ 2.54 g salt, and add sodium from all foods, condiments, and meals across the day.
2. Gradually use less salt, soy sauce, oyster sauce, soup base, and dipping sauce; use vinegar, lemon, herbs, and spices for flavor.
3. Choose fewer pickled foods, salty snacks, processed meats, and high-sodium ready-to-eat products. Avoiding pickles alone does not ensure a low total sodium intake.
4. If using a potassium-enriched lower-sodium salt, treat it as a replacement for part of regular table salt, not permission to use unlimited amounts. WHO's 2025 recommendation is conditional [10].

## Don't

1. Do not estimate 5 g salt with “one bottle cap”; cap size and filling are not standardized.
2. Do not treat one spot-urine estimate as your usual sodium intake or use it to decide that intake is “too low.” Complete repeated 24-hour urine collections are stronger for individual usual intake in research; dietary questionnaires can miss discretionary salt and changing product recipes.
3. Do not equate lower blood pressure, stroke, or cardiovascular mortality with all-cause mortality or longer life.
4. Do not independently switch to potassium-enriched salt if you have impaired kidney function, another condition that limits potassium excretion, or take potassium-sparing diuretics or potassium supplements. Children and pregnant women are also outside the scope of WHO's 2025 lower-sodium salt-substitute recommendation [10].
5. People with heart failure, type 1 diabetes, risk of hyponatremia, or a medically supervised diet should not independently pursue a uniform low-sodium target [1].

## When to Seek Medical Advice

- Diagnosed hypertension with home readings persistently above the individualized target set by your clinician.
- Heart failure, kidney disease, hyponatremia, or medicines affecting sodium/potassium balance before making a large salt change or using potassium-enriched salt.
- Marked weakness, altered mental status, palpitations, or muscle weakness; these symptoms are nonspecific and require assessment with the medication history and electrolyte testing.

## Evidence Boundaries

- “Sodium” in mortality studies is often estimated from urinary sodium; “salt” usually means sodium chloride. Their gram values are not interchangeable.
- Repeated 24-hour urinary sodium is stronger for estimating an individual's usual intake. A single 24-hour collection, spot-urine equations, and dietary questionnaires have different errors.
- Observational associations between high sodium and all-cause mortality do not establish causality. The most consistent direct randomized endpoint is blood pressure. Salt-substitute trials support clinical benefit in selected high-risk populations but change both sodium and potassium.
- Pooled HRs and RRs do not provide a universal personal absolute risk. Baseline risk, age, disease, medication, and food sources all alter benefit and harm.

- The WHO evidence review was publicly supported by WHO and other public sources; some authors disclosed unpaid membership in salt-reduction advocacy organizations [6].
