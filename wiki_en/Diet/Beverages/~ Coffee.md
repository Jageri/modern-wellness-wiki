# Coffee

**Mortality Impact**: Moderate intake is associated with lower all-cause mortality; estimates are roughly 14%–17% lower in different studies, but causality is unproven and no single number applies to every definition of a “cup”
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Consistent large prospective cohorts and dose-response meta-analyses; no established causal effect on all-cause mortality)
**Actionability**: Medium

## Conclusion

Habitual coffee drinking is consistently associated with lower all-cause mortality in general adult populations, but the evidence is predominantly observational. A 2014 dose-response meta-analysis found the lowest relative risk near 4 cups/day (16% lower; 95% CI 13%–18%); a 2017 umbrella review reported RR 0.83 (95% CI 0.79–0.88) for 3–4 cups/day versus none[^1][^2]. This is not a 17-percentage-point absolute reduction and cannot be translated into years of life gained.

A “cup” is not standardized. Studies in the umbrella review used cups, servings, milliliters, and other measures; bean, roast, and brewing method also change caffeine and other compounds[^2]. A 2025 US NHANES cohort defined one cup as 8 fluid ounces (about 240 mL) and reported HR 0.83 (95% CI 0.75–0.93) for 2 to <3 cups/day and 0.85 (0.77–0.95) for ≥3 cups/day[^11]. These study cups are not interchangeable with an espresso shot or a large café drink.

## How to read the evidence

- **Dose-response**: The association is nonlinear, with the lowest point around 3–4 cups/day and no further reduction above about 4 cups. In three US cohorts, 1–5 cups/day was associated with lower mortality, while >5 cups/day was not significant in the overall analysis[^1][^3][^4]. This is not a prescription to increase intake to 3–4 cups.
- **Caffeinated and decaffeinated**: Both were associated with lower all-cause mortality in the three cohorts, so the observed association cannot be attributed to caffeine alone; other compounds and lifestyle confounding remain possible[^4].
- **Smoking confounding**: Coffee intake is strongly related to smoking. Stratifying by smoking changed the curve and magnitude; among never-smokers, RR per additional cup/day was 0.94 (95% CI 0.93–0.96)[^3]. Detailed adjustment still cannot eliminate residual confounding or reverse causation from illness-related coffee cessation.
- **All-cause versus cause-specific mortality**: Cohorts more consistently show inverse associations for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality; total cancer mortality is generally not significantly associated. Three US cohorts also observed lower cardiovascular, neurologic, and suicide mortality, but cause-specific event counts were smaller and no single cause proves a longevity mechanism[^1][^4].
- **Absolute risk**: These RRs and HRs are relative effects. The 2025 cohort included 46,222 adults and 7,074 deaths but did not provide dose-specific standardized absolute risk differences applicable to an average person[^11]. Individual absolute differences depend on age, smoking, comorbidity, and follow-up; “deaths prevented per 100 people” cannot be calculated from these estimates.
- **Brewing method**: In a Norwegian cohort of 508,747 people, filtered coffee versus none had an all-cause mortality HR of 0.85 in both men and women; associations for unfiltered coffee were weaker, but this does not prove that filtering lowers mortality[^5]. A meta-analysis of randomized trials found that unfiltered coffee raises total and LDL cholesterol, whereas filtered coffee had much smaller effects[^6]. French press, boiled, and Turkish coffee are relevant unfiltered preparations.
- **Causality and counterevidence**: A Danish analysis observed the same U-shaped pattern, but Mendelian randomization using caffeine-intake genetic variants did not support causal effects on all-cause mortality or cardiovascular disease[^7]. Genetic instruments represent lifelong propensity and have their own limitations, so they do not independently disprove the cohorts; together, the evidence means “consistent association, causality unproven.”

## What to do

1. If you already drink coffee and tolerate it, continuing a moderate amount is reasonable. Do not start drinking it for “longevity,” and do not chase the minimum point of an observational curve.
2. Track actual volume and caffeine, not just “cups.” An 8-fluid-ounce study cup is about 240 mL; café sizes, espresso, and cold brew can differ greatly in caffeine.
3. Limit added sugar and high-saturated-fat ingredients. In the 2025 cohort, inverse associations were mainly seen for black coffee or coffee low in sugar and saturated fat, but this relied on one or two 24-hour recalls and does not prove that additives cancel a coffee effect[^11].
4. If LDL cholesterol is high or intake is large, prefer paper-filtered coffee and reduce unfiltered coffee. Do not interpret the filtered-coffee mortality cohort as proof that paper filtering extends life[^5][^6].
5. Caffeine can impair sleep, with large individual differences in metabolism. In a randomized crossover trial, participants slept 36 minutes less on caffeinated-coffee days; set a personal cutoff based on sleep response rather than a universal “3 PM” rule[^8].

## Special populations and risks

- **Pregnancy**: Current ACOG patient guidance recommends keeping caffeine from all sources below 200 mg/day. This cannot be reliably converted to “1–2 cups” because brands and volumes vary widely[^10].
- **Arrhythmia or palpitations**: A short randomized trial found no significant rise in premature atrial contractions, but premature ventricular contractions increased from 102 to 154 per day (rate ratio 1.51, 95% CI 1.18–1.94)[^8]. In the DECAF trial of 200 previous/current coffee drinkers after cardioversion for AF/flutter, recurrence at 6 months was lower with about 1 cup/day (47% vs 64%; HR 0.61), but this cannot be generalized to never-drinkers, high doses, or every arrhythmia[^9]. Follow personal symptoms and clinical advice.
- **High cholesterol**: Unfiltered coffee can raise total and LDL cholesterol; brewing method matters independently of caffeine content[^6].
- **Anxiety, reflux, or insomnia**: If symptoms repeatedly follow coffee, reduce intake, switch to decaf, or stop. Do not use coffee to replace sleep.

## Medical red flags

- Palpitations with chest pain, fainting, marked shortness of breath, or a sustained abnormal rhythm
- Severe insomnia, anxiety, or reflux that persists after reducing intake

## Evidence limitations and funding

Mortality evidence relies on self-reported intake, heterogeneous cup sizes, coffee types, and brewing methods, with no long-term mortality RCT. The 2017 umbrella review rated about 25% of outcome evidence low quality and 75% very low quality; 20% of meta-analyses that could be tested had signals of publication bias, while many others were too small to test[^2].

The core umbrella review reported no specific funding. It found two meta-analyses with coffee-industry links, neither selected as the representative result for its outcome, and did not audit funding of every primary study[^2]. The three-cohort study was NIH-funded, but one author disclosed research funding from Nestec S.A.[^4]. The 2025 NHANES cohort was NIH-funded and reported no coffee-industry conflict[^11]. Disclosures do not remove observational confounding and do not by themselves invalidate a result.
