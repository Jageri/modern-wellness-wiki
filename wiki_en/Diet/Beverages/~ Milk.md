# Milk

**Mortality impact**: Uncertain; 200 g/day of milk was not significantly associated with all-cause mortality (HR 0.98, 95% CI 0.93–1.02; very-low-certainty evidence)
**Evidence level**: ⭐⭐ (Pooled observational cohorts with substantial heterogeneity by region, sex, product, and dose)
**Actionability**: Moderate

## Conclusion

There is no reliable evidence that drinking milk itself extends life. A 2026 dose-response meta-analysis included 29 prospective cohorts and 1.68 million people; milk at 200 g/day was not significantly associated with all-cause mortality. Milk, total dairy, yogurt, cheese, and products with different fat contents are not interchangeable exposures. The mortality evidence cited here is observational and does not establish causality.

## Evidence

- **Latest milk synthesis**: Across 11 studies, the highest versus lowest milk intake was not associated with all-cause mortality (HR 1.02, 0.90–1.16; I²=96.3%). After excluding four sex-stratified studies, the estimate was 0.97 (0.91–1.03). At 200 g/day, the HR was 0.98 (0.93–1.02), graded very low certainty. These milk findings do not support a numeric filename prefix.[^1]
- **Earlier umbrella review**: Across eight meta-analyses available through 2018, RRs for milk ranged from 0.99 to 1.01 per 200–244 g/day; total dairy ranged from 0.96 to 1.01 per 200 g/day. The overall association was neutral.[^2]
- **PURE's 17% estimate is not a milk effect**: In 136,384 adults aged 35–70 across 21 countries, **total dairy >2 servings/day versus none** was associated with all-cause mortality HR 0.83 (0.72–0.96). The milk-specific result was >1 serving/day versus none for the composite of death or major cardiovascular events (HR 0.90, 0.82–0.99), not all-cause mortality from milk. Cardiovascular mortality HR 0.77 (0.58–1.01) included the null.[^3]
- **Counterevidence and regional variation**: In two Swedish cohorts, each additional glass of milk was associated with all-cause mortality HR 1.15 (1.13–1.17) in women and 1.03 (1.01–1.04) in men; women drinking ≥3 glasses/day versus <1 had HR 1.93 (1.80–2.06). The authors cautioned about residual confounding and reverse causation.[^4] In three U.S. cohorts, about 4.2 versus 0.8 total-dairy servings/day was associated with all-cause mortality HR 1.07 (1.04–1.10); each additional 0.5 serving/day of whole milk had HR 1.11 (1.09–1.14). Results also depended on the replacement food.[^5]
- **Do not pool whole and low-fat milk**: A cohort meta-analysis through 2020 associated high-fat milk with higher all-cause mortality (pooled effect 1.15, 1.09–1.20). A 2025 Norwegian cohort of 73,860 people followed for a median 33 years reported all-cause mortality HR 1.22 (1.16–1.28) for the highest versus lowest total-milk intake, 1.15 (1.10–1.21) for high whole-milk intake versus none, and 0.89 (0.87–0.92) for low-fat versus whole milk. However, diet was measured in 1974–1988 in a high-milk-intake population, and baseline-only analyses attenuated the associations; this does not show that switching to low-fat milk will necessarily lower an individual's mortality risk.[^6][^7]
- **Fermented dairy is not plain milk**: In the 2026 analysis, yogurt at 200 g/day was associated with all-cause mortality HR 0.89 (0.83–0.96; moderate certainty), whereas cheese at 15 g/day had HR 0.99 (0.97–1.00; very low certainty). A later 2026 cohort meta-analysis of fermented foods also associated higher intakes of fermented milks (including yogurt) and cheese with lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. These remain observational associations: they neither prove that fermented dairy extends life nor constitute a milk effect.[^1][^8]

## How to interpret this evidence

- Cohorts span Europe, North America, Asia, and multiple countries. Background diets, milk fat content, fortification, and replacement foods differ, so no single estimate can be assumed to apply everywhere.
- Food-frequency questionnaires and self-reported diets misclassify exposure. Health awareness, income, smoking, overall diet, and existing illness can produce residual confounding and reverse causation. No long-term randomized trial with mortality as an endpoint shows that milk extends life.
- Reported HRs are relative measures. Comparable adjusted absolute death risks by intake group were not provided, so a personal absolute risk difference or NNT cannot be calculated reliably.
- The 2026 review found no statistically significant publication bias for total dairy, cheese, or yogurt analyses, but did not report a formal publication-bias test for milk. “Not detected” does not mean publication bias was excluded.[^1]
- The 2026 review was supported by Chinese public-institution programs and declared no commercial or financial conflicts. The 2019 umbrella review noted that none of its included meta-analyses reported funding of their underlying studies, so dairy-industry influence on the evidence base cannot be excluded. PURE had broad funding, including pharmaceutical companies, Brazil's Unilever Health Institute, and the South African Sugar Association; its article did not list the Dairy Farmers of Canada, stated that funders had no role, and declared no competing interests. The former entry's dairy-funding claim should not be retained.[^1][^2][^3]

## Do

1. Decide whether to drink milk based on nutritional needs, overall diet, tolerance, and preference—not a claimed “17% longevity benefit.”
2. Lactose intolerance is not milk-protein allergy. People with lactose intolerance may try small amounts with meals, lactose-free milk, yogurt, or hard cheese; complete dairy avoidance is not automatically necessary.[^9]
3. People with milk-protein allergy should avoid milk-protein products. Wheezing, swelling of the face/lips/tongue, breathing difficulty, or suspected anaphylaxis requires use of the person's established emergency plan and urgent medical care.[^10]
4. Plant-based beverages were not the exposure in these milk/dairy mortality studies. Compare protein, calcium, vitamin D, added sugar, and saturated fat; products vary widely, and fortified unsweetened soy beverage is generally nutritionally closest to milk.[^11]
5. Infants and young children, pregnant people, and people with chronic kidney disease or strict potassium, phosphorus, or protein limits need population-specific professional advice rather than extrapolation from adult cohorts.
