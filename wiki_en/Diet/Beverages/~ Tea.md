# Tea

**Mortality Impact**: Multiple cohort studies associate tea drinking with lower all-cause mortality, but the magnitude is inconsistent and causality is unproven
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (Large prospective cohorts and meta-analysis; all mortality evidence is observational)
**Actionability**: Medium (Tea can be an unsweetened everyday beverage, but there is no proven “longevity dose”)

## Conclusion

If you already enjoy tea, moderate unsweetened consumption can be part of a healthy dietary pattern. Current evidence does not prove that starting tea or increasing the number of cups extends life. Green and black tea have both been studied, but differences in tea type, serving size, and brewing strength prevent treating their effects as identical or recommending one type for longevity.

## Evidence

- A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis included 38 prospective cohort data sets from 27 papers, totaling 1,956,549 participants. The effect estimate for all-cause mortality was 0.90 (95% CI 0.86–0.95) for the highest versus lowest intake; in a nonlinear model, about 2 cups/day corresponded to 0.91 (0.88–0.94), with no further reduction at higher intake. Heterogeneity was high (I²=81.6%); most cohorts did not adequately report tea type, and cup size, composition, and adjustment factors differed.[PMID: 38938012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38938012/)
- In the Shanghai Men's and Women's Health prospective cohorts, the overall HR for green tea and all-cause mortality was 0.95 (0.90–1.01). Because the CI crosses 1, this overall result was not statistically significant. The never-smoker subgroup was 0.89 (0.82–0.96), highlighting the possible influence of smoking and residual confounding.[PMID: 28135196](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28135196/)
- China-PAR included 100,902 Chinese adults. Habitual tea drinkers had an all-cause mortality HR of 0.85 (0.79–0.90) versus never or non-habitual drinkers. This was a questionnaire-based prospective cohort association, not a randomized trial.[PMID: 31914807](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31914807/)
- UK Biobank included 498,043 adults aged 40–69 in a predominantly black-tea-drinking population. Compared with no tea, 2–3 cups/day was associated with an all-cause mortality HR of 0.87 (0.84–0.91). Intake was self-reported, and serving size and tea strength were not assessed.[PMID: 36037472](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36037472/)
- All mortality evidence above is observational. Healthy-user bias, beverage substitution, smoking, socioeconomic factors, and reverse causation cannot be fully excluded. These studies support an association; they do not prove that tea itself lowers mortality.

## Practical Guidance

1. Do not force yourself to drink tea or chase a high cup count for “longevity.” A “cup” and brewing strength were not standardized across studies.
2. If caffeinated tea impairs sleep or triggers palpitations, move it earlier, reduce the amount, or choose a lower-caffeine/caffeine-free drink. A caffeine meta-analysis found shorter sleep on average, but doses were not defined as a standard cup of tea, so a universal fixed cutoff before bedtime is not justified.[PMID: 36870101](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36870101/)
3. There is no need for everyone to wait exactly 1 hour after every meal. A controlled trial in only 12 young, iron-replete women found that tea taken with a non-heme-iron meal reduced absorption from that meal and that a 1-hour interval attenuated the effect. This was an acute absorption study, not evidence that long-term tea drinking inevitably causes anemia.[PMID: 29046302](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29046302/)
4. People with iron deficiency, iron-deficiency anemia, or oral iron treatment should pay closer attention to timing. Do not swallow iron tablets with tea; follow the product label or clinician/pharmacist advice. NHS patient guidance for ferrous sulfate recommends a 2-hour gap from tea or coffee.[NHS: About ferrous sulfate](https://www.nhs.uk/medicines/ferrous-sulfate/about-ferrous-sulfate/)
5. “Never take any medicine with tea” is too broad. A clinical review found drug-specific pharmacokinetic changes with some green-tea/catechin exposures, but effects differ among medicines and between brewed tea and concentrated extracts. People taking nadolol, atorvastatin, raloxifene, or narrow-therapeutic-index medicines—and anyone using concentrated green tea extracts—should check with a clinician or pharmacist.[PMID: 39748104](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39748104/); [NCCIH: Green Tea—Usefulness and Safety](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/green-tea)
6. Reliable evidence does not support a blanket ban on tea on an empty stomach. If it reproducibly causes nausea, stomach discomfort, or palpitations, take it with/after food, brew it weaker, or stop. Persistent symptoms deserve medical assessment rather than automatically attributing them to tea.

## When to Seek Medical Advice

- Persistent or marked palpitations, chest pain, fainting, or recurrent symptoms after tea or other caffeine-containing products.
- Fatigue, breathlessness, dizziness, or pallor that could indicate anemia should be medically evaluated rather than diagnosed by stopping tea alone.
- If efficacy changes or adverse effects appear after starting heavy tea intake or concentrated green tea extract while taking medication, contact a clinician or pharmacist; do not stop or change the prescribed dose yourself.
