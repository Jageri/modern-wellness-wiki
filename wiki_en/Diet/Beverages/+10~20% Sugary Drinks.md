# Sugary Drinks

**Mortality Impact**: Higher intake is associated with about +10–20% all-cause mortality (observational evidence, not a causal effect)
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Large prospective cohorts and a meta-analysis of cohorts)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion
Drink fewer or no sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs), and preferentially replace them with water or unsweetened tea or coffee. The number in the title is a relative hazard difference for specific intake groups versus near-nonconsumers, not a guaranteed increase in an individual's absolute risk.

## Exposure Definition
- Here, an SSB means a cola, other carbonated drink, fruit drink, lemonade, sports drink, or similar beverage containing added caloric sweeteners such as sucrose, high-fructose corn syrup, or fruit-juice concentrate.
- In the main US cohorts, one serving was a standard glass, bottle, or can; the paper describes a typical soda serving as 12 fluid ounces (about 355 mL). The meta-analysis standardized one serving to 355 mL.
- The estimates below exclude 100% fruit juice and artificially sweetened beverages (ASBs). Evidence for those exposures must be interpreted separately.

## Evidence
- The US Nurses' Health Study and Health Professionals Follow-Up Study included 118,363 adults free of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, or cancer at baseline and followed them for 28–34 years. Compared with less than one serving per month, the all-cause mortality HR was 1.14 (95% CI 1.09–1.19) for 1 to less than 2 servings/day and 1.21 (1.13–1.28) for at least 2 servings/day; each additional daily serving was associated with HR 1.07 (1.05–1.09). This is the basis for the title's "+10–20%," but it is a relative association and may still contain residual confounding. [Malik et al., 2019, PMID: 30882235](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30882235/)
- In the same study, at least 2 servings/day was associated with CVD mortality HR 1.31 (1.15–1.50) and cancer mortality HR 1.16 (1.04–1.29). In the European EPIC cohort, however, at least 2 glasses/day (about 250 mL/glass) versus less than 1 glass/month had an all-cause mortality HR of only 1.08 (1.01–1.16), and total cancer mortality was not higher. Effect size and cause-specific findings were therefore not fully consistent. [Mullee et al., 2019, PMID: 31479109](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31479109/)
- A 2021 meta-analysis included 11 cohorts, 965,851 participants, and 114,935 deaths in its all-cause analysis. Each additional 355 mL/day of SSB was associated with all-cause mortality HR 1.08 (1.04–1.12) and CVD mortality HR 1.08 (1.04–1.12); cancer mortality was not significantly associated. All-cause heterogeneity was high (I²=70.5%); associations were not statistically significant in Asian, female, and generally low-intake subgroups, and tests suggested a small possibility of publication bias. [Zhang et al., 2021, PMID: 33786594](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33786594/)

## Boundaries and Uncertainty
- **Causality**: These numbers come from observational cohorts, not long-term randomized trials. High-intake participants can differ in smoking, exercise, overall diet, and socioeconomic characteristics. Adjustment cannot eliminate residual confounding, so the evidence does not show that each serving inevitably causes a fixed increase in mortality.
- **Absolute risk**: The main papers report adjusted HRs, not intake-group-specific adjusted absolute risks, absolute risk differences, or numbers needed to harm that can be applied to an individual. The US cohorts recorded 36,436 deaths during 3,415,564 person-years overall, but that crude overall rate cannot be converted into an exposure-group absolute excess risk.
- **Fruit juice**: 100% juice is outside the SSB definition above. A UK Biobank cohort of 198,285 participants did not find a robust association between 100% fruit/vegetable juice and higher all-cause mortality, but the finding was sensitive to diet-quality adjustment, residual confounding, and reverse causation. This neither justifies treating juice as an SSB nor establishes that juice extends life. [Anderson et al., 2020, PMID: 32326961](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32326961/)
- **Artificially sweetened beverages**: ASBs are not the exposure in this entry. Their observational mortality associations may be affected by reverse causation from weight loss, obesity, or pre-existing illness. They should not be promoted as unlimited "healthy drinks," and their estimates should not be merged with SSB estimates.
- **Substitution**: In Malik's statistical substitution model, replacing 1 serving/day of SSB with 1 serving/day of ASB was associated with all-cause mortality HR 0.96 (0.94–0.98). This is a modeled relative association, not a randomized intervention effect. When practical, water or an unsweetened beverage is the more conservative routine replacement.
- **Population**: The title estimate comes mainly from US nurses and male health professionals, predominantly non-Hispanic White and free of major chronic disease at baseline; the European estimate was smaller. Do not directly apply 14–21% to children, Asian populations, people with chronic disease, or low-intake populations.
- **Measurement error**: Beverage intake came from self-reported food-frequency questionnaires or 24-hour dietary recalls and is vulnerable to underreporting, misclassification, and change over time. Such error can dilute an association or bias it when correlated with other behaviors.

## Do
1. First reduce daily or several-times-weekly intake; substitute water, sparkling water, unsweetened tea, or coffee without added sugar.
2. Read ingredient lists: sucrose, glucose-fructose syrup, high-fructose corn syrup, and fruit-juice concentrate can all be added-sugar sources.
3. If a short transition is needed to reduce added sugar, an ASB can be considered; this does not establish a mortality benefit, and water or unsweetened drinks remain the preferred long-term target.

## Don't
1. Do not interpret HR 1.14 as "14 percentage points more deaths," or write an observational association as certain causation.
2. Do not transfer evidence between SSBs, 100% juice, and ASBs.
3. Do not overlook added sugar in sports drinks, fruit drinks, flavored milk, and sweetened coffee.

## Medical Red Flags
- Recurrent thirst, frequent urination, or unexplained weight change warrants medical assessment for abnormal glucose.
- If you have diabetes, gout, chronic kidney disease, or cardiovascular disease, tailor beverage substitutions to your condition and clinical advice.
