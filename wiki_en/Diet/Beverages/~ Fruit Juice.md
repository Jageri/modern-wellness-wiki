# Fruit Juice

**Mortality impact**: Observational evidence on 100% fruit juice and all-cause mortality is inconsistent; there is no reliable single percentage
**Evidence level**: ⭐⭐ (conflicting cohort studies and meta-analyses; no long-term randomized mortality trial)
**Actionability**: Moderate (prefer whole fruit and do not use juice as the default hydration drink)

## Conclusion

One hundred percent fruit juice is not equivalent to whole fruit, but it should not be conflated with sugar-added fruit drinks or other sugar-sweetened beverages. All long-term mortality evidence is observational. Some cohorts associate high intake with higher mortality, while pooled estimates have found no significant association. The "+24%" estimate therefore is not a general or causal effect of fruit juice, and the evidence does not establish that fruit juice is "more dangerous than sugary drinks."

## Evidence

- A 2022 mortality systematic review included 13 cohorts and 1,539,127 participants, but judged the associations of **100% fruit juice** with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality uncertain. In its fruit-juice analyses, the highest-versus-lowest all-cause mortality HR was 1.00 (95% CI 0.78–1.29; 3 cohorts; I²=83%), and the dose-response HR was 0.88 (0.77–1.01; 2 cohorts). Neither supports a fixed harmful percentage. [PMID: 34121531](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34121531/)
- The old title's **+24%** came from a secondary REGARDS analysis: 13,440 Black and White US adults aged 45 years or older, free of coronary heart disease, stroke, and diabetes at baseline, were followed for a mean of 6.0 years, during which 1,000 died. Each additional 12 oz (about 355 mL) of fruit juice per day was associated with all-cause mortality HR 1.24 (1.09–1.42). The HR for sugar-sweetened beverages in the same study was 1.06 (0.96–1.16), not 1.11 as the old entry stated; 1.11 applied to the combined "sugary beverage" exposure. This self-reported, linear-model estimate is not trial evidence that one glass causes 24% more deaths. [PMID: 31099861](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31099861/)
- An NHANES cohort included 40,074 US adults, with 4,904 deaths over a median 7.8 years. Compared with non-consumers, participants consuming at least 250 g/day of 100% fruit juice had all-cause mortality HR 1.30 (1.11–1.52) and heart-disease mortality HR 1.49 (1.01–2.21); however, the CIs crossed 1 for total cardiovascular mortality, HR 1.38 (0.93–2.06), and cerebrovascular mortality, HR 0.89 (0.30–2.62). In a modeled isocaloric replacement of 5% of energy from whole fruit with juice, the all-cause mortality HR was 1.09 (1.02–1.16). Juice was measured mainly by baseline 24-hour recalls, sugar-added fruit drinks could have been misclassified as 100% juice, and residual confounding remains possible. [PMID: 35631268](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35631268/)
- A 2023 meta-analysis of cardiometabolic outcomes reported an all-cause mortality RR of 1.26 (1.14–1.40) for highest versus lowest "fruit juice" intake, but this pooled only 2 studies, and the authors explicitly noted that fruit-juice definitions varied across studies. It supports caution about high intake but cannot supply a stable title number specific to 100% juice. [PMID: 37006931](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37006931/)
- Surrogate and disease outcomes must not be presented as mortality outcomes. In NutriNet-Santé, each additional 100 mL/day of 100% fruit juice was associated with overall cancer **incidence** HR 1.12 (1.03–1.23); this was not an all-cause or cancer-mortality effect. [PMID: 31292122](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31292122/)
- Counter-evidence exists. A 2021 review analyzing 21 prospective studies and 35 short-term RCTs found no association of 100% fruit juice with higher overall cardiovascular risk and inverse associations at low-to-moderate intakes (up to about 170 mL/day for total cardiovascular events and 200 mL/day for stroke); it also found no significant association with coronary heart disease or diabetes. Its RCTs mainly assessed surrogate outcomes such as blood pressure and cannot establish longer life. The review received an unrestricted grant from the European Fruit Juice Association, which requires transparent, cautious interpretation alongside its methods. [PMID: 33150530](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33150530/)
- A 2025 umbrella review likewise found no significant association between 100% fruit juice and all-cause mortality in the meta-analyses it summarized, but certainty was low or very low. The observational diabetes estimate it summarized, adjusted for adiposity, was RR 1.07 (1.01–1.14) for highest versus lowest intake; this was incidence, not mortality. The review was funded by Ausveg and Hort Innovation, and all authors worked for or collaborated with FOODiQ Global. That does not automatically invalidate the findings, but it is relevant to appraisal. [PMID: 38679915](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38679915/)

## Definitions and practical choices

1. **Whole fruit** retains more intact fiber and food structure; an equal volume of juice is not nutritionally equivalent to whole fruit.
2. **100% fruit juice** has no added sugar (and may be reconstituted from concentrate). A **fruit drink/fruit-flavored drink** may contain only some juice plus added sugar; check the ingredient list and nutrition label separately.
3. If choosing 100% juice, use a small glass and count it toward daily energy intake. Do not interpret 355 mL in REGARDS or 250 g in NHANES as a safety line or toxicity threshold. Current studies do not establish a universal life-extending or life-shortening dose.
4. Water and unsweetened drinks are better defaults for hydration. Juice is not required and is not a "detox" product.
5. Do not substitute evidence about sugar-sweetened beverages for juice evidence, or extrapolate interchangeably among juice, whole fruit, and vegetable juice. Vegetable juice is not automatically "better."

## Causality, absolute risk, and applicability

- All long-term mortality estimates are HRs or RRs describing relative associations. They are not "+24 percentage points" and do not prove that juice itself caused deaths.
- In REGARDS, 1,000 of 13,440 participants (7.4%) died over about 6 years overall, but the paper did not report a directly usable, dose-standardized absolute risk difference for fruit juice. The overall event proportion cannot be used to calculate an individual's juice-attributable risk.
- REGARDS included only Black and White US adults aged 45 years or older. NHANES was nationally representative of US adults but relied mainly on baseline dietary recalls. Generalization to other countries, children, particular juice types, or clinical populations remains uncertain.
- Healthy-user bias, smoking, income, overall diet, obesity, reverse causation, and self-report error can leave residual confounding. The modeled replacement of whole fruit with juice is a statistical substitution, not a randomized replacement trial.
