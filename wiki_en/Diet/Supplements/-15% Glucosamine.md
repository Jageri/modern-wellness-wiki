# Glucosamine

**Mortality Impact**: Regular use was associated with all-cause mortality HR 0.85 (95% CI 0.82–0.89), an observed difference of about -15%; this is not a randomized-trial mortality benefit [1]
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐ (mortality evidence is observational and vulnerable to important selection bias and residual confounding)
**Actionability**: Low for longevity; osteoarthritis use requires an individualized discussion of symptoms, formulation, and medication risks

## Conclusion

No randomized trial has shown that glucosamine lowers all-cause mortality or extends life. The UK Biobank `-15%` estimate is a statistically significant observational association, not proof that the supplement reduces an individual's mortality risk by 15%; healthy-user characteristics, residual confounding, and collider-stratification selection bias may explain some or all of it [1][2]. Do not take glucosamine for longevity.

The osteoarthritis evidence is also not uniform. Higher-quality trials and guidelines have not established an important, consistent benefit for ordinary over-the-counter glucosamine, and results for different salts, brands, and prescription-grade preparations are not interchangeable [4][5][6].

## Evidence

- **Mortality cohort**: UK Biobank included 495,077 women and men (mean age 56.6 years) followed for a median of 8.9 years; 19.1% reported regular glucosamine use at baseline. Adjusted HRs were 0.85 (95% CI 0.82–0.89) for all-cause mortality, 0.82 (0.74–0.90) for cardiovascular mortality, and 0.94 (0.88–0.99) for cancer mortality [1]. Use was not randomized, and the study could not link a dose, salt, or brand to the mortality result.
- **Bias counterevidence**: A methods analysis of 11 observational studies found a mean all-cause mortality HR of 0.84 (95% CI 0.81–0.87), but noted that all used “prevalent-user” cohorts in which use began before enrollment. The authors illustrated that collider-stratification selection bias could make a true mortality rate ratio of 1.0 appear to be about 0.82; the studies therefore cannot support glucosamine to prevent death [2]. This is more specific than the general label “healthy-user bias” and does not exclude unmeasured lifestyle confounding.
- **Evidence from an improved design**: In a matched prevalent new-user analysis of 20,541 pairs of patients with osteoarthritis, glucosamine was not significantly associated with incidence of any cancer (HR 0.97, 95% CI 0.91–1.02) [3]. This outcome was cancer incidence, not all-cause mortality, so it does not directly refute [1], but it supports caution about protective associations from prevalent-user cohorts.
- **Joint RCT**: The NIH-funded GAIT randomized 1,583 people with knee osteoarthritis. Glucosamine hydrochloride 1,500 mg/day increased the 24-week pain response rate by only 3.9 percentage points versus placebo (P=0.30), with no significant overall benefit. A signal in the moderate-to-severe pain subgroup came from glucosamine plus chondroitin and was exploratory; it cannot be attributed to glucosamine alone [4].
- **Formulation, brand, and conflicts**: A meta-analysis of 25 placebo-controlled trials found high heterogeneity (I²=88%). Low-risk-of-bias trials of one branded product showed only a small effect (SMD -0.27, 95% CI -0.43 to -0.12), while non-brand trials were null overall (SMD -0.11, 95% CI -0.46 to 0.24); brand and risk of bias explained much of the heterogeneity [5]. This does not justify the simple claim that “sulfate works and hydrochloride does not,” and evidence for prescription crystalline sulfate cannot be generalized to every supplement.
- **Guideline**: The 2019 ACR/Arthritis Foundation guideline strongly recommends against glucosamine for knee, hip, or hand osteoarthritis. It cites differences between industry- and publicly funded trials, concern about publication bias, and the absence of important benefit in the lowest-risk-of-bias evidence [6]. NCCIH also notes disagreement among guidelines, so the clinical controversy is not completely resolved [7].
- **Safety boundary**: Large osteoarthritis studies have not identified major safety problems, but “minimal side effects” or unconditional long-term safety is too broad. NCCIH notes possible blood-glucose increases in some people, increased bleeding risk with warfarin, and little pregnancy or breastfeeding safety information [7].

## Do

1. Have persistent joint pain evaluated. Exercise, weight management, and physical therapy have more consistent guideline support; do not use a supplement in place of diagnosis or foundational care [6].
2. If you still consider glucosamine, review the exact salt, formulation, concomitant medicines, and a measurable symptom goal with a clinician or pharmacist. Do not generalize results from one prescription-grade product to every supplement.
3. Consult a clinician before starting or continuing it if you take warfarin, have abnormal glucose regulation, are pregnant, or are breastfeeding [7].
4. If a shared decision is made to try it, agree on a reassessment date in advance and stop if there is no clear, reproducible improvement in pain or function.

## Don't

1. Do not self-prescribe it for “anti-aging” or a claimed 15% mortality reduction; current mortality evidence does not establish causation.
2. Do not extrapolate osteoarthritis symptom trials to cancer prevention, cardiovascular protection, or longevity.
3. Do not treat sulfate, hydrochloride, prescription crystalline products, and combination products as equivalent.
4. Do not use glucosamine to mask new, rapidly worsening, or unexplained joint symptoms.

## When to Seek Care

- Seek prompt care for a suddenly red, hot, swollen, severely painful joint, fever, or inability to bear weight, to exclude infection, gout, or injury.
- While taking warfarin, contact a clinician immediately for unusual bruising, persistent nosebleeds, black stools, blood in urine, or other bleeding; severe or ongoing bleeding needs emergency care.
- Seek emergency care for breathing difficulty, swelling of the face, lips, or tongue, or widespread hives after a dose.
