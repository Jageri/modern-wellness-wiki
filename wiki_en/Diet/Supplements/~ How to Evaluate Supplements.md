# How to Evaluate Supplements

**Mortality Impact**: No direct all-cause mortality evidence; the goal is to reduce ineffective spending, duplicate dosing, interactions, and delayed appropriate treatment
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (decision method; regulatory facts are high-confidence, but no single rule can universally establish authenticity or efficacy)
**Actionability**: High
**Regulatory information checked**: 2026-09-06 (mainland China focus; cross-border and U.S. rules are identified separately)

## Conclusion
Do not judge a supplement from a “Blue Hat” logo, foreign origin, a “natural” claim, pharmacy availability, or a testing seal alone. A practical sequence is: **verify that the product identity matches the official record, check whether advertising exceeds the authorized claim, then decide whether the evidence, dose, and risks fit you**. Registration or filing in China indicates a regulatory pathway; it does not prove that a product treats disease, extends life, or benefits every individual [1-4].

## Six Steps Before Buying

### 1. Define the purpose
- Write down a checkable outcome: correction of a documented deficiency, relief of a symptom, change in a laboratory marker, or reduction in a clinical event such as fracture or hospitalization.
- Stop if the pitch promises to “cure,” “reverse,” replace antihypertensive/diabetes/cancer treatment, or says everyone needs the product. Health foods in China may not claim to prevent or treat disease [1,2].

### 2. Verify product identity in mainland China
- A product sold through ordinary domestic channels as a health food should carry the “Blue Hat” mark and a registration or filing number. Do not trust the image alone: use SAMR's [Special Food Information Query](https://ypzsx.gsxt.gov.cn/specialfood/#/food) and compare the product name, manufacturer/filer, health function, target population, and excluded population. A screenshot, application number, or “accepted for review” message is not a database match [2,5].
- Current common formats are: domestic registration `国食健注G + four-digit year + four-digit serial`; imported registration `国食健注J + four-digit year + four-digit serial`; domestic filing `食健备G + four-digit year + two-digit provincial code + six-digit serial`; and imported filing `食健备J + four-digit year + 00 + six-digit serial` [2]. Some lawfully renewed legacy products may retain older approval numbers, so formats are not an authenticity test; the official record controls.
- Ordinary food, health food, and medicines are different categories. Lack of a Blue Hat does not make ordinary food counterfeit, but ordinary food may not claim a health function or disease treatment. A food-production licence (“SC”), business licence, patent, or trademark is not health-food approval.

### 3. Match the authorized/recorded claim to the sales pitch
- **Registration** involves systematic regulatory evaluation and review of submitted materials; **filing** means submitting required materials for retention, publication, and reference. Products using ingredients outside the health-food ingredient catalogue and most first-time imported health foods require registration. Catalogue-based products, and first-time imported vitamin/mineral products using catalogue nutrients, use filing [2].
- Claims on the package, product page, livestream, and from sales staff should not exceed the official record. China's permitted functions are catalogue-based and can change. A Blue Hat does not legitimize claims such as treating cancer, lowering glucose or blood pressure, repairing the liver or kidneys, reversing aging, or guaranteeing results [1,3,4].
- Registration materials address safety, claimed health function, and quality control, but an authorized function is not a prescription or proof of benefit for you. Separately look for human evidence matching the **same formulation, dose, population, and outcome**. Filing, animal or mechanism research, patents, and isolated biomarkers are not proof of clinical efficacy.

### 4. Read the full label, not just front-panel marketing
- Compare the official record with the ingredients and excipients, functional/marker ingredient amounts, health function, suitable and unsuitable populations, daily amount and directions, package size, storage, shelf life, and precautions [2].
- The current warning states: “Health food is not medicine and cannot replace medicine for treating disease”; SAMR guidance also requires a prominent warning area [3]. Do not buy if the production date is absent, the product is expired, the seal is broken, or information conflicts.
- Calculate the **total daily dose**, not merely the amount per capsule. Add duplicate ingredients from multivitamins, fortified foods, and other supplements.

### 5. Interpret imports and third-party testing correctly
- Imported health foods distributed through ordinary trade in mainland China also follow Chinese registration/filing rules and should have Chinese labeling and importer information. Goods purchased directly from abroad for personal use through cross-border e-commerce may follow origin-country standards and differ from Chinese standards. “Overseas version” or “original import” alone proves neither quality, authenticity, nor efficacy [5].
- In the United States, FDA generally does not approve dietary supplements for safety, effectiveness, or labeling before marketing, and it does not test every product before sale. “FDA registered” must not be interpreted as “FDA approved” [6].
- Seals from independent quality programs such as USP or NSF may help assess manufacturing, labeled contents, or contaminant control. Verify the exact product and certification scope in the certifier's own directory. Such seals **do not guarantee safety or effectiveness** and do not replace any registration or filing required in China [7].

### 6. Run a personal risk check
- Make one list containing every product, ingredient, daily dose, and reason for use, and ask a clinician or pharmacist to review it. This matters especially with prescription medicines, multiple supplements, pregnancy or breastfeeding, children or frail older adults, liver or kidney disease, bleeding risk, or planned surgery [6,7].
- Interactions are concrete, not theoretical. Vitamin K can reduce warfarin's anticoagulant effect, and St. John's wort can accelerate the metabolism of many medicines. High doses, stacking products, and replacing prescribed treatment increase adverse-event risk [7].
- Consider buying only if identity is verifiable, claims stay within bounds, labeling is complete, the goal is defined, evidence fits you, and risk is acceptable. If any critical item remains unresolved, defer the purchase.

## If Something Goes Wrong
- Stop use and seek emergency help for breathing difficulty, swelling of the face/lips/tongue, fainting, chest pain, stroke-like symptoms, or major bleeding. Seek prompt care for persistent severe vomiting/diarrhea, dark urine, reduced urination, or jaundice [8].
- Keep the package, lot number, ingredient panel, dose, start/stop dates, symptom timeline, order, and receipt. Address the medical problem first; do not wait for the seller.
- In China, counterfeit goods, labeling discrepancies, exaggerated claims, and consumer disputes can be reported through the national 12315 platform or local 12315 hotline [9]. In the United States, consumers and clinicians can submit suspected supplement adverse events to the FDA Safety Reporting Portal even when causation is uncertain [8].

## Don't
1. Do not infer “clinically proven efficacy” from a genuine Blue Hat.
2. Do not infer “risk-free” or “treats disease” from a test report or certification seal.
3. Do not use package color, barcode prefixes, dissolution, burning, or sediment “tricks” to judge authenticity or quality.
4. Do not lower the evidence standard for “all natural,” traditional-secret-formula, expert endorsement, testimonials, or limited-time-free pitches.
5. Do not keep testing a product after symptoms appear or stop prescribed treatment to replace it with a supplement.
