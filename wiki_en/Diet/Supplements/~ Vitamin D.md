# Vitamin D Supplementation

**Mortality Impact**: A broad adult meta-analysis of randomized trials found no reduction in all-cause mortality (RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.95-1.02); populations and regimens differ, and a conditional guideline recommendation remains for adults aged 75 years or older
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Meta-analysis of randomized trials, multiple large randomized trials, and an evidence-based guideline)
**Actionability**: Medium

## Conclusion
For most healthy adults not selected for vitamin D deficiency or bone disease, empiric supplementation above routine nutritional needs has not been shown to reduce all-cause mortality, cancer incidence, major cardiovascular events, or fractures. This is not evidence that documented deficiency should go untreated: rickets or osteomalacia, established deficiency, comprehensive osteoporosis treatment, and disorders affecting absorption or metabolism require separate clinical assessment.

## Evidence
- A 2019 systematic review included 52 randomized trials and 75,454 participants: all-cause mortality RR 0.98 (95% CI 0.95-1.02) and cardiovascular mortality RR 0.98 (0.88-1.08). Cancer mortality produced a secondary signal (RR 0.84, 0.74-0.95). The interaction between D3 and D2 subgroups differed, but neither subgroup by itself established lower all-cause mortality [1]
- D-Health randomized 21,315 Australians aged 60 years or older to D3 60,000 IU monthly or placebo. After 5 years of intervention, all-cause mortality was not lower (HR 1.04, 95% CI 0.93-1.18), nor was cancer mortality (HR 1.15, 0.96-1.39). This direction conflicts with the earlier cancer-mortality signal and cannot be generalized to daily low-dose regimens [2]
- In VITAL, 25,871 middle-aged and older US adults received D3 2,000 IU/day. Over a median 5.3 years, supplementation did not reduce invasive cancer (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.88-1.06) or major cardiovascular events (HR 0.97, 0.85-1.12). All-cause mortality was HR 0.99 (0.87-1.12), and cancer mortality was HR 0.83 (0.67-1.02), with the latter confidence interval including 1 [3]
- Participants in the VITAL fracture ancillary study were not selected for deficiency, low bone mass, or osteoporosis. D3 2,000 IU/day did not reduce total fractures (HR 0.98, 95% CI 0.89-1.08), nonvertebral fractures, or hip fractures [4]
- Intermittent megadosing is not simply an equally effective convenience. In 2,256 women aged 70 years or older at high fracture risk, annual D3 500,000 IU increased falls (incidence rate ratio 1.15, 95% CI 1.02-1.30) and fractures (1.26, 1.00-1.59). These harm estimates apply to this extreme annual regimen, not to every vitamin D dose [5]
- The 2024 Endocrine Society guideline suggests that healthy adults younger than 75 should not routinely exceed dietary reference intakes for disease prevention and should not undergo routine 25(OH)D screening. For adults aged 75 years or older, it conditionally suggests empiric supplementation because of a potential mortality benefit, but the optimal dose is unclear. When supplementation is indicated in adults aged 50 or older, daily lower-dose treatment is preferred over intermittent high doses [6]

### ⚠️ Limitations
- A null result in broadly screened adults does not mean documented deficiency should go untreated; severely deficient participants were uncommon in major prevention trials
- Baseline 25(OH)D, age, calcium co-administration, daily versus intermittent dosing, and follow-up differed; estimates from one regimen should not be transferred to another
- Observational associations between low 25(OH)D and disease can be confounded by illness, frailty, obesity, and reduced outdoor activity and do not by themselves establish causation
- The 2024 guideline did not establish one "optimal" 25(OH)D threshold for all disease-prevention outcomes in healthy people; people with established clinical indications are outside its prevention scope

## Do
1. Distinguish routine nutrition from evaluation for rickets or osteomalacia, established deficiency, osteoporosis, malabsorption, or medication effects
2. Follow local dietary reference intakes for food or routine supplementation; do not treat intake above those levels as a cancer-prevention or longevity regimen [6][7]
3. Adults aged 75 years or older can discuss empiric supplementation with a health professional; when supplementation is needed, prefer a daily, lower-dose regimen over self-directed intermittent megadoses [6]
4. If deficiency is diagnosed or bone disease is being treated, follow the clinical plan for formulation, dose, duration, and monitoring
5. Check labels on vitamin D, calcium, and multivitamin products and report the total intake to a clinician or pharmacist

## Don't
1. Don't market a secondary, inconsistent cancer-mortality signal as proof of cancer prevention or longer life
2. Don't assign every healthy person a universal "optimal blood level" or repeatedly screen without an indication [6]
3. Don't chronically exceed the total tolerable upper intake level without supervision. The US NIH lists 4,000 IU/day for people aged 9 years or older; this is a risk-management ceiling, not proof that every lower dose is beneficial, and clinicians may use higher doses temporarily to treat deficiency [7]
4. Don't use supplements instead of proper evaluation and treatment for osteoporosis, malabsorption, chronic kidney disease, or parathyroid disease

## Medical Red Flags
- Recurrent low-trauma fractures, substantial bone pain, proximal muscle weakness, or abnormal growth or bone development in a child warrant assessment
- Persistent nausea or vomiting, marked thirst and urination, weakness, or altered consciousness during supplementation requires stopping the product and prompt evaluation for hypercalcemia [7]
- Ask a clinician before supplementing if you have kidney stones, chronic kidney disease, parathyroid disease, granulomatous disease, or take medicines affecting calcium and phosphate metabolism
