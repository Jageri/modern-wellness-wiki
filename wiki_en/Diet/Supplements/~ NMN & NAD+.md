# NMN and NAD+

**Mortality impact**: Unknown; no human trial has shown that NMN extends lifespan or reduces all-cause mortality
**Evidence level**: ⭐⭐ (Raises blood NAD+, but clinical benefits are inconsistent and long-term outcomes are absent)
**Actionability**: Medium (Commercial availability is not evidence of efficacy)

## Conclusion

NMN is a precursor of NAD+. Short randomized trials fairly consistently show that oral NMN can raise blood NAD+ or related metabolites. That establishes bioavailability; it **does not establish slower aging, disease prevention, or longer life**. As of 2026, human studies remain small and short, with no evidence on lifespan, all-cause mortality, or major cardiovascular events. NMN should not be presented to generally healthy people as a proven “anti-aging” supplement.

## Human evidence

- **The overall clinical evidence is mostly negative**: A 2025 systematic review included 12 randomized trials and 513 participants. NMN increased blood NAD overall, but most clinically relevant outcomes did not differ significantly from control. Seven trials had “some concerns” for risk of bias and the other five were rated high risk.
- **The latest pooled safety and metabolic evidence**: A 2026 systematic review included 15 parallel-group randomized trials using 250–2,000 mg/day for 14 days to 24 weeks. It found no significant effects on body weight, BMI, fasting glucose, HbA1c, lipid profiles, or systolic blood pressure. The small reduction in diastolic blood pressure was exploratory because blood pressure was not the primary outcome in most trials and multiple outcomes were tested.
- **A positive surrogate endpoint is not a longevity outcome**: A 10-week trial in 25 postmenopausal women with prediabetes who were overweight or obese found improved skeletal-muscle insulin sensitivity measured by a hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp and changes in muscle signaling. This was a small, selected population and a short-term surrogate endpoint—not diabetes incidence, mortality, or lifespan. The authors disclosed NMN-related patent/licensing interests.
- **Other short trials have not consistently reproduced functional benefit**: A 28-day trial in 30 adults aged at least 45 years who were overweight or obese used 2,000 mg/day of pharmaceutical-grade NMN. Circulating NAD and related metabolites rose, but muscle strength, fatigability, exercise capacity, insulin sensitivity, and liver and intra-abdominal fat did not improve significantly versus placebo. Changes in weight, diastolic pressure, and some lipids require confirmation in larger and longer trials; several authors were affiliated with the company developing the formulation.

## Safety and regulation

- In the 2026 review, 10 trials contributed safety data. Short-term NMN did not clearly increase overall or serious adverse events, withdrawals due to adverse events, system-specific events, or ALT/AST elevations.
- This supports tolerability only **for up to 24 weeks**. Long-term use, variation in commercial product quality, drug interactions, and safety during pregnancy, breastfeeding, cancer, or severe liver or kidney disease remain inadequately studied. Current data neither show that NMN causes cancer nor establish long-term safety in these groups.
- In September 2025, the U.S. FDA reconsidered its position and concluded that NMN is not excluded from the dietary-supplement definition by the drug-preclusion clause. This is a product-category determination, **not FDA approval of NMN's anti-aging efficacy or safety**. Rules may differ in other jurisdictions.

## What to do

1. If the goal is longer life or lower mortality, do not treat a higher NAD+ level as proof of benefit; there is no human hard-endpoint evidence for NMN.
2. Do not interpret the 250–2,000 mg/day doses used in short trials as a recommended self-treatment range; no optimal dose has been validated against long-term outcomes.
3. If you still plan to use NMN, review medical conditions, medicines, and product quality with a clinician or pharmacist, and track cost and adverse effects. Stop and seek care if symptoms occur.
4. NMN and nicotinamide riboside (NR) are different NAD+ precursors; findings should not be transferred between them without direct evidence.
