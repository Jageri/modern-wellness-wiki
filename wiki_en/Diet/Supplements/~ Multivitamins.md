# Multivitamins

**Mortality Impact**: No proven reduction in all-cause mortality (confidence intervals crossed 1 in the large RCTs)
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Strong evidence for no material mortality/CVD benefit; small cancer, cognitive, and aging-biomarker effects remain uncertain)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion
For generally healthy adults without a known nutrient deficiency, current evidence does not support using a daily multivitamin to extend life or prevent cardiovascular disease. Neither PHS-II nor COSMOS showed a statistically significant reduction in all-cause mortality. A 2024 three-cohort analysis likewise found no lower mortality among daily users, but observational results cannot establish harm or eliminate residual confounding.

The cancer evidence is not simply “no effect.” PHS-II found a small reduction in total cancer incidence among male physicians, and an updated evidence review found a similarly small pooled signal; COSMOS did not replicate the total-cancer result, and there is no demonstrated cancer-mortality or lifespan benefit. COSMOS cognitive substudies found small, short-term improvements in test scores among older adults, but did not show prevention of dementia or longer life. A 2026 COSMOS substudy reported very small changes in two epigenetic clocks; these are surrogate outcomes and do not justify a numeric lifespan filename.

Treating a diagnosed deficiency or meeting a life-stage need such as pregnancy is a different clinical question. Supplement the identified nutrient or use an appropriate prenatal/life-stage formulation rather than extrapolating prevention trials into a recommendation for everyone.

## Evidence
- **PHS-II cancer trial** (14,641 US male physicians aged ≥50 years at entry; median follow-up 11.2 years): total cancer incidence was 17.0 vs 18.3 per 1,000 person-years, HR 0.92 (95% CI 0.86–0.998; P=0.04). This is an approximately 8% relative difference in cancer incidence, not all-cause mortality. Prostate cancer, colorectal cancer, and cancer mortality were not significantly reduced.
- **PHS-II cardiovascular trial** (the same trial population): major cardiovascular events HR 1.01 (95% CI 0.91–1.10; P=0.91) and all-cause mortality HR 0.94 (95% CI 0.88–1.02; P=0.13), both nonsignificant.
- **COSMOS** (21,442 older US adults; women ≥65 and men ≥60; median follow-up 3.6 years): invasive cancer HR 0.97 (95% CI 0.86–1.09; P=0.57), composite CVD HR 0.98 (95% CI 0.86–1.12), and all-cause mortality HR 0.93 (95% CI 0.81–1.08), all nonsignificant. The shorter follow-up cannot exclude smaller or later effects.
- **Updated USPSTF evidence review**: four RCTs yielded an OR of 0.93 (95% CI 0.87–0.99) for any cancer; absolute risk differences in adequately powered trials ranged from -0.2% to -1.2%. The review nevertheless identified important limitations in the multivitamin evidence. The USPSTF therefore retains an “insufficient evidence” conclusion for balancing benefits and harms of multivitamins for CVD or cancer prevention in community-dwelling, nonpregnant adults without known deficiency.
- **2024 three-cohort study** (390,124 US adults without cancer or other chronic disease at baseline; up to 27 years): daily use was associated with all-cause mortality HR 1.04 (95% CI 1.02–1.07) in the first half of follow-up and HR 1.04 (95% CI 0.99–1.08) in the second. Users were more often women and college educated and less often current smokers, illustrating healthy-user differences. Adjustment and time-split analyses reduced bias, but self-reported exposure and residual confounding still limit causal interpretation. The defensible conclusion is “no lower mortality observed,” not “multivitamins cause death.”
- **Cognition and aging biomarkers**: a within-COSMOS meta-analysis of three nonoverlapping cognitive substudies found a 0.07-SD difference in global cognition over 2–3 years (95% CI 0.03–0.11). The effect was small, all studies came from the same parent trial, and dementia prevention was not established. In 2026, a 958-person substudy found differences in annual change for only two of five DNA-methylation clocks (PCGrimAge -0.113 years; PCPhenoAge -0.214 years); clinical meaning is unknown.
- **Conflict-of-interest boundary**: PHS-II and COSMOS both received public funding, while relevant companies supplied trial pills/packaging; some COSMOS investigators also disclosed research funding or lecture/travel support from nutrition and supplement-related companies. Randomization and blinding reduce, but do not erase, concerns about interpretation and generalizability.

## What to do
1. Do not start a multivitamin for “longevity,” and do not use one to replace diet, smoking cessation, exercise, or indicated treatment.
2. If anemia, malabsorption, a strict vegan diet, surgery, or another condition creates deficiency risk, identify the nutrient and cause first; treat a confirmed deficiency with targeted clinical guidance.
3. People planning or capable of pregnancy should follow pregnancy guidance (including folic acid) and use a life-stage-appropriate plan; a generic adult multivitamin is not a substitute for prenatal guidance.
4. If you still choose a basic daily product, add up nutrients across all supplements, medications, and fortified foods. “Multivitamin + single nutrient + fortified drink” can exceed tolerable upper intake levels, especially with high-potency formulas.
5. Smokers and former smokers should avoid products with large amounts of beta-carotene or vitamin A; avoid excess preformed vitamin A in pregnancy; consult a clinician before combining vitamin-K products with warfarin; and avoid high-iron formulas without an indication.
