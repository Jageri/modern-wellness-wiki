# Spermidine

**Mortality Impact**: Unknown; observational findings conflict, and no human randomized trial has used mortality or lifespan as an endpoint
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐ (Contested: some cohorts show an inverse association, while another large cohort did not replicate it)
**Actionability**: Moderate (available in ordinary foods, but no evidence supports extra intake for longevity)

## Conclusion
Spermidine is a polyamine naturally present in the human body and in foods. Animal experiments suggest that it can affect autophagy and extend mouse lifespan, but human evidence does not establish that increasing dietary spermidine or taking a supplement extends life. Existing mortality studies are observational and have produced inconsistent results across populations.

## Evidence
- **Supportive observational evidence**: The Bruneck cohort included 829 White adults aged 45–84 years and recorded 341 deaths over 20 years. Crude mortality rates across low, middle, and high spermidine-intake thirds were 40.5, 23.7, and 15.1 per 1,000 person-years. The more comparable age-, sex-, and energy-adjusted 20-year cumulative mortality estimates were 48%, 41%, and 38% (a 10-percentage-point absolute difference between the highest and lowest groups). The fully adjusted HR per 1-SD higher intake was 0.76 (95% CI 0.67–0.86), not an adjusted “60% reduction” for the top group. The study relied on food-frequency questionnaires, residual confounding remains possible, and the authors stated that the results cannot readily be extrapolated to high-dose supplements. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29955838/)
- **Another supportive observational study**: A U.S. NHANES analysis included 23,894 people and recorded 2,365 deaths. The highest versus lowest quartile of estimated spermidine intake had an all-cause mortality HR of 0.70 (95% CI 0.60–0.82). This remains an observational association based on dietary recall and mortality linkage; it cannot exclude confounding by the overall dietary pattern and lifestyle represented by spermidine-containing foods. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36249217/)
- **Contrary evidence**: The Japanese Takayama study included 29,079 adults aged ≥35 years and recorded 5,339 deaths over 16 years. After full adjustment, spermidine intake was not significantly associated with all-cause or cause-specific mortality in either sex. A suggestive positive association with cancer mortality appeared in women (highest vs lowest quartile HR 1.38, 95% CI 0.99–1.93; P for trend = 0.02); this requires further study and is not proof of harm. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37964604/)
- **A newer cohort that should not be conflated**: A UK Biobank study of 184,732 people found a nonlinear association between *total polyamine* intake and mortality, with the lowest risk in quintile 2 (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.76–0.88, versus quintile 1). Because the exposure combined spermidine, spermine, and putrescine, it cannot establish an effect of spermidine alone. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39770955/)
- **No randomized human longevity evidence**: SmartAge randomized 100 adults aged 60–90 years with subjective cognitive decline to a spermidine-rich wheat-germ extract providing 0.9 mg/day or placebo for 12 months. The between-group difference in the primary memory endpoint was -0.03 (95% CI -0.11 to 0.05; P=.47), and secondary outcomes showed no overall differences. Mortality and lifespan were not studied. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35616942/)
- **Dose and absorption remain uncertain**: In a crossover pharmacokinetic study of only 12 healthy adults, 15 mg/day for each 5-day intervention period did not raise plasma spermidine but did raise its metabolite spermine. This study cannot establish clinical benefit or long-term safety. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37111071/)
- **Mechanistic boundary**: The pivotal evidence that spermidine improves autophagy-related and cardiovascular phenotypes and lifespan comes from mouse and rat experiments; it cannot establish a human lifespan benefit. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27841876/)

## Do
1. Do not target a spermidine dose for “life extension”; no effective dose has been established in a human randomized trial.
2. Whole grains, legumes, and mushrooms can be part of a balanced diet, but do not attribute the overall health associations of these foods to spermidine alone.
3. Do not self-purchase spermidine supplements solely for anti-aging. Human trials are small and use limited endpoints, so they do not resolve long-term benefit or safety.
4. If you are receiving treatment for a medical condition or considering a high-dose product, check with a clinician or pharmacist first; do not substitute a supplement for proven treatment.
