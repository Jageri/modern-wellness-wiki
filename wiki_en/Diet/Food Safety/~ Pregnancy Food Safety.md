# Pregnancy Food Safety

**Mortality Impact**: No direct evidence that pregnancy food-safety measures reduce all-cause mortality or extend lifespan; the relevant outcomes are maternal infection, miscarriage/stillbirth/preterm birth, severe neonatal infection, and fetal neurodevelopment—not adult all-cause mortality [1–10]
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (Authoritative guidance is highly consistent, but randomized trials are generally infeasible; food-specific risks rely mainly on surveillance, case-control/cohort studies, and systematic reviews)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion

Most foods remain available during pregnancy. The goal is not to keep shrinking the menu, but to reduce a short list of serious, preventable exposures: pathogens such as *Listeria* and *Toxoplasma*; raw or undercooked meat, eggs, and shellfish; unpasteurized foods; raw-to-ready-to-eat cross-contamination; prolonged room-temperature storage; and alcohol, excess caffeine, preformed vitamin A, and high-mercury fish. Advice on eggs, cheeses, cold-smoked fish, tuna, and locally caught fish differs by country, so local official guidance and obstetric advice take priority [1][4][6][12].

## Evidence Boundaries

- **Listeriosis is rare but can have severe pregnancy consequences.** The US CDC estimates that pregnant women are about 10 times as likely as the general population to acquire listeriosis; this is a relative-risk estimate from US surveillance, not an absolute risk for every pregnant person. Maternal illness can be limited to fever, myalgia, and fatigue—or be asymptomatic—while infection may cause miscarriage, stillbirth, preterm birth, or neonatal sepsis/meningitis. Reviews support the severity, but food-avoidance measures themselves rely mainly on observational evidence and outbreak investigations and cannot promise zero risk [1–3].
- **Toxoplasmosis prevention concerns primary infection during pregnancy and fetal transmission.** Sources include raw or undercooked meat, unwashed produce, contaminated soil or water, and cat feces; merely owning a cat does not equal infection. Cooking meat to a safe internal temperature, rinsing produce under running water, washing after raw meat or gardening, and having someone else clean litter (or using gloves and cleaning daily) are practical measures. A systematic review supports hygiene for primary prevention, while screening and treatment policies vary internationally [4][5].
- **Choose fish; do not remove all fish.** Fish provides protein, fatty acids, and micronutrients. Reviews generally find that moderate consumption of lower-mercury fish has neurodevelopmental benefits that outweigh risk, although mercury findings are not entirely consistent and observational studies struggle to separate fish nutrients from mercury exposure [6–8]. US FDA/EPA advice is 2–3 servings (8–12 oz, about 225–340 g) weekly from its “Best Choices” category; that is a US classification, not a universal species list. Check local advisories for self-caught fish. Cooking does not remove mercury from fish tissue [6].
- **Avoid alcohol during pregnancy.** Authorities have not identified a safe amount, time, or type. Higher prenatal exposure can cause lifelong harm, including fetal alcohol spectrum disorders. Low-dose studies are limited by underreporting, confounding, and selection bias and cannot prove that “a little is safe”; a quasi-experimental systematic review suggests harm to birthweight and neurodevelopment but cannot define an individual threshold. Abstinence is therefore precautionary guidance, not a claim that one inadvertent small exposure inevitably harmed the fetus; stop and disclose the exposure at prenatal care [9][10].
- **The 200 mg/day caffeine limit is precautionary, not a proven safety threshold.** ACOG and the NHS advise keeping total intake below 200 mg/day. An observational meta-analysis linked higher intake with miscarriage, stillbirth, low birthweight, and small-for-gestational-age birth, but heterogeneity, residual confounding, small-study effects, and publication bias were substantial. Count coffee, tea, cola, energy drinks, chocolate, and relevant medicines/supplements together [11–13].

## Safer Practice

1. **Use a thermometer rather than color.** US CDC/USDA reference temperatures are: whole cuts of beef, pork, and lamb 63°C/145°F plus a 3-minute rest; ground meat 71°C/160°F; poultry 74°C/165°F; fin fish 63°C/145°F; and leftovers or deli meats reheated to 74°C/165°F or steaming throughout. Not every animal food requires 74°C. Cook eggs thoroughly; if a dish must contain raw or undercooked egg, use pasteurized eggs/egg products. The UK British Lion exception for runny hen eggs is a local certified-control exception and should not be generalized to eggs without an equivalent system [1][4][14].
2. **Avoid the clearest pathogen exposures.** Do not drink unpasteurized milk or juice. Check that soft cheese is made with pasteurized milk and follow local advice. Deli meats, hot dogs, refrigerated pâté/meat spreads, and cold-smoked fish are higher-risk ready-to-eat foods for *Listeria*; US guidance permits deli meats after reheating to 74°C/165°F or steaming, while UK food categories differ [1][14][15].
3. **Clean, separate, and chill.** Keep raw meat and ready-to-eat foods on separate boards and in separate containers; wash after handling raw meat, eggs, soil, or litter. Do not leave perishables at room temperature for more than 2 cumulative hours, or 1 hour above 32°C/90°F; keep the refrigerator at or below 4°C/40°F. A packed meal is safer only if it remains cold and is reheated thoroughly [1][14].
4. **Use the fish list for your location.** On the US list, examples of lower-mercury “Best Choices” include salmon, sardines, cod, and shrimp; “Choices to Avoid” include bigeye tuna, shark, swordfish, marlin, orange roughy, Gulf of Mexico tilefish, and king mackerel. UK lists and portion limits differ, so these should not be merged into one global prohibition list [6][12].
5. **Avoid liver and high-dose retinol supplements.** Use pregnancy supplements according to local guidance and obstetric advice; needs for folic acid, iron, iodine, and other nutrients vary by region, diet, and medical history. Do not remove fish, eggs, dairy, nuts, meat, fruit, or vegetables solely because of folk taboos, since overly broad restriction can reduce protein and micronutrient intake [12].

## When to Contact a Clinician

- After eating food recalled or linked to a *Listeria* outbreak, promptly contact obstetric care if fever occurs with unusual fatigue or muscle aches, and report the food and timing. An asymptomatic exposed person usually does not need self-initiated testing or preventive antibiotics [2][15].
- Seek care for persistent vomiting or diarrhea, bloody stool, marked dehydration, severe abdominal pain, or noticeably reduced fetal movement.
- One accidental high-risk food exposure or small alcohol exposure does not mean fetal harm is inevitable. Stop further exposure, note timing and quantity, and discuss it at prenatal care rather than taking medicines or supplements to “detox.”
