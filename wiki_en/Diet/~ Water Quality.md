# Water Quality

**Mortality Impact**: No single percentage; unsafe water can cause infection or chronic chemical exposure, and individual benefit depends on source, contaminant, and actual exposure
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (Known hazards are clear, but benefits of household measures are highly context-dependent)
**Actionability**: Medium

## Conclusion
Identify the water source and contaminant before deciding whether to boil, filter, or switch to a safe source. Municipal water that meets current local standards and is not under an advisory does not require a filter merely because of generalized water-quality anxiety; building storage, premise plumbing, and changes after an outage still need separate assessment.[1][2][4]

## Evidence
- WHO drinking-water guidance uses source-to-consumer risk management rather than a “developed versus developing” label. China's current mandatory standard is **GB 5749—2022** (effective 2023-04-01; verified current on **2026-09-07**).[1][2]
- When **microbial contamination** is suspected or officials issue a boil-water advisory, bringing clear water to a rolling boil can inactivate pathogenic bacteria, viruses, and protozoa. Cool and store it in a clean, covered container to avoid recontamination.[3]
- Boiling **does not remove chemical contaminants** and cannot make water contaminated by fuel, heavy metals, or nitrate safe; evaporation may increase concentrations of non-volatile contaminants. Follow official instructions and use a safe alternative source or a treatment specifically validated for the contaminant.[4]
- A home filter is not universally necessary or universally effective. First identify the target contaminant from a local report or qualified laboratory test, check the device's explicit reduction claim, and replace cartridges according to its instructions; an expired cartridge may no longer work effectively.[5]
- There is no high-quality basis for a universal “clean every 1–2 months” rule for all dispensers. Follow the device instructions; for bottle-fed coolers, clean water-contact parts when changing bottles, and act immediately after visible soil, odor, prolonged disuse, or a contamination concern.[6]
- A systematic review of 10 eligible studies found no additional health effects of alkaline or oxygenated water versus mineral water. The evidence and outcomes were limited and do not establish anti-aging or longevity benefits (PMID: [36571558](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36571558/)).[7]
- Small human studies of hydrogen-rich water exist, but a systematic review of 25 articles still called for larger samples and more rigorous designs. Preliminary biomarker or condition-specific findings cannot currently be generalized into anti-aging, disease-prevention, or longevity claims (PMID: [38256045](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38256045/)).[14]
- Microplastics have been detected in both tap and bottled water. A 2020 systematic review found extreme methodological heterogeneity (**I²>95%**), so particle counts cannot be translated directly into human disease risk (PMID: [32735575](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32735575/)). WHO considers the evidence limited and calls for more research. Microplastics alone therefore do not justify a categorical rule against keeping bottled emergency water.[8][9]

## Selected Limits and Testing
These are selected routine indicators from GB 5749—2022, not a complete home-testing panel: *E. coli* **not detectable in 100 mL**; arsenic and lead each **≤0.01 mg/L**; nitrate (as N) **≤10 mg/L**; fluoride **≤1.0 mg/L**; turbidity **≤1 NTU**; pH **6.5–8.5**; total dissolved solids **≤1000 mg/L**. The full standard includes conditions and many more parameters; qualified laboratories should use standard methods.[2][10]

- Municipal supply: check the local utility's published results and public-health advisories. In the absence of a concern, households do not need comprehensive testing on a fixed schedule.[2]
- Private wells, rainwater, and other unregulated sources: test at least annually for microorganisms and locally relevant chemicals, and promptly after flooding, well or plumbing repair, a change in color/odor, a nearby contamination event, or unexplained gastrointestinal illness. “Annual” is a conservative CDC/EPA recommendation for private wells, not a substitute for Chinese local requirements.[5][11]
- A TDS meter, appearance, odor, and taste cannot rule out pathogens, lead, arsenic, or other hazards; rely on qualified testing and official information.[5]

## Do
1. During a microbial boil-water advisory, follow the notice; if it gives no specific procedure, use WHO's rolling-boil-and-cool principle.[3]
2. Choose filters by target contaminant, validated reduction claim, and timely maintenance—not marketing terms or a single TDS reading.[5]
3. Keep dispensers and storage containers clean, tightly covered, and cool; do not put hands or utensils into stored water, and never reuse a container that held fuel, pesticides, or other toxic chemicals.[6][12]
4. Store commercial bottled water as labeled and use it before expiry; rotate emergency supplies under local emergency guidance rather than abandoning needed reserves because of microplastic concerns.[8][9]

## Don't
1. Do not use boiling as a remedy for lead, arsenic, nitrate, fuel, or other chemical contamination.[4]
2. Do not keep using an expired filter cartridge or assume that activated carbon, softening, or reverse osmosis removes every contaminant.[5]
3. Do not treat “alkaline,” “hydrogen/oxygen-rich,” or “micro-clustered” water as a proven anti-aging or longevity therapy.[7][14]
4. Do not drink untested and inadequately treated well, rain, spring, or other unregulated water over the long term.[11]

## When to Seek Help
- If officials issue a contamination, boil-water, or “do not drink” advisory, distinguish the type: chemical contamination must not be managed by boiling in place of a safe source.[4]
- If several people develop diarrhea, vomiting, or fever after drinking the same water—or if an individual has bloody stool, persistent vomiting, marked dehydration, or altered consciousness—contact local public health officials and seek medical care. Infants, pregnant people, older adults, and immunocompromised people warrant earlier advice.[15]
- If a private-source test exceeds a limit, stop using it for drinking and cooking and ask local public-health authorities or a qualified professional to interpret the result and plan confirmation testing, repair, treatment, or source replacement.[11]

## Evidence Boundary
The core evidence concerns drinking-water standards, public-health guidance, and studies of outcomes such as diarrheal disease—not trials showing that a special water prolongs life. Most point-of-use treatment studies were conducted in low- and middle-income settings with substantial microbial contamination; many were unblinded and used self-reported diarrhea, so relative effects cannot be directly transferred to households receiving compliant municipal water.[13]
