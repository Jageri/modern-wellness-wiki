# Vaccination

**Longevity Impact**: No all-cause mortality reduction percentage applies across all vaccines and adults; benefit varies by vaccine, circulating disease, age, baseline risk, and time since vaccination
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (strong evidence for specific disease outcomes; no unified all-cause mortality effect)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion

Vaccinate according to the current local schedule, age, medical risk, and product label. Vaccines can prevent their target infections or severe outcomes, but incidence, hospitalization, disease-specific death, and all-cause death from different vaccines cannot be combined into a claim of "30–50% lower mortality." WHO's estimate that immunization averts 3.5–5 million deaths annually is a global population estimate across multiple vaccines, not an individual's relative reduction in all-cause mortality. [1]

## Evidence by vaccine

- **Influenza**: The Chinese CDC's 2026–2027 guidance recommends seasonal vaccination for people aged 6 months or older without contraindications, prioritizing adults aged 60 or older, people with chronic illness, pregnant people, and other high-risk groups. Effectiveness varies by season, strain match, product, and outcome. Large all-cause mortality reductions in observational studies are particularly vulnerable to healthy-vaccinee bias. [2][3]
- **Pneumococcal**: In the CAPiTA randomized trial of 84,496 adults aged 65 or older, PCV13 efficacy was 45.6% (95.2% CI 21.8–62.5) against vaccine-type community-acquired pneumonia, but efficacy against community-acquired pneumonia of any cause was not significant (5.1%, 95% CI -5.1–14.2), and deaths were similar between groups. This does not support a uniform all-cause mortality reduction. [4]
- **Herpes zoster**: In the ZOE-70 randomized trial, recombinant zoster vaccine efficacy against herpes zoster was 89.8% (95% CI 84.2–93.7) in adults aged 70 or older. Solicited local/systemic reactions were more common, while serious adverse events and deaths occurred at similar frequencies to placebo. The endpoint was shingles incidence, not longer life. The trial was funded by GSK. [5]
- **COVID-19**: WHO's 2026 position paper says additional doses improved protection against severe disease for the months after vaccination, but protection could wane to little or none by 6 months in high-risk groups. Estimates depend on variant, time, and risk group. Observational all-cause mortality comparisons are also affected by healthy-vaccinee bias. [6][7]

## Do

1. Review vaccination records and catch up under current local policy and product labels. People with chronic illness, immunocompromise, pregnancy, or a history of severe allergy should confirm the product and timing with a vaccination clinic.
2. Receive influenza vaccine each season. China's 2026–2027 guidance recommends completion in September–October; vaccination remains possible throughout the influenza season if that window is missed. [2]
3. Pneumococcal, zoster, and COVID-19 vaccine ages, dose counts, and intervals vary by product, risk, and current policy. Do not substitute a fixed age slogan for current local recommendations.

## Safety and medical care

Pain, fatigue, or a brief fever can occur after vaccination. Temporal sequence alone does not prove causation; suspected serious events require expert causality assessment. [8] Seek emergency care for breathing difficulty, face/throat swelling, collapse, or a rapidly worsening generalized reaction. Contact the vaccination clinic or a clinician for other persistent or worsening symptoms.
