# Antibiotics

**Mortality impact**: Can be lifesaving when indicated; unnecessary use, an unsuitable agent, or excessive duration increases adverse-event and resistance risks
**Evidence level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (strong evidence for appropriate-use principles; no evidence that antibiotics themselves extend life in people without an infection)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion

Antibiotics are essential treatments for certain bacterial infections, and delaying them in a serious bacterial infection can be life-threatening. They do not treat viral infections such as the common cold or influenza. Do not self-treat a cold or nonspecific upper-respiratory infection merely because nasal discharge or sputum is yellow or green.

If an antibiotic has been prescribed, take the drug, dose, interval, and number of days on that prescription. Do not stop just because you feel better, and do not extend treatment on your own to "finish a course." Contact the prescriber or pharmacist before shortening, stopping, or changing treatment, including when an adverse effect makes it difficult to continue. For many common infections, evidence supports the shortest effective duration selected by a clinician according to the infection, severity, pathogen, and clinical response; this is not permission for patient-directed discontinuation.

## Evidence

- **Necessary treatment and misuse are different problems**: WHO and CDC both emphasize that antibiotics are indispensable for some life-threatening bacterial infections, while unnecessary use, the wrong antibiotic, and overuse are major drivers of resistance. Bacteria become resistant; the patient's body does not "get used to" the medicine.[1][2]
- **AMR burden estimates are population models**: In 2021, an estimated 1.14 million deaths (95% uncertainty interval 1.00–1.28 million) were attributable to bacterial antimicrobial resistance (AMR), while 4.71 million (4.23–5.19 million) were associated with it. "Attributable" compares resistant infections with susceptible infections; "associated" compares them with no infection. These estimates are not interchangeable and do not quantify the mortality effect of one person's antibiotic course. The analysis covered 204 countries and territories but statistically modelled locations with sparse data.[3]
- **Upper-respiratory symptoms require an indication, not a blanket rule**: Antibiotics do not treat the common cold, influenza, or most acute bronchitis. Group A streptococcal pharyngitis, pertussis, and bacterial pneumonia can have valid indications. "Do not treat a cold" must not be turned into "refuse antibiotics for every respiratory illness."[2][4]
- **Duration depends on the infection and the patient**: A systematic review through July 2024 included 315 randomized duration trials; 85% reported no statistical difference, equivalence, or non-inferiority for shorter versus longer treatment. Evidence was robust for shorter courses in adult bacterial sinusitis, community-acquired pneumonia, female cystitis/pyelonephritis, uncomplicated cellulitis, and intra-abdominal infection after adequate source control. However, only 15% of trials were at low risk of bias, and evidence was sparse in critical illness and low- and middle-income settings.[5] The subsequently published BALANCE randomized trial enrolled 3,608 hospitalized patients with bloodstream infection (55% were in an ICU at enrollment). Ninety-day mortality was 14.5% with 7 days and 16.1% with 14 days, meeting the prespecified non-inferiority criterion. However, the trial excluded *Staphylococcus aureus* bloodstream infection, foci requiring prolonged treatment, and severe immunosuppression, so it does not justify a short course for every serious infection.[10]
- **Microbiome changes are not proven lifespan loss**: A systematic review of 31 studies at low or unclear risk of bias found rapid reductions in bacterial diversity and altered relative abundance after several common antibiotics. Most people moved toward baseline within weeks, although some studies found changes lasting 2–6 months. Substantial methodological heterogeneity prevents concluding that typical use necessarily causes allergy, autoimmune disease, or shorter life.[6]
- **`C. difficile` is important but does not occur in everyone**: In a systematic review and meta-analysis of adult hospital inpatients, cases of healthcare-facility-associated `C. difficile` infection were more than twice as likely as controls to have had recent carbapenem or third-/fourth-generation cephalosporin exposure. Effect estimates varied, and most studies came from North America or Europe, so this is not an absolute risk for every community user.[7]
- **Adverse effects differ by drug**: Common problems include nausea, diarrhea, and rash; less common but important harms include severe allergic reactions and `C. difficile` infection. Some classes have specific warnings. For example, the FDA requires systemic fluoroquinolones to carry warnings about disabling and potentially persistent adverse effects involving tendons, muscles, joints, nerves, and the central nervous system. This does not mean that fluoroquinolones have no role in serious infection; clinicians should weigh the indication and alternatives.[8]

## Do

1. Use only the antibiotic prescribed for this illness at this visit and take it as directed. Ask: What infection is being treated? For how many days? When should improvement occur? Which reactions require medical care?
2. Tell the clinician or pharmacist about drug allergies, pregnancy or breastfeeding, liver or kidney disease, rhythm disorders, and all other medicines; these can affect drug and dose selection.
3. Seek reassessment if you do not improve within the prescriber's expected timeframe, worsen, or develop new high fever, breathing difficulty, or altered mental status. Do not switch or add antibiotics yourself.
4. Dispose of unused medicine through local take-back options or as a pharmacist advises. Do not save it for a future illness or share it.

## Don't

1. Do not self-purchase antibiotics for a cold, yellow/green mucus, fever, or a general desire for an "anti-inflammatory" drug.
2. Do not use someone else's medicine or leftovers, and do not change the dose, switch drugs, stop, or extend the course on your own.
3. Do not assume all prophylactic antibiotics are useless. Evidence-based indications can exist around surgery, specific pathogen exposure, or in selected high-risk immunocompromised people; routine "just in case" use for a cold or without an indication is inappropriate.[9]
4. Do not interpret microbiome markers, global AMR model estimates, or observational associations as proof that antibiotics inevitably shorten an individual's life. Conversely, do not refuse indicated treatment because of fear of resistance.

## When to seek care

- **Emergency care now**: trouble breathing or swallowing; swelling of the face, tongue, or throat; fainting; or a widespread blistering or peeling rash.
- **Prompt medical assessment**: substantial or persistent watery diarrhea, bloody stool, severe abdominal pain, diarrhea with fever, or dehydration. These may occur during or after treatment. Do not self-treat severe symptoms with an antidiarrheal drug.
- **Contact the prescriber promptly** for a new or unusual adverse effect. If taking a fluoroquinolone (such as levofloxacin or ciprofloxacin) and new tendon/joint pain, weakness, numbness or tingling, confusion, or similar serious symptoms occur, contact a clinician immediately for drug-specific stop/substitution instructions.[8]

## Evidence boundaries

- This entry cannot replace diagnosis, pathogen testing, local susceptibility data, organ-function assessment, or an individualized prescription.
- Drug and duration vary by infection, organism, age, pregnancy or breastfeeding, liver and kidney function, and immune status. There is no universal number of days.
- Global AMR deaths are statistical model estimates useful for public health; they cannot calculate an individual's net lifespan effect from antibiotics.
