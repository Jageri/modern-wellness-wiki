# High Cholesterol

**Mortality Impact**: ~ (No reliable evidence supports a uniform 30%–50% increase in all-cause mortality; the main evidence concerns atherosclerotic cardiovascular events)
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (In people with an indication, lowering LDL-C reduces major vascular events; the all-cause mortality effect and individual absolute benefit are not uniform)
**Actionability**: High

## Conclusion
LDL-C is an important modifiable cause of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), but a single test result or universal threshold should not decide treatment. Plans should incorporate established ASCVD, overall risk, age, comorbidities, family history, and patient preferences. Lifestyle is foundational, and statins are generally first-line drug therapy.[1]

## Evidence
- A CTT meta-analysis of 26 randomized trials and about 170,000 participants found that each 1 mmol/L LDL-C reduction lowered the relative risk of major vascular events by about 22% (RR about 0.78). This is a **treatment effect on cardiovascular events**, not a 22% increase in all-cause mortality from high LDL-C and not a 30%–50% mortality effect.[2]
- In a primary-prevention systematic review, statins yielded an all-cause mortality RR of 0.92 (95% CI 0.87–0.98), with an absolute risk difference of -0.35%; absolute differences were -0.85% for myocardial infarction and -1.28% for composite cardiovascular outcomes. Absolute benefit depends on baseline risk, and evidence in adults older than 75 was sparse.[3]
- Observational cohorts support an association between higher LDL-C and myocardial infarction/ASCVD. In one cohort without baseline ASCVD, diabetes, or statin therapy, each 1 mmol/L higher LDL-C was associated with HR 1.34 for myocardial infarction and HR 1.16 for ASCVD. Observational associations cannot be converted directly into treatment effects or all-cause mortality effects.[4]
- LDL-C ≥4.9 mmol/L (190 mg/dL) is severe hypercholesterolemia and warrants prompt evaluation for familial hypercholesterolemia and secondary causes; the value alone does not diagnose familial hypercholesterolemia.[1]
- For adults aged ≥75 without ASCVD, starting lipid-lowering medication should be individualized. A post hoc ALLHAT-LLT analysis in older primary-prevention adults found no all-cause mortality benefit from pravastatin (age ≥65: HR 1.18, 95% CI 0.97–1.42); crossover and a modest between-group LDL difference limit certainty.[5]

## Do
1. Repeat fasting or nonfasting lipid testing as advised (total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglycerides). When results are abnormal, assess history, medications, and secondary causes such as thyroid, liver, or kidney disease.
2. Assess overall ASCVD risk rather than total cholesterol alone, including age, blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, prior ASCVD, and family history of premature ASCVD. When a decision remains uncertain, a clinician may consider coronary artery calcium scoring. Adults may consider at least one lifetime lipoprotein(a) measurement and selective apolipoprotein B testing.[1]
3. Replace some saturated fat with unsaturated fat, avoid trans fat, choose fiber-rich whole foods, exercise, maintain a healthy weight, and avoid smoking. Supplements are not a substitute for indicated lipid-lowering treatment.[1]
4. People with ASCVD, LDL-C ≥4.9 mmol/L, or high overall risk should discuss medication promptly. Statins are generally first-line; add-on therapy and goals depend on risk category, response, tolerance, and local guidance.[1]

## Don't
1. Do not interpret “about 22% fewer events per 1 mmol/L reduction” as 22% longer life or 22% lower all-cause mortality for an individual.
2. Do not self-start medication after one mildly high result or stop medication solely from fear of adverse effects; especially in primary prevention, compare personal absolute benefit and harm.
3. Do not attribute every muscle symptom during statin use to the drug. In double-blind trials, pain or weakness occurred in 27.1% vs 26.6%, with about 11 excess events per 1,000 person-years in year 1. Most reports were not caused by statins, but symptoms still deserve assessment and can often be managed by adjusting the dose, drug, or regimen.[6]

## Medical Red Flags
- LDL-C ≥4.9 mmol/L, or personal/close-family premature myocardial infarction, stroke, or known familial hypercholesterolemia
- Prior myocardial infarction, stroke, peripheral artery disease, or other ASCVD requiring prompt risk stratification and secondary prevention
- Severe or progressive muscle pain/weakness, dark urine, or other marked symptoms during therapy: seek care promptly and do not permanently stop treatment without medical advice
