# Metformin

**Mortality impact**: No adequately powered randomized controlled trial has shown that metformin extends lifespan in the general population without diabetes. A systematic review dominated by observational studies found all-cause mortality HR 0.93 (95% CI 0.88–0.99) for metformin-treated patients with diabetes versus non-diabetic controls; this is a cross-disease observational comparison that cannot be attributed to metformin or generalized to treatment effects in the general population without diabetes.
**Evidence level**: ⭐⭐ (longevity evidence is disputed)
**Actionability**: Low (prescription medicine; do not self-prescribe for "anti-aging")

## Conclusion

Metformin is an established glucose-lowering medicine, but it is not a proven human anti-aging or lifespan-extending drug. Its clinical value in type 2 diabetes does not establish a longevity benefit in people without diabetes. Early observational signals conflict with randomized evidence: long-term DPP/DPPOS follow-up in adults at high risk of diabetes also found no all-cause mortality benefit. As of September 2026, TAME was still raising funds to launch, not an ongoing trial with a reliable results date.

## Evidence

- **A specific RCT signal in diabetes**: UKPDS 34 randomized 753 newly diagnosed overweight patients with type 2 diabetes to intensive glucose control with metformin or a conventional policy based mainly on diet, with median follow-up of 10.7 years. Metformin reduced the relative risk of all-cause mortality by 36% (95% CI 9%–55%; p=0.011). This finding directly applies only to a similar diabetes population and historical comparator; it does not demonstrate longevity in healthy people. [[1]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9742977/)
- **Observational longevity signal**: A 2017 systematic review of 53 studies reported HR 0.93 (95% CI 0.88–0.99) for all-cause mortality in metformin-treated patients with diabetes versus non-diabetic controls, and HR 0.72 (95% CI 0.65–0.80) versus patients receiving other diabetes therapies. The former is a cross-disease observational comparison between treated patients with diabetes and people without diabetes, and the paper noted baseline differences capable of biasing results. Residual confounding from treatment selection, contraindications, disease severity, and comparator composition means these estimates cannot be attributed to metformin or generalized to treatment effects in the general population without diabetes. [[2]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28802803/)
- **Long-term randomized mortality results in adults at high risk of diabetes**: DPP/DPPOS randomized 3,234 adults at high risk of diabetes to intensive lifestyle intervention, masked metformin, or placebo. Over a median 21 years, 453 participants died; by original assignment, the all-cause mortality HR for metformin versus placebo was 0.99 (95% CI 0.79–1.25), showing no reduction. This was not a general population without diabetes. Moreover, the placebo and original lifestyle interventions stopped in 2001, everyone was offered a modified lifestyle program, and open-label study metformin continued in the group originally assigned to it, making long-term exposure more complex than the initial blinded phase. The result cannot prove no effect in the general population, but it does not support a lifespan claim. [[3]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34697033/)
- **Randomized evidence did not confirm mortality or cancer prevention**: A pooled analysis of 13 RCTs with 552 deaths found all-cause mortality RR 0.94 (95% CI 0.79–1.12). A pooled analysis of 11 RCTs with 398 cancers found cancer-outcome RR 1.02 (95% CI 0.82–1.26). Both confidence intervals include 1, so the data do not support causal claims of reduced mortality or a one-third reduction in cancer risk. Limitations included heterogeneous comparators, missing cancer data, and short follow-up. Author affiliations included Takeda Pharmaceutical and Novartis Pharma; this does not establish the direction of bias but should be disclosed. [[4]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22875195/)
- **Counterevidence in healthy older adults**: In the double-blind MASTERS trial, 94 healthy adults aged 65 or older performed 14 weeks of progressive resistance training. Gains in lean body mass (p=0.003) and thigh muscle mass (p<0.001) were greater with placebo than metformin. These are short-term physiological rather than mortality outcomes, but they show that metformin does not necessarily confer net benefit in healthy older adults. [[5]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31557380/)
- **Functional counterevidence in frail older adults**: MET-PREVENT randomized 72 UK participants aged 65 or older with probable sarcopenia and physical prefrailty or frailty to metformin or placebo for 4 months. The adjusted between-group difference in 4-m walking speed was 0.001 m/s (95% CI -0.06 to 0.06; p=0.96), showing no improvement, and the investigators reported poor tolerability. This was a small, short functional trial, not a mortality or lifespan trial. [[6]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40147475/)
- **Current uncertainty**: A 2025 critical review concluded that methodological limitations in early epidemiology, replication attempts, and trials in people without type 2 diabetes have increased uncertainty about metformin's anti-aging potential. Some authors disclosed consulting, speaking, or grant relationships with pharmaceutical companies, so the review should be interpreted alongside independent evidence. [[7]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40582648/)
- **Safety and guideline boundary**: The 2026 ADA guideline describes metformin as a commonly used glucose-lowering agent when no other considerations dominate drug choice, not the sole first-line option for everyone. Gastrointestinal intolerance is common; long-term use can cause vitamin B12 deficiency; serious lactic acidosis is rare, but metformin is contraindicated at eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m². [[8]](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc26-s009)
- **TAME status**: AFAR explicitly labels TAME as raising funds to launch, with a proposed enrollment of 3,000 participants aged 65–79. Until it launches and completes, it is not efficacy evidence. [[9]](https://www.afar.org/tame-trial)

## Do

1. If you do not have diabetes, do not self-prescribe metformin for longevity; no adequately powered randomized controlled trial in the general population without diabetes has shown that it extends lifespan.
2. If you have type 2 diabetes, drug choice should be individualized by a clinician based on glycemic goals, cardiovascular disease, heart failure, kidney disease, weight, and tolerability. Do not start or stop treatment because of anti-aging marketing.
3. If you use metformin long term, discuss kidney function and vitamin B12 monitoring with your clinician. Seek evaluation for persistent diarrhea, marked appetite loss, unexplained fatigue, or neurological symptoms.
4. Do not substitute an unproven anti-aging drug for exercise, diet, sleep, smoking cessation, or blood-pressure management.
