# Helicobacter pylori Infection

**Mortality Impact**: Randomized trials indicate that eradication reduces gastric-cancer mortality (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.62–0.98; 5 trials), but have not shown lower all-cause mortality (RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.87–1.11; 5 trials, 7,079 participants) [1][2]
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (multiple randomized trials support gastric-cancer prevention, but absolute benefit depends on baseline risk; no established all-cause mortality benefit)
**Actionability**: High
**Currency**: Evidence checked through 2026-09-08; dynamic guideline anchors are the 2026 Chinese adult clinical-practice guideline and the 2024 ACG guideline [3][4]

## Conclusion

*H. pylori* is an important modifiable cause of gastric cancer. Testing and downstream management should be decided together: test when there is a clinical indication or participation in an organized population program and when a positive result will lead to clinical assessment and treatment. Once active infection is reliably confirmed, current guidelines generally recommend eradication unless a contraindication or competing factor outweighs benefit. This is not a mandate for everyone to buy a self-test, and it does not justify self-purchased antibiotics [1][3][4].

## Evidence

- The IARC/WHO Working Group's synthesis of updated randomized evidence reported relative reductions of **36%** in gastric-cancer incidence and **22%** in gastric-cancer mortality; corresponding NNTs were 228 and 812. These NNTs are population averages under specific baseline risks and follow-up conditions, not fixed individual benefits [1].
- A 2025 update included 11 randomized trials and 13 observational studies. Among *H. pylori*-positive people without gastric neoplasia at baseline, 8 randomized trials gave an RR of 0.64 (95% CI 0.48–0.84) for gastric-cancer incidence, and 5 randomized trials gave an RR of 0.78 (95% CI 0.62–0.98) for gastric-cancer mortality. The IARC synthesis of 5 all-cause-mortality trials found 420/3,551 versus 426/3,528 deaths (RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.87–1.11), with no demonstrated all-cause benefit [1][2].
- China's 2026 guideline covers 45 clinical questions spanning diagnosis, treatment, and prevention. The 2024 ACG guideline treats the decision to test and the decision to treat a positive result as one linked process and requires confirmation of cure after treatment. Their geographic settings, drug availability, and resistance backgrounds differ, so a regimen from one country should not be copied mechanically elsewhere [3][4].
- Eradication regimens can cause diarrhea, nausea, headache, rash, and other adverse effects and increase antibiotic exposure; risks vary by regimen. Cancer-prevention trials reported adverse events too incompletely to yield a reliable universal harm rate. Evidence about reflux after eradication is not fully consistent and should not be framed as a certain long-term harm or benefit [1][4][5].

## Do

1. **Test in an appropriate setting.** Current or prior peptic ulcer, selected uninvestigated dyspepsia, gastric MALT lymphoma, unexplained iron-deficiency anemia, immune thrombocytopenic purpura, gastric atrophy/metaplasia/dysplasia, increased gastric-cancer risk, and adult household contact with a case confirmed by nonserologic testing are reasons to discuss testing. Age, alarm features, cancer risk, and local guidance determine whether noninvasive testing or endoscopy comes first [4].
2. **Distinguish active-infection testing from screening.** Urea breath, stool antigen, and biopsy-based tests can detect active infection. Serology can have a role in a designed population-screening program, but it cannot distinguish past from active infection and cannot confirm cure [1][4].
3. **Let a clinician select the regimen.** Report penicillin and other allergies, all previous antibiotic exposure, previous eradication regimens and failures, pregnancy or breastfeeding, kidney and liver disease, and current medicines. The 2024 ACG guideline advises against empiric clarithromycin- or levofloxacin-containing regimens without demonstrated susceptibility; a penicillin-allergy label may warrant professional assessment [4].
4. **Complete the prescribed course** and contact the prescriber about important adverse effects rather than changing, adding, substituting, or stopping medicines independently.
5. **Obtain a test of cure.** Use a urea breath test, stool antigen test, or biopsy-based method at least 4 weeks after therapy. To reduce false negatives, stop PPIs/PCABs for at least 2 weeks and antibiotics and bismuth for at least 4 weeks before testing; a clinician should arrange symptom-control bridging [4].
6. **Continue indicated surveillance.** If gastric atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, or prior early gastric cancer is present, successful eradication does not reset cancer risk to zero and does not cancel clinician-recommended endoscopic surveillance [1][4].

## Don't

1. Do not buy, combine, or repeat antibiotics without medical supervision. Previous exposure, resistance, allergy, interactions, and adherence all change the probability of cure and harm [3][4][5].
2. Do not infer that absence of stomach pain excludes infection, or that a positive result means gastric cancer is already present.
3. Do not treat an entire household because one person is positive. Adult household members may discuss testing, but each person should first have infection confirmed and then be assessed individually [4].
4. Do not use serology to confirm cure, substitute eradication for indicated endoscopic follow-up, or translate lower gastric-cancer mortality into a proven claim of longer overall survival [1][4].
5. During pregnancy or breastfeeding, do not start an eradication regimen without specialist review. Drug combinations have different boundaries: for example, the current U.S. label for the fixed bismuth–metronidazole–tetracycline product PYLERA contraindicates it in pregnancy and instructs patients to pump and discard milk during therapy and for 2 days after the last dose. This product-specific label cannot be generalized to every regimen; gastroenterology and obstetric/pediatric clinicians should decide timing and treatment [5].

## Medical Red Flags

- Vomiting blood, or black tarry stool with dizziness, weakness, or palpitations, requires urgent care.
- Progressive difficulty swallowing, unexplained weight loss or anemia, or persistent vomiting needs prompt gastrointestinal assessment [4].
- Breathing difficulty, swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or throat, or a severe widespread rash after medication requires emergency care [5].

## Caveats

- Randomized evidence comes mainly from infected Asian, especially East Asian, populations. Absolute benefit varies with regional gastric-cancer burden, age, gastric-mucosal status, eradication success, and follow-up [1][2].
- Lower gastric-cancer incidence and gastric-cancer mortality do not establish lower all-cause mortality; randomized evidence still shows no clear all-cause difference [1].
- This entry does not prescribe a regimen. Availability, resistance patterns, regulatory indications, and guidelines change; decisions should follow current local guidance and clinical assessment.
