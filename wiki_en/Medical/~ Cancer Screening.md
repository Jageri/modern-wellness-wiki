# Cancer Screening

**Mortality Impact**: Cannot be summarized by one percentage; benefit depends on cancer type, age, risk, test, and follow-up quality
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Cancer-specific moderate- to high-quality evidence; no valid single pooled effect)
**Actionability**: High, but only after confirming eligibility

## Conclusion

Screening means looking for precancer or early cancer in **people without symptoms** according to a defined program. Evidence supports particular combinations of cancer, population, and test—not indiscriminate testing. Symptoms or an abnormal screen require diagnostic evaluation rather than waiting for the next screening round.

## Evidence

- **Lung cancer**: NLST randomized 53,454 high-risk smokers aged 55–74 to three rounds of low-dose CT (LDCT) or chest radiography. LDCT produced a 20.0% relative reduction in lung-cancer mortality (95% CI 6.8%–26.7%) and a 6.7% relative reduction in all-cause mortality. These results do not apply to low-risk or never-smoking populations. Under the older NLST criteria, 96.4% of positive LDCT results were false positives; modern nodule-management protocols can reduce this proportion.[1][2]
- **Colorectal cancer**: At 13 years in the randomized NordICC trial, invitation to one colonoscopy reduced colorectal-cancer incidence from 1.80% to 1.46% (RR 0.81, 95% CI 0.71–0.90). Colorectal-cancer mortality was 0.47% versus 0.41% (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.68–1.08), which was not a statistically significant mortality benefit. Observational estimates of a 50%–68% mortality reduction must not be presented as established randomized evidence.[3]
- **Breast cancer**: Systematic reviews of randomized trials estimate roughly a 20% reduction in breast-cancer-specific mortality, with uncertainty by age and screening interval and no demonstrated reduction in all-cause mortality. False positives, additional biopsies, overdiagnosis, and overtreatment are material harms. Observational estimates are also vulnerable to healthy-screenee bias.[4][5]
- **Cervical cancer**: Compared with cytology alone, strategies that included high-risk HPV testing reduced invasive cervical cancer in an individual-participant-data meta-analysis (RR 0.60, 95% CI 0.40–0.89). This is not evidence of an 80% reduction in cervical-cancer mortality. HPV-based strategies also increased first-round false positives and colposcopy referrals.[6]
- **Five-year survival is not a screening-effect measure**: Screening advances the date of diagnosis and detects some lesions that would never cause harm. Lead-time bias and overdiagnosis can therefore improve postdiagnosis survival without showing that people live longer.[7]

## Do

1. **First confirm that a screening recommendation applies**: review age, smoking and occupational exposure, personal and first-degree family history, inherited susceptibility, prior screening, life expectancy, and willingness or ability to undergo diagnostic work-up and treatment.
2. **Lung cancer**: China's 2024 National Health Commission program generally targets high-risk adults aged 50–74 for annual LDCT. High-risk criteria include at least 20 pack-years with quitting less than 15 years ago, and specified long-term secondhand-smoke exposure, COPD, occupational carcinogen exposure, or a first-degree relative with lung cancer. Chest radiography, PET-CT, and biomarkers are not recommended substitutes for LDCT screening.[8]
3. **Colorectal cancer**: China's 2024 program targets high-risk adults aged 40–74. First-line colonoscopy is generally repeated every 5–10 years (10 years after a normal examination); annual fecal occult blood testing and other methods are alternatives when colonoscopy is not tolerated or accepted. Familial adenomatous polyposis and Lynch syndrome require earlier, more intensive specialist programs. Separately, the USPSTF recommends screening average-risk asymptomatic adults aged 45–75 and individualized decisions at 76–85; this should not be conflated with China's high-risk program.[8][9]
4. **Breast cancer**: China's public-health breast and cervical cancer program serves women aged 35–64, with the specific pathway set by the local program. As a separately identifiable average-risk reference, the USPSTF recommends biennial mammography from age 40 through 74; evidence is insufficient after age 74. People with prior breast cancer, high-risk mutations, or high-dose chest radiation at a young age are outside this average-risk recommendation.[10][11]
5. **Cervical cancer**: China's public-health program also covers women aged 35–64. As an average-risk reference, the USPSTF recommends cytology every 3 years at ages 21–29; at 30–65, use cytology every 3 years, primary high-risk HPV testing every 5 years, or cotesting every 5 years. Stop after 65 only after adequate prior negative screening and in the absence of high-risk factors. Immunosuppression and prior high-grade precancer or cervical cancer require individualized follow-up.[10][12]
6. A positive screening test means that protocol-based follow-up or diagnosis is needed; it is not itself a cancer diagnosis. A complete program includes result interpretation, referral, and follow-up.[7]

## Don't

1. Do not use a "comprehensive tumor-marker panel" or an unproven multicancer blood test for asymptomatic, average-risk screening. Circulating tumor markers are generally not sensitive or specific enough for population screening.[13]
2. Do not use whole-body CT or PET-CT as indiscriminate cancer screening in healthy, asymptomatic people. Whole-body CT has not been shown to be effective screening and adds radiation and follow-up of incidental findings.[14]
3. Do not treat one positive screen as a diagnosis, or one negative screen as a reason to ignore new symptoms.
4. Do not shorten screening intervals simply for reassurance; more testing can increase false positives, biopsies, overdiagnosis, and overtreatment without adding benefit.

## Medical Red Flags

Unexplained weight loss, persistent or worsening cough or coughing blood, blood in stool or a persistent bowel-habit change, a new breast lump, abnormal vaginal bleeding, and any abnormal screening result require timely diagnostic evaluation—not more routine screening or waiting for the next checkup.
