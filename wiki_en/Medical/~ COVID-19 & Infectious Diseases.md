# COVID-19 and Infectious Diseases

**Mortality impact**: No single percentage applies. Acute COVID-19 can cause death; post-infection all-cause mortality and organ-specific risks vary substantially by acute severity, age and comorbidity, pandemic era, vaccination, and prior infection. Current evidence does not support a fixed “30% increase” for all infected people.
**Evidence level**: ⭐⭐⭐ (Authoritative surveillance and large observational studies support the existence of risk, but causality and generalizability of long-term effect sizes across eras and populations are limited.)
**Actionability**: Medium
**Dynamic information current to**: 2026-09-08

## Conclusion

SARS-CoV-2 continues to circulate globally, but present-day individual risk should not be inferred directly from 2020–2021 data, when infection-derived immunity was less common and vaccine coverage was lower. Acute infection mortality, pandemic excess mortality, post-infection risk, and other infectious diseases should be considered separately, with measures tailored to personal risk and current local guidance.

## Evidence

### 1. Acute infection mortality

- WHO (updated 2025-11-27) states that COVID-19 continues to cause hospitalizations and deaths. Older adults, unvaccinated people, and people with diabetes, obesity, heart, lung, liver or kidney disease, cancer, dementia, or immunosuppression have higher risks of severe disease.[1]
- Acute-phase deaths are not the same endpoint as all-cause mortality among people who survive the infection.

### 2. Excess mortality during the pandemic

- A modeling study estimated 18.2 million excess deaths worldwide (95% uncertainty interval 17.1–19.6 million) from 2020-01-01 through 2021-12-31. Excess mortality is the difference between observed deaths and historically expected deaths and includes direct and indirect effects of the pandemic. It does not mean that 18.2 million deaths were all among people with confirmed infection, nor does it estimate an infected person's long-term risk.[2]

### 3. Post-infection risk and all-cause mortality

- An early US Veterans Affairs cohort found higher risks of multiple incident cardiovascular outcomes from 30 days through 1 year after infection than in contemporaneous controls, with risk increasing by the intensity of care during acute illness.[3] The data were mainly from 2020–2021 and the population was predominantly older male veterans; the results cannot be directly generalized to today's vaccinated, previously infected general population.
- A 2024 systematic review found that risks of pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction, and stroke were highest in the acute phase and declined over time; risks were also lower after less severe acute disease.[4] Outcomes, eras, severity, and comparator definitions differed across studies, so the review does not justify one universal “one-year mortality increase.”
- **All-cause mortality**: Some observational cohorts report an increase after infection, but estimates are affected by testing behavior, baseline health, health-care use, acute severity, and pandemic era. The cited evidence does not support the former claim that all infected people have a 10%–30% increase in one-year all-cause mortality; that number has therefore been removed.

### 4. Post-COVID-19 condition (Long COVID / PASC)

- WHO (2025-11-27) estimates that about 6% of infected people develop post-COVID-19 condition, commonly involving fatigue, breathlessness, and cognitive problems. Definitions and estimates vary by method and era.[1]
- In a US Veterans Affairs electronic-health-record study, vaccinated people infected during the Omicron era had a one-year cumulative incidence of PASC of 3.50 events per 100 persons (95% CI 3.31–3.71), compared with 7.76 events per 100 persons (95% CI 7.57–7.98) among unvaccinated people infected during the Omicron era.[5] This is an observational estimate from a specific health system, not a current incidence estimate for the general Chinese population and not a mortality estimate.
- Reinfection may add risk, but the blanket statement that “every infection causes cumulative immune-system damage” is not an established clinical conclusion and is not used here.

### 5. Other infectious diseases: seasonal influenza as an example

- WHO (2025-02-28) estimates that seasonal influenza causes about 290,000–650,000 **respiratory deaths** annually, with severe disease and death concentrated in high-risk groups. This is not all-cause mortality and is not the same endpoint as long-term COVID-19 risk.[6]

## Do

1. **Use risk- and season-specific vaccination guidance**: WHO's 2026 position paper uses a risk-based approach; people at high risk may need revaccination 6–12 months after their most recent COVID-19 dose. Product, interval, and contraindication decisions should follow current local public-health or vaccination-clinic guidance.[7]
2. **Review influenza vaccination annually**: China's 2025–2026 guidance recommends influenza vaccination for everyone aged ≥6 months without a contraindication, prioritizing older adults, people with chronic illness, pregnant people, children, health workers, and other key groups.[8]
3. **Pneumococcal and other vaccines do not follow one schedule for everyone**: Ask a vaccination clinic about age, medical conditions, previous doses, and the local immunization schedule; do not treat them as interchangeable with annual influenza vaccination.
4. **Reduce exposure**: If you have respiratory symptoms or test positive, stay home when possible and avoid contact with high-risk people. Wear a well-fitting mask if you must be around others. Improve ventilation in crowded indoor spaces and use cough etiquette and hand hygiene.[1]
5. **If you are at high risk, contact a clinician early after infection**: Eligibility for antiviral treatment depends on time since symptom onset, risk of progression, kidney and liver function, and drug interactions. Do not self-prescribe.
6. **Evaluate persistent symptoms rather than assuming a cause**: Seek medical assessment when persistent or new symptoms begin within about 3 months of infection, last at least 2 months, and affect daily life. The WHO case definition requires exclusion of alternative diagnoses.[9]
7. **Emergency boundary**: Seek emergency care for difficulty breathing at rest or inability to speak in sentences, confusion, drowsiness or loss of consciousness, persistent chest pain or pressure, cold/clammy/pale/bluish skin, or sudden loss of speech or movement.[1]

## Limitations

- Post-infection risk changes with variant, prior immunity, vaccine product and time since vaccination, access to care, and population structure; effect sizes from earlier eras can rapidly lose comparability.
- Most long-term outcome evidence comes from observational electronic-health-record studies and may be affected by residual confounding, missed infections, outcome-coding error, and differences in health-care use.
- Veterans Affairs findings have limited generalizability to women, younger people, Asian populations, and currently circulating variants. Relative risk also does not substitute for absolute risk.
