# Hyperglycemia

**Mortality impact**: No single “+30%” estimate applies. Diagnosed type 2 diabetes, prediabetes, hospital stress hyperglycemia, fasting glucose, and HbA1c are different populations or measures.
**Evidence level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (strong evidence of an association between diabetes and mortality; this does not establish that more intensive glucose lowering reduces all-cause mortality)
**Actionability**: High, but testing, targets, and treatment must be individualized.

## Conclusion
A single high glucose result does not diagnose diabetes. The cause should be assessed clinically using fasting plasma glucose, an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), HbA1c, symptoms, and the acute-care context. After diagnosis, build a comprehensive plan with a clinician covering diet, activity, weight, blood pressure, lipids, and medication. Do not pursue the lowest possible glucose level on your own.

## Distinguish the condition and the measure
- **Diabetes diagnosis (nonpregnant adults)**: fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L), 2-h plasma glucose during a 75-g OGTT ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L), or HbA1c ≥6.5%. Random plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) is diagnostic when accompanied by classic hyperglycemic symptoms or a crisis. Without unequivocal hyperglycemia, an abnormal result requires confirmation [1].
- **Prediabetes (ADA criteria)**: fasting plasma glucose 100–125 mg/dL (5.6–6.9 mmol/L), 2-h OGTT glucose 140–199 mg/dL (7.8–11.0 mmol/L), or HbA1c 5.7%–6.4%. These measures are not interchangeable, and some organizations use a different lower fasting-glucose cutoff [1].
- **Hospital stress hyperglycemia**: acute infection, trauma, surgery, or medication can temporarily raise glucose. ADA defines inpatient hyperglycemia as glucose >140 mg/dL (>7.8 mmol/L); this does not by itself establish pre-existing outpatient diabetes and should be interpreted with prior HbA1c and clinical context [3].

## How to read the mortality evidence
- A Swedish registry study compared 435,369 people with type 2 diabetes with 2,117,483 matched controls. All-cause mortality was 17.7% versus 14.5% (adjusted HR 1.15, 95% CI 1.14–1.16). The association varied greatly by age, HbA1c, and renal complications, so it does not support a fixed 15% or 30% increase for everyone with high glucose [4].
- In a meta-analysis of 26 prospective cohorts, the all-cause mortality association for prediabetes depended on its definition: RR 1.12 (95% CI 1.05–1.20) for fasting glucose 110–125 mg/dL, but RR 1.07 (95% CI 0.92–1.26) for 100–125 mg/dL, with the confidence interval including 1. Impaired glucose tolerance defined by OGTT had RR 1.33 (95% CI 1.24–1.42) [5].
- Stress hyperglycemia often reflects acute illness severity. Short-term hospital associations should not be extrapolated to a long-term “hyperglycemia effect” in the general population.

## Intervention evidence and limits
- The DPP randomized 3,234 high-risk adults with overweight and elevated fasting and post-load glucose. Over a mean 2.8 years, intensive lifestyle intervention reduced diabetes incidence by 58% (95% CI 48%–66%) versus placebo. This was prevention or delay of incident diabetes in a selected group—not proof of universal “reversal” or lower mortality [6].
- At a median 21-year follow-up in the same cohort, all-cause mortality was not reduced by lifestyle intervention (HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.81–1.28) or metformin (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.79–1.25). Interventions changed across groups during long-term follow-up and may have reduced between-group contrasts [7].
- In a systematic review of 14 randomized trials including 28,614 people with type 2 diabetes, intensive versus conventional glycemic control did not reduce all-cause mortality (RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.91–1.13) and increased severe hypoglycemia (RR 2.39, 95% CI 1.71–3.34) [8]. An observational gradient between glucose and risk therefore does not mean “lower is always better.”

## Do
1. Repeat results near a diagnostic threshold as advised by a clinician; do not diagnose yourself from one home glucose-meter reading.
2. Use a sustainable diet, regular physical activity, weight management, and smoking cessation while also managing blood pressure, lipids, kidney health, and foot risk.
3. An HbA1c goal <7% is appropriate for many nonpregnant adults without severe hypoglycemia risk. Older or frail adults and people with cognitive or functional limitations, major comorbidity, or high hypoglycemia risk often need less stringent, individualized goals [2].
4. Medication choice and deintensification should account for cardiovascular disease, heart failure, kidney disease, weight, hypoglycemia risk, cost, and personal preferences with a clinician.

## Don't
1. Do not interpret “reduce carbohydrates” as eliminating staple foods, and do not substitute a single food or supplement for comprehensive care.
2. Do not stop basal insulin or increase insulin or sulfonylurea doses on your own.
3. Do not treat an observational association per 1% lower HbA1c as the causal effect of every drug in every population.

## Seek urgent care
Seek immediate care for persistent vomiting or inability to drink, marked dehydration, deep or rapid breathing, altered mental status, or glucose/ketones that do not improve under the person's established sick-day plan. Diabetic ketoacidosis can occur with glucose below 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L), so a single glucose cutoff cannot rule out an emergency [2,9].
