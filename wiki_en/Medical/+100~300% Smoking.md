# Smoking

**Mortality Impact**: In current versus never smokers, all-cause mortality is usually about +100% to +200%; it can approach +300% in cohorts of heavy continuing smokers
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (highly consistent all-cause mortality findings; exact estimates are observational and vary by dose, age, region, and past cessation)
**Actionability**: High (complete cessation substantially reduces risk)

## Conclusion
If you currently smoke cigarettes, the most important action is to quit completely rather than only cut down. Quitting at any age is better than continuing, and earlier cessation generally recovers more years of life.[3][5][6]

## All-cause mortality evidence
- In a prospective cohort of 551,388 US adults aged 25–84, the all-cause mortality rate ratio for current versus never smoking was 2.80 (95% CI 2.73–2.88). Race/ethnicity-specific estimates ranged from about 2.01 to 3.00 and were similar by sex.[1]
- In an Australian cohort of 204,953 adults aged ≥45, the adjusted all-cause mortality RR was 2.96 (95% CI 2.69–3.25). The RR was about 2 for ≤14 cigarettes/day and about 4 for ≥25/day. Thus, “+100% to +300%” in the filename is an approximate range for current smoking across doses, not a fixed increase for every person who has ever smoked.[2]
- Low-intensity smoking is not safe. In a US cohort, the HR was 2.32 (95% CI 2.25–2.38) for daily smoking and 1.82 (95% CI 1.65–2.01) for lifelong nondaily smoking. Reducing to nondaily smoking still carried more risk than having quit for ≥10 years.[4]
- Former smokers must not be grouped with current smokers. In a systematic review of adults aged ≥60, pooled relative mortality was 1.83 (95% CI 1.65–2.03) for current smoking and 1.34 (95% CI 1.28–1.40) for former smoking. Quitting remained beneficial even at advanced ages.[5]

## Cessation and years of life
- A 50-year study of 34,439 male British doctors estimated that lifelong continuing smokers lost about 10 years of life. Stopping at about age 60, 50, 40, or 30 gained about 3, 6, 9, or 10 years, respectively, versus continuing. These figures came only from male British doctors and are not precise individual forecasts for every sex or region.[3]
- A 2024 analysis pooling 1.48 million adults in the US, UK, Norway, and Canada found that current smokers lost about 12–13 years of survival between ages 40 and 79 versus never smokers. Lower mortality was already associated with <3 years of cessation; ≥10 years of cessation averted about 10 years of life lost and produced survival similar to never smoking. This was still observational and cannot guarantee the same gain for an individual.[6]

## What to do
1. Set a complete quit date and combine it with behavioral support. The 2024 WHO guideline recommends brief or intensive counseling and lists varenicline, nicotine replacement therapy (NRT), bupropion, and cytisine as effective adult cessation treatments.[7][9]
2. Cessation medicines are not interchangeable self-treatment. Choose with a clinician or pharmacist in light of medical history, pregnancy, kidney function, mental health, seizure risk, and drug interactions. A large randomized trial did not find a significant increase in moderate or severe neuropsychiatric events from varenicline or bupropion versus nicotine patch or placebo, but common adverse effects differed and the trial was funded by Pfizer and GSK.[10]
3. In cessation RCTs, nicotine e-cigarettes increased quit rates versus NRT (RR 1.59, 95% CI 1.29–1.93), but evidence for serious adverse events was uncertain and the longest follow-up was only two years. They are not risk-free; if used, the goal should be complete replacement of combustible cigarettes and ultimately ending tobacco/nicotine dependence, while avoiding prolonged dual use.[8]
4. Avoid secondhand smoke. CDC states that there is no safe exposure level and that exposed nonsmoking adults have about 20%–30% higher lung-cancer risk. This is a disease-specific risk, not the all-cause mortality increase in this entry’s title.[11]

## What not to do
1. Do not equate “smoking less” with quitting; cutting down may reduce some risk but is not complete cessation.[4]
2. Do not apply one mortality multiplier to former, occasional, and heavy current smokers.[1][2][4][5]
3. Do not present lung-cancer, cardiovascular-disease, or COPD-specific risk ratios as all-cause mortality ratios.

## Lung-cancer screening boundary
Annual low-dose CT is not recommended for every person who smokes. The US USPSTF recommends it only for adults aged 50–80 with ≥20 pack-years who currently smoke or quit within 15 years; screening stops after 15 smoke-free years or when health no longer permits curative lung-cancer treatment. Criteria differ by country, so use shared decision-making under local guidance.[12]

## Limitations
Mortality ratios mainly come from observational cohorts using self-reported smoking. Residual confounding can include socioeconomic conditions, alcohol use, and illness-related cessation. Cohorts also differ in age, smoking history, cigarette products, and baseline mortality. Although the overall evidence establishes that smoking causes disease and premature death, an individual's absolute risk and years recoverable cannot be calculated directly from these relative risks.
