# Probiotics

**Mortality Impact**: Insufficient evidence; no direct evidence shows that routine supplementation in healthy people extends life or lowers all-cause mortality
**Evidence Level**: ⭐⭐ (for "longevity/general health"; clinical effects vary by indication, strain or combination, dose, and population)
**Actionability**: Medium (meaningful only when the product precisely matches the clinical evidence)

## Conclusion
Do not routinely buy probiotics for "longevity" or vague "gut health." Probiotics are not one interchangeable intervention. A result for one strain or formulation in one indication cannot be transferred to another strain, dose, disease, or healthy population, and it is not evidence of life extension.

## Evidence
- The accepted definition requires live microorganisms that confer a health benefit when administered in adequate amounts. A specific health claim must match a defined strain or combination, an efficacious dose, and the studied indication; a genus name such as "Lactobacillus" or a total CFU count alone does not establish that match.[1]
- In adults receiving antibiotics, a systematic review of 42 RCTs (11,305 participants) reported a pooled risk ratio of 0.63 for antibiotic-associated diarrhea (AAD; 95% CI 0.54–0.73; moderate certainty). However, organisms, strains, doses, and baseline risks varied, and studies with low baseline AAD risk showed no clear benefit. This result does not establish that any retail product works.[2] A 2025 Cochrane update judged the absolute benefit for preventing *C. difficile*-associated diarrhea to be small (NNT 65; 95% CI 48–97), found no direct comparisons that identify the best formulation, and noted that the two largest trials showed no clear benefit.[3]
- IBS evidence does not permit choosing a product from a claim that "probiotics work overall." A 2023 review included 82 RCTs (10,332 participants), but only 24 were at low risk of bias in every domain; certainty ranged from moderate to very low across organisms, formulations, and outcomes.[4] AGA's currently posted guidance recommends probiotics for symptomatic children and adults with IBS only in a clinical trial and states that evidence is insufficient for most digestive conditions.[5]
- Short-term use in healthy people usually causes, at most, mild gastrointestinal symptoms, but this safety inference does not apply to high-risk patients.[10] In a multicenter RCT in predicted severe acute pancreatitis, one six-strain formulation given by jejunal tube at a total daily dose of 10^10 bacteria did not reduce infection. Deaths were 24/152 versus 9/144 (RR 2.53, 95% CI 1.22–5.25), and bowel ischemia occurred in 9 versus 0 patients. This harm signal is specific to that regimen and severely ill population; it does not mean every probiotic is dangerous.[6]
- Severe illness, immunocompromise, central venous access, impaired intestinal barriers, and enteral or parenteral feeding require individualized clinical risk–benefit assessment. In a single-center retrospective ICU study, 6/522 patients receiving *Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus* GG (LGG) developed bacteremia with that organism versus 0/21,652 not receiving LGG; whole-genome evidence supported transmission from capsule to blood. The result does not apply to ordinary healthy consumers.[7] A systematic review also documents *Saccharomyces boulardii*-associated fungemia, concentrated in patients with ICU stays, enteral/parenteral nutrition, or gastrointestinal disease.[8]
- Product quality limits transfer from trial to shelf: commercial products may have strain misidentification, nonmatching viable counts, or contamination. The strain identity and efficacious dose should still be present at the end of shelf life.[9] In the United States, probiotics may be regulated as foods, dietary supplements, or drugs depending on intended use; dietary supplements do not have to demonstrate drug-level efficacy before marketing.[10]

## Do
1. Do not supplement routinely for longevity or the vague goal of "improving the microbiome."
2. If a clinician recommends a trial, record the full organism and strain identifier, daily CFU, course, viable count through expiration, and storage requirements; verify that they match the product studied for the same indication.
3. If considering a probiotic while taking antibiotics, ask a clinician or pharmacist to weigh baseline AAD or *C. difficile* risk, the exact formulation, and immune status. Do not treat prevention evidence as evidence for treating an established infection.
4. Fermented foods can be ordinary food choices, but not all contain live microorganisms, and live cultures do not by themselves establish a validated probiotic treatment effect.

## Don't
1. Do not generalize findings about AAD, IBS, infection, or microbiome/immune surrogate markers into "general health" or "longer life."
2. Do not choose by "more strains" or "more CFU" alone. A higher dose is not universally better across strains, indications, or populations.
3. Do not self-administer live bacterial or yeast products if immunocompromised, critically ill/in an ICU, with a central venous catheter, severe pancreatitis, or an impaired gut barrier.
4. Do not substitute probiotics for standard evaluation or treatment of infection, persistent diarrhea, IBS, or another illness.

## Medical Red Flags
- Seek prompt care for diarrhea with blood or black stool, persistent high fever, severe or persistent abdominal pain, marked dehydration, altered mental status, or frequent watery stools after antibiotics.
- A high-risk user who develops fever, chills, or systemic deterioration should stop the product and seek urgent assessment, bringing the full strain and lot information.
